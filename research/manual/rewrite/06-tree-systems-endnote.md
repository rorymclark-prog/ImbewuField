# Endnote points removed — 06-tree-systems

Legal, permit and "check with the authorities" points taken out of the chapter, one per line, for the end-of-book paragraph:

- Many plants popular in overseas permaculture books are declared invaders under NEMBA's Alien and Invasive Species Lists (2020); it is against the law to plant them. Ask the nursery for the Latin name and check it is not a listed invader before you buy.
- Listed invaders named in the old chapter: wattles (*Acacia mearnsii* and other Australian *Acacia*), *Leucaena leucocephala*, *Sesbania punicea*, *Robinia pseudoacacia*, *Paraserianthes lophantha*, American elderberry (*Sambucus canadensis*, Category 1b) and white mulberry (*Morus alba*, Category 3).
- Beefwood (*Casuarina cunninghamiana*, *C. equisetifolia*) and many gum and pine species are Category 2 invaders: they may be grown only with a permit, inside a demarcated area, and never within 30 m of a river, dam or wetland.
- Kikuyu grass (*Pennisetum clandestinum*) is a Category 1b invader in protected areas and wetlands.
- Collecting wild seed needs the landowner's permission; a permit is needed to collect plants or seed in nature reserves and to collect protected species.
- Picking protea flowers from the wild, or selling protected plants, needs a permit from the provincial conservation authority.
- Many indigenous trees are protected (National Forests Act 84 of 1998); a licence is needed to cut, damage or remove a protected tree. Buy trees from a nursery rather than digging them up in the veld.
- Under the National Veld and Forest Fire Act (101 of 1998), landowners in fire-risk areas must make and maintain firebreaks on their boundaries; the local Fire Protection Association can advise.
- Medicinal plants (wilde als, pepperbark, African potato, aloe and others) can be harmful in large doses, in pregnancy and for young children, and some react with clinic medicines; do not use them instead of medical treatment, and ask a health worker first.
- Rue (*Ruta graveolens*), often planted as a pest repellent, was dropped from the chapter: its sap blisters skin that is then exposed to sunlight, and it is poisonous if eaten, especially in pregnancy.
