# Endnote points removed from 03-sector-planning

- The National Veld and Forest Fire Act (Act 101 of 1998) requires landowners where a veld fire may start, burn or spread to prepare and maintain a firebreak on their side of each boundary (neighbours may agree to place it elsewhere).
- Under the same Act a firebreak must be wide and long enough to have a reasonable chance of stopping a fire, must not cause soil erosion, and must be reasonably free of inflammable material; the Act sets no fixed width, so ask the local Fire Protection Association (FPA) what is usual.
- Before burning a firebreak, agree a date with neighbours; if you cannot agree, give neighbours and the FPA at least 14 days' written notice.
- It is an offence to light or use a fire in the open air when the South African Weather Service fire danger rating for the area is high or extreme.
- Landowners must have the equipment, protective clothing and trained people needed to fight a fire, and must leave responsible people nearby when they are away.
- Joining a Fire Protection Association is strongly advised: if a fire spreads from a non-member's land, the court presumes negligence unless the owner proves otherwise (this presumption does not apply to members).
- On communal or rented land, ask the owner, the traditional council or the FPA who carries the firebreak duties.
- Ask the FPA whether a strip of fire-resistant plants counts as a legal firebreak.
- Get firebreak-burning training from the FPA or the Working on Fire programme before burning.
- The local FPA can tell you the usual direction fires come from.
- Many pines, gums and wattles are listed invasive species under NEMBA.
- Guava, purple granadilla (*Passiflora edulis*) and tree tomato (*Solanum betaceum*) are NEMBA-listed invaders in several provinces (including KwaZulu-Natal, the Eastern Cape, Limpopo and Mpumalanga), and white mulberry (*Morus alba*) is listed nationally; check your province's rules before planting them (black mulberry, *Morus nigra*, is not listed).
- Under the National Water Act (Act 36 of 1998, Schedule 1) you may, without a licence, take water for reasonable household use and a small non-commercial garden, water stock (not a feedlot) grazing your land, and store rainwater from your roof; more than this, such as business irrigation or a dam on a stream, may need registration or a licence from the Department of Water and Sanitation.
