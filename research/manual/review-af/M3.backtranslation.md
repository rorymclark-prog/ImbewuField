=== SECTION 08-soil#8 ===
## Way 4: Compost

A forest floor makes compost slowly all the time. A good heap does it much faster. Compost is dark, crumbly and smells sweet. It improves both sandy soil and clay soil, holds water, feeds plants slowly, and turns waste into fertility instead of smoke.

### What compost needs

Compost needs **brown material**, **green material**, **air** and **water**. Then it needs **time**.

Brown material is dry and rich in carbon. Green material is fresh and rich in nitrogen. The balance between them is the **carbon-to-nitrogen ratio** (carbon to nitrogen ratio). For a hot heap, aim for about 25 to 30 parts carbon to 1 part nitrogen, which in practice is roughly **2 to 3 buckets of brown material for every bucket of green material**.

| Brown material (carbon) | Green material (nitrogen) |
|---|---|
| Straw and dry grass | Fresh grass clippings |
| Dry leaves | Green weeds without seed |
| Chopped maize stalks | Vegetable and fruit peels |
| Shredded cardboard and newspaper | Animal manure |

Slimy and smells of ammonia: add brown material. Stays cool: add green material or manure. Smells rotten: it is too wet, so turn it over and add dry brown material.

**Air.** Compost life needs oxygen. Turning lets air in, and a base of sticks does too.

**Water.** Keep the heap as moist as a squeezed-out sponge. Squeeze a handful: only a drop or two should come out.

Keep the following out: dog and cat manure and human excrement; meat, fish, bones and dairy, which attract rats; sick plants and weeds with seed, unless the heap gets properly hot; couch-grass ("kweekgras") roots (drown them first for a few weeks in a bucket); and treated wood, coal ash and plastic. A little ordinary wood ash is fine.

### How to build a hot compost heap

A **hot compost heap** reaches 55 to 65 °C, hot enough to kill most germs and weed seed. To get that hot, it must be at least 1 m wide, long and high, and not higher than about 1.5 m, so that you can still turn it.

1. Choose a shady, level place near the garden and water. Build directly on the ground so that worms can move in.
2. Lay down about 20 cm of twigs, small branches or maize stalks, to let air in and water out.
3. Add 10 to 15 cm of brown material.
4. Add 5 to 10 cm of green material. Bury kitchen waste in the middle, away from the sides, to keep flies and rats away.
5. Add a thin layer of manure, about 5 cm.
6. Sprinkle a handful of garden soil or old compost over it to bring in the micro-organisms.
7. Water each layer until it is moist, but not soaking wet.
8. Repeat steps 3 to 7 until the heap is 1 to 1.5 m high.
9. Cover it with straw and then old sacks or a tarpaulin to keep heavy rain out, and leave the sides slightly open for air.

### Look after the heap

1. Within 2 to 4 days the heap should be hot. Push a metal rod into the middle for 5 minutes; if it is too hot to hold comfortably when you pull it out, the heap is working.
2. After 5 to 7 days it starts to cool. **Turn it now.** Fork the whole heap into a new heap alongside, the outside to the middle and the middle to the outside, and add water if it is dry.
3. It gets hot again. Turn it every time it cools, about weekly for the first month. Four or five hot turnings kill most germs and weed seed.
4. If it goes above about 70 °C, turn it or add water. Too much heat kills the useful micro-organisms.
5. Stop adding fresh material. Start a second heap for new waste.

A hot heap that is turned well is ready within a few months in warm weather; a heap that is never turned can take 6 months to a year or longer. It shrinks by a third to a half. Ready compost is dark, crumbly and smells like a forest floor. If it is still warm when you turn it, wait: half-rotted compost takes nitrogen out of the soil, and your plants turn yellow. Once the heap has cooled, red wigglers (see Way 5) will finish it. Keep ready compost covered and use it within a few months.

=== SECTION 08-soil#9 ===
## Way 5: Earthworms and worm farming

Charles Darwin watched earthworms for years. In 1881 he wrote: "It may be doubted whether there are many other animals which have played so important a part in the history of the world as these simply organised creatures."

### Earthworms in your soil

Earthworms are the garden's quiet workers. They pull dead leaves down into the soil, their tunnels let air and water in, and their **castings** (worm manure) bind soil into the small crumbs that mark healthy soil. To attract more earthworms, mulch, add compost, dig less and use no poison.

### Compost worms

A **worm farm** is a container in which special compost worms turn kitchen waste into rich castings. These worms live in rotting leaves and manure, not deep in the soil, and they breed fast in a full bin. The **red wiggler** (*Eisenia fetida*) is the most common in South Africa and handles cold and heat the best. The **African nightcrawler** (*Eudrilus eugeniae*) and **Indian blue worm** (*Perionyx excavatus*) work well in warm areas, but do poorly in cold winters.

Buy them from a worm farm or nursery. Garden earthworms will not survive in a worm farm.

A worm farm gives you two things:

- **Worm castings**: a fine, dark compost with more nutrients that plants can take up than the waste or soil it came from. Use it as top-dressing, in seedling mix, or a handful per planting hole.
- **Worm tea** (worm tea): the liquid that drains out of the worm farm. Dilute it with about 10 parts water, until it looks like weak tea, and pour it on the soil around plants. If it smells bad, the worm farm is too wet; give that liquid to the compost heap or the trees, not to vegetables.

### Building and managing a worm farm

A worm farm is simply a shady, moist, dark home that drains well, safe from chickens, birds and ants. An old bath on bricks works: the plug hole at the low end over a bucket, stones and shade cloth in the bottom, and a hessian sack and a roof sheet on top. The easiest to harvest is a stack of three crates or buckets:

1. Get three dark containers of the same size, about 20 to 30 cm deep.
2. Leave the bottom of the **lowest container** whole: it catches the liquid. Put in a small tap, or stand it over a bowl.
3. Punch many holes of about 5 mm in the bottoms of the **two top containers**, and a few air holes near the top of their sides.
4. Stand the lowest container on bricks in the shade, with one working container on top.
5. Fill the working container two-thirds with **worm bedding**: shredded cardboard, newspaper, dry leaves and straw, soaked and squeezed out, with a little well-rotted manure and a handful of soil.
6. Add about 500 g of worms, roughly a thousand. With only a handful of worms it takes many months to get going.
7. Cover with a damp sack and a lid. Start feeding after a few days.
8. When the working container is full of dark castings, usually after 2 to 4 months, put the third container on top with fresh worm bedding and food, so that it touches the castings below.
9. Over a few weeks the worms move up. Take out the lower container, put any worms back, and use the castings. The empty container becomes the next top one.
10. Drain the lowest container every few days so that it never floods the worms.

Red wigglers do best between about 15 and 25 °C, and above about 30 °C they start to die. Keep the worm farm in deep shade in summer with a wet sack on top, shelter it in a cold winter, and keep the worm bedding as moist as a squeezed-out sponge.

When the worm farm is full of worms and small, lemon-shaped **cocoons** (worm eggs), take out a few handfuls to start another worm farm or to add to a cooled compost heap.

### Feed your worms

1. Start slowly. Only add more when most of the previous meal is gone.
2. Chop or mash the food finely so that it breaks down faster.
3. Bury it under the worm bedding, in a different corner each time, to keep fruit flies away.
4. Add a handful of dry worm bedding with each feeding.

An established worm farm eats up to about half of its own weight in food per day: for 1 kg of worms, up to about 500 g of waste.

| Worms like | Worms do not like |
|---|---|
| Vegetable and fruit peels | Onions, garlic, chillies and a lot of citrus |
| Tea leaves, coffee grounds and crushed eggshells | Meat, fish, bones and dairy |
| Soaked cardboard and paper | Oily, salty or cooked food |
| Well-rotted cattle, horse or rabbit manure | Dog and cat manure |

Some farmers brew **compost tea** by bubbling air through castings in water with a little molasses. Research on whether it helps is mixed, and the molasses can feed germs such as *E. coli* and *Salmonella*. Plain worm tea is simpler. Pour either on the soil, never over vegetables that are eaten raw.

## Food chains and the ecological pyramid

Imagine life on a farm as a pyramid.

- At the bottom are the **plants**, which capture the sun's energy. They rest on the **soil** and its decomposers.
- Above them are the **plant-eaters**, such as caterpillars, aphids, mice and cattle.
- Higher up are the **predators (predator)** that eat them, such as ladybirds, spiders, birds and lizards.
- At the very top are a few **top predators**, such as owls, eagles and jackals.

Each level higher up has fewer living things, because at each step energy is lost. It takes a lot of grass to feed one cow, and many mice to feed one owl.

What this means on the farm:

1. Don't try to kill every pest. Poison all the aphids, and the ladybirds and birds that eat them go hungry and move away. When the aphids come back, nothing stops them.
2. Feed the base. The whole pyramid rests on plants and soil, so soil health determines how much life, and how much harvest, your land can carry.
3. Give the predators a home (see Habitat below).

=== SECTION 09-balanced-ecology#4 ===
## Habitat

A **habitat** is the home of a plant or animal: the place that gives it food, water, shelter, space and a safe place to breed. A frog needs water for its eggs, long grass and stones around the pond to hide from birds and snakes, and insects to eat. That pond is its habitat. Loss of habitat is one of the main reasons why plants and animals go extinct.

Think of habitat as a job advertisement. A farm has many jobs: eating pests, pollinating flowers, recycling waste. Instead of paying for that, we advertise for wild helpers, and the advertisement must offer food, water and a safe bed.

1. **Provide water.** A birdbath or small pond quickly brings birds, frogs and dragonflies, which eat large numbers of insects. Put stones or sticks in the water so that bees can drink without drowning. Fence ponds off or cover them with strong mesh so that small children cannot fall in. Frogs, tadpoles and dragonfly larvae eat mosquito larvae; don't add mosquitofish (muskietvis), because they also eat the tadpoles.
2. **Leave wild areas.** Keep any natural veld or vlei on your land. This is your **Zone 5**. If you have none, give a corner back to **indigenous (indigenous)** plants. This is probably the most important thing you can do for habitat, because local plants feed many more local insects and birds than exotic plants.
3. **Plant hedgerows.** Plant mixed rows along fences and field edges: Cape honeysuckle (Kaapse kanferfoelie, *Tecoma capensis*), plumbago (*Plumbago auriculata*), sand olive (sandolien, *Dodonaea viscosa*), Kei apple (keiappel, *Dovyalis caffra*), and karee and other *Searsia* species. They give shelter, food and nesting places, and they break the wind.
4. **Stack stones and logs in heaps** away from the house and from where children play. Lizards bask on the warm stones and eat many insects. Logs shelter beetles, frogs and fungi.
5. **Put up an owl box** on a pole, tree or building, at least 3 m above the ground, out of reach of cats. A family of barn owls (nonnetjie-uile) can eat 1 000 or more rodents in a year. Owls die if they eat poisoned rats, so if you want owls, stop using rat poison. Where people fear owls as a bad omen, talk to your neighbours about the work they do.

=== SECTION 09-balanced-ecology#5 ===
## Biodiversity

**Biodiversity (biodiversity)** is the variety of life in a place: the many kinds of plants, animals, insects, fungi and soil life, and the differences within each kind. Walk through healthy veld or fynbos and you see it. A pine plantation shows the opposite: hectare after hectare of one tree, with very little growing underneath. Several kinds of pine trees also spread into grassland and fynbos, and use a lot of water.

Diversity pays:

- **It spreads the risk.** If one crop fails because of drought, pests or disease, others still give a harvest.
- **It helps to control pests.** A mixture of plants feeds and shelters the predators that eat pests, and pests struggle to find their favourite plant among many. Diverse gardens are less likely to get serious outbreaks, although they are never pest-free.
- **It builds healthy soil.** Different roots and plant residues feed a wider variety of soil life.

To build it up:

1. Plant **polycultures**: many crops together in the same bed or field, instead of one crop in long rows.
2. Plant **perennial plants (perennial)** (plants that live for years, such as fruit trees, herbs and perennial vegetables) alongside your annual crops.
3. Plant a **food forest**, a garden in layers from tall trees down to **groundcover (groundcover)** and root crops.
4. Keep a mixture of animals, each with a job: chickens eat pests, and cattle manure feeds the **compost (compost)**.
5. Plant flowers, including indigenous flowers, to feed bees and other useful insects the whole year.

When the output of one part feeds another (manure to the soil, crop residues to the animals, pests to the birds), the farm begins to look after itself.

## Try it

Make a habitat corner this week.

1. Choose an unused corner near your vegetable beds, at least 2 m by 2 m, away from where children play.
2. Sink an old basin, a cut-off drum or a tyre lined with plastic into the ground. Fill it with water and edge it with stones. Put a few stones or sticks inside so that insects can climb out. Cover it with strong mesh if small children live nearby.
3. Build a small stone heap on the sunny side (north) and a log heap on the shady side.
4. Plant, or leave, indigenous plants and a little long grass around it.
5. Go and look once a week at dawn and dusk. Write down every little creature you see.

## Key points

- The pyramid of life rests on plants and soil. Kill every pest and you let the predators that help you go hungry.
- Succession is how land heals. In Southern Africa it often ends in grassland, savanna or fynbos, not forest.
- Water, wild corners, hedgerows, stone heaps and owl boxes bring wild helpers to work on your farm.
- Diversity spreads risk and keeps pests in check.

Go and sit by the pond in a month and count what has moved in. Every new visitor is a worker you do not have to hire.

=== SECTION 10-natural-pest-control#0 ===
# Natural pest control

A caterpillar on a cabbage is not an enemy. It is food. Birds, frogs, lizards, spiders, ladybirds and small wasps all live off the little creatures we call pests. Kill every pest, and we let the helpers go hungry too, and the garden becomes less stable, not more.

So if pests suddenly explode, we read it as a message. Something is out of balance: exhausted soil, plants stressed by drought, too much of one crop in one place. A quick organic fix can save this season's harvest. The work that remains is to treat the cause.

This does not mean that we just sit back. "Obtain a yield" is one of our principles, and the family must eat. We protect the crops that are always at risk, such as cabbage and seedlings. But we do not expect every plant to survive. A small share goes back to the system, and the system does much of the pest control for us.

**By the end of this chapter you will be able to:**

- Recognise the main garden pests of Southern Africa and deal with each without poison
- Attract predators (predators) to your garden with flowers, water and shelter
- Use trap crops (trap crops) and good neighbours, and know which claims about companion planting (companion planting) are proven
- Make a garlic, onion and chilli spray and use it without harming bees
- Plan a crop rotation (crop rotation) so that no crop family returns to the same bed for three to four years

=== SECTION 10-natural-pest-control#1 ===
## Why we avoid chemical poison

Chemical pesticides cost money every season and do harm to people and animals. Natural pest control costs little once it is set up, and gets easier every year.

Poison also traps the farmer in a cycle:

1. The spray kills the pests, and also the **predators**, the animals and insects that hunt and eat pests.
2. Pests breed much faster than their predators.
3. The pests come back first, and now there is nothing to eat them.
4. The farmer sprays again. The problem gets bigger, and many pests become resistant to the poison.

Before chemical farming there was no word for "organic", because all farming was organic. The old methods still work, and a young garden needs them most.

=== SECTION 10-natural-pest-control#2 ===
## How to manage pests: step by step

Most pest control happens before a pest arrives. Strong plants in fertile soil (Chapter 8) resist attacks. **Biodiversity** (biodiversity), a wide variety of plants, insects, birds and soil life, gives every pest an enemy (Chapter 9). A big block of one crop does the opposite: the pest finds its food easily and multiplies.

If pests do come, work through these steps in order:

1. **Look carefully.** Walk through the garden at least twice a week and look under the leaves. Pests are easiest to control if you find them early.
2. **Encourage predators.** Give them food, water and shelter.
3. **Remove pests by hand.** Pick off caterpillars, snails and beetles. Squash aphid colonies.
4. **Use barriers.** Netting, shade cloth, collars and fences keep pests out.
5. **Bring in the birds.** Between crops, chickens or ducks in a pen you can move, an **animal tractor** (animal tractor) (Chapter 5), clean up slugs, snails and grubs. Keep them away from young seedlings, because they scratch them out.
6. **Spray only as a last resort**, and only the plants that are attacked.

It can take a few seasons before the balance forms. The less poison and the more variety, the faster it comes.

=== SECTION 10-natural-pest-control#3 ===
## Common pests in Southern Africa

Know your enemy before you act. Most pests are insects, and they feed in one of two ways. **Sap-suckers** pierce the plant and drink its sap: aphids, scale insects, mealybugs, whiteflies, thrips, red spider mites (tiny relatives of spiders) and root-knot nematodes (microscopic little worms that make knots on roots). In large numbers they weaken the plant, and many carry viruses from plant to plant. **Plant-chewers** chew leaves, stems, fruit or roots: caterpillars, beetles, slugs and snails. They are bigger, so you can often pick them off by hand. Mole-rats, porcupines and some birds are also pests.

### Aphids

Aphids (aphids) are small, soft, pear-shaped insects, green, black or grey, that cluster on young shoots and under leaves. Cabbage, beans and peas suffer the most. They make plants wilt/languish, make leaves curl and carry viruses. In warm weather the females produce live young without mating, so a colony can explode within weeks. When it gets too crowded, aphids with wings fly to new plants.

1. Squash small colonies with your fingers, or spray them off with a strong jet of water.
2. Protect ladybirds, hoverflies, lacewings and parasitic wasps. They eat aphids or breed inside them.
3. Control the ants that tend them (see Ants, below).
4. Plant nasturtiums (nasturtiums) along the edge of the bed as a **trap crop**, a plant the pest likes better than your food (see Companion planting).
5. Be sparing with fresh manure and nitrogen. Soft, fast, juicy growth attracts aphids.

> **Tip:** An aphid colony that has turned brown and papery has been killed by parasitic wasps. Leave it alone. The next wasps hatch out of it.

### Cutworms

Cutworms (cutworms) are fat, grey-brown caterpillars that curl into a C if you disturb them. By day they hide in the soil, and at night they come out to cut seedlings off at ground level: tomatoes, cabbage, maize and beans.

1. Clear the bed of weeds a few weeks before you plant, so that the young caterpillars have nothing to eat.
2. If you find a cut-off seedling, dig gently around it. The cutworm is usually within a few centimetres. Remove it.
3. Put a collar around each seedling: a cardboard tube, or a plastic bottle with the top and bottom cut off, pressed 3 to 5 cm into the soil.
4. Turn the soil before you plant, and let birds and chickens peck through it.

### African bollworm

The African bollworm (African bollworm, *Helicoverpa armigera*) is a striped caterpillar, green, brown or pinkish, that bores into tomatoes, maize cobs, beans, peas, sweet peppers and sorghum. A small, round hole in a tomato is the usual sign.

1. Check plants regularly while they flower and bear fruit. Pick off eggs and caterpillars.
2. Pick off damaged fruit and destroy it, so that the caterpillars inside cannot complete their life cycle.
3. Protect parasitic wasps, predatory bugs and birds.
4. If you must spray, use Bt (*Bacillus thuringiensis*), a natural bacterium that kills only caterpillars. It works on young caterpillars, before they enter the fruit.

### Diamond-back moth

The diamond-back moth (diamond-back moth, *Plutella xylostella*) is the worst pest of cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower, rape (rape) and their family. The caterpillar is about 1 cm long, light green and pointed at both ends. Touch it and it wriggles backwards and drops down on a silk thread. Young caterpillars leave transparent "windows" in the leaf; older caterpillars eat holes right through. This moth has beaten many chemical insecticides, so natural control is most important here.

1. Look under the leaves and remove caterpillars by hand.
2. Protect the tiny wasps that breed inside the caterpillars. Poison kills the wasps first.
3. Cover young plants with fine netting or shade cloth.
4. Give the bed a break from crops of the cabbage family, so that the moth's life cycle is broken.
5. Overhead irrigation knocks off many small caterpillars. Spray Bt on young caterpillars if it is needed.

### Fall armyworm

Fall armyworm (fall armyworm, *Spodoptera frugiperda*) comes from the Americas. It was first confirmed in South Africa in February 2017, in Limpopo, and spread quickly. It mainly attacks maize, also sorghum and other grasses. Look for a light upside-down "Y" on the head, and four dark spots in a square on the second-to-last body segment. Older caterpillars eat ragged holes and fill the funnel (whorl) of young maize with droppings that look like sawdust.

1. From the day the maize comes up, go scouting twice a week. Look in the funnel for fresh droppings.
2. Squash egg masses (fluffy clusters on the leaves) and young caterpillars.
3. Plant early, at the same time as your neighbours, so that your maize is not young when armyworm numbers are at their highest.
4. Plant maize together with beans, cowpeas (cowpeas) or pumpkins as an intercrop. Mixed fields keep more of the ants, earwigs, predatory bugs and parasitic wasps that eat armyworm.
5. If you must spray, put Bt in the funnel while the caterpillars are small.

### Fruit flies

Fruit flies lay their eggs under the skin of fruit that is ripening. The maggots feed inside, and the fruit rots and falls off. South Africa has the Mediterranean and the Natal fruit fly, and the Oriental fruit fly (Oriental fruit fly, *Bactrocera dorsalis*), which was first found in Limpopo in 2010 and is now established in several northern provinces. Pumpkin flies do the same to pumpkins, melons and cucumbers.

1. Every few days pick up all the fallen and damaged fruit. Seal it in a plastic bag and leave it in hot sun until the maggots are dead, or bury it at least 50 cm deep.
2. Harvest fruit as soon as it is ripe.
3. Tie paper or cloth bags over young guavas, mangoes and peaches.
4. Hang baited traps in the trees.
5. Work together with your neighbours. Fruit flies move between gardens, and one neglected orchard re-infests all the others.

### Slugs and snails

Slugs and snails live in damp places and eat leaves, and they do real damage when there are many or when they get at seedlings. **Mulch** (mulch), the covering of dry plant material that we spread on the soil, keeps them damp and sheltered, but coarse, dry, scratchy mulch suits them much less than wet, soft material.

1. Pick them off in the evenings by torchlight, especially after rain.
2. Lay a plank or an old sack on damp soil. In the mornings pick up the snails that hide under it.
3. Let ducks or chickens clean the beds between crops.
4. Encourage frogs, toads, birds and ground beetles.
5. Pull the mulch back from young seedlings until they are stronger.

Snails and slugs can carry rat lungworm (rat lungworm), a parasite that occurs in rats in South Africa and can make people very sick. Wear gloves or wash your hands after you have picked them up, and wash morogo well before you eat it. We do not use slug pellets: they poison dogs and cats as well as snails.

### Birds

Most birds are friends. The red-billed quelea (red-billed quelea) is the exception: it flies in huge flocks and can strip sorghum, millet and wheat bare, worst when grass seed in the veld is scarce.

1. Span net over small plots of grain. [Ambiguous Afrikaans: "net" can read as "only" — "Only stretch over small plots of grain" — or as "netting".]
2. Scare the birds away early morning and late afternoon, when they feed, with noise, flags or shiny tape.
3. Harvest as soon as the grain is ripe.
4. Never put out poison. The eagles and owls that eat the dead birds die too.

### Ants

Ants seldom eat crops. They live off honeydew, the sugary liquid that aphids, scale insects and mealybugs give off, and in return they protect those sap-suckers against ladybirds and wasps. On honeydew black sooty mould also grows on the leaves, which blocks the light. Elsewhere ants hunt termites and caterpillars, so we act only where they "farm" sap-suckers.

1. On fruit trees wrap a band of grease or sticky material around the trunk, on a strip of cloth or tape rather than on the bark.
2. Cut away branches that touch the ground or a wall. Ants use them as bridges.
3. Deal with the aphids and scale insects, and the ants lose interest.

### Mole-rats and porcupines

The "moles" that eat bulbs, potatoes and sweet potatoes in South Africa are usually mole-rats: rodents that push up heaps of soil from deep tunnels. Golden moles are a different animal. They tunnel just under the surface and eat insects and earthworms, not plants. Several kinds are rare, so leave them in peace.

1. Line the bottom of raised beds, and the planting holes of valuable plants, with strong wire mesh.
2. Plant bulbs and young trees in wire baskets.
3. Welcome mole snakes and owls. Never use poison: a poisoned mole-rat kills the owl, snake, dog or cat that eats it.
4. Porcupines dig out roots and strip bark. A strong fence, with mesh that is buried well into the ground, stops them digging underneath.

=== SECTION 10-natural-pest-control#4 ===
## Encourage predators

Give the hunters a **habitat** (habitat), a place with the food, water and shelter they need, and they move in: stones and logs for lizards, a small pond for frogs, mulch for ground beetles, flowers for wasps and hoverflies.

- **Chameleons and lizards** eat insects and shelter among warm stones.
- **Frogs and toads** eat slugs and insects. They need a damp place or a small pond.
- **Ladybirds** and their larvae eat large numbers of aphids.
- **Hoverflies** look like small bees. Their larvae eat aphids.
- **Lacewings** are delicate, green insects. Their larvae eat aphids, mites and small caterpillars.
- **Parasitic wasps** are tiny and cannot sting people. They lay their eggs inside caterpillars and aphids.
- **Ground beetles, praying mantises and spiders** hunt almost any insect that is smaller than themselves.
- **Insect-eating birds** catch caterpillars, moths, termites, scale insects and even snails.

> **Tip:** Not every ladybird is a friend. The shiny ones eat aphids. Dull orange ladybirds with many black spots and a slightly hairy back, and their spiny yellow larvae, eat the leaves of pumpkins, potatoes and their family. Learn to tell them apart.

### Flowers that feed predatory insects

Adult wasps, hoverflies and lacewings live on nectar and pollen, and small open flowers like those of the carrot family suit them best. Grow a mixture that flowers through the year:

- Herbs that flower: dill, coriander, parsley and caraway, and a few carrots that you let flower
- Yarrow, calendula, chamomile, zinnia, sunflower, borage (borage) and lemon verbena
- French and African marigolds (French and African marigolds), and cosmos (near veld you must cut off the heads before they drop seed, because cosmos spreads into grassland)
- **Indigenous** (indigenous) flowers, those that naturally belong here: gazania, African daisies (African daisies: *Arctotis*, *Osteospermum*, *Dimorphotheca*), pincushion (pincushion, *Scabiosa*) and everlastings (everlastings, *Helichrysum*)

Let a few herbs and vegetables flower in every bed. The helpers stay where the food is.

=== SECTION 10-natural-pest-control#5 ===
## Companion planting

**Companion planting** means that you grow crops, herbs and flowers together so that they help each other. It is very old. Farmers in China for centuries grew a small floating fern together with their rice, because it adds nitrogen to the water. In the Americas maize, beans and squashes grew together as the "three sisters": the maize holds the beans upright, the beans add nitrogen, and the big squash leaves shade the ground and smother weeds.

Trap crops, flowers for predatory insects and mixed plantings are well tested. Many other claims are only farmers' reports. Try them, watch carefully and keep what works on your land.

**Mixed planting.** A mixture of crops feeds more soil life and more predators. Many insects find their food plant by its shape, colour and smell, and a bed with many shapes and colours confuses them, so some move on.

**Trap crops.** A trap crop is grown near the food crop to lure the pest away. Nasturtiums planted near the crop often lure aphids, especially black aphids, away from it. To use one:

1. Plant nasturtiums along the edge of the bed, not in the middle.
2. Check them twice a week.
3. When the leaves are full of aphids, pick those leaves and feed them to the chickens.

A trap crop that you never check becomes a breeding ground.

**Scent disruptors.** A strong smell can hide the crop the pest is looking for. Gardeners plant onions to protect carrots against carrot fly (carrot fly). Research in England found that this works only with many more onions than carrots, and only while the onions are young and leafy, before they form bulbs.

**Booster plants.** Basil grown together with tomatoes reduced thrips and whitefly a little in some trials. The claim that it improves their taste is not proven.

### Plants that may repel pests

- **French and African marigolds** reduced whitefly on greenhouse tomatoes in trials. They suppress root-knot nematodes only if they are grown densely as a cover crop for 2 to 4 months before the vegetables. A few plants here and there do little.
- **Mint, rosemary, sage, lavender and wilde-als** (*Artemisia afra*, which is traditionally used to keep insects away) reduce aphids and cabbage moths according to farmers. Worth trying. Grow mint in a tin/container, because it spreads fast.

=== SECTION 10-natural-pest-control#7 ===
## Home-made sprays against pests

A spray is a last resort. Even a "natural" spray kills the ladybirds, hoverflies and bees that you worked so hard to attract. Garlic, onion and chilli mostly repel pests rather than kill them, and the smell fades, so the spray must be repeated.

### Garlic, onion and chilli spray

This spray repels soft insects such as aphids and young caterpillars. The soap helps it stick to the leaves.

You need:

- 1 medium onion
- 4 cloves of garlic
- 2 cups of fresh mint leaves, or 20 drops of peppermint oil
- 2 tablespoons of chilli powder or cayenne pepper (cayenne), or a handful of fresh, hot chillies
- 2 tablespoons (30 ml) of soft liquid soap, or ordinary bar soap dissolved in water, without bleach, degreaser or strong perfume
- 3 litres of water

Method:

1. Put on rubber gloves and keep your hands away from your eyes. Chilli and garlic burn.
2. Chop the onion, garlic, mint and chillies finely. Pound it in a mortar and pestle, or mix it in a blender with about 1 litre of the water.
3. Cover and let it soak for a few hours.
4. Strain it through a fine cloth or sieve.
5. Add the other 2 litres of water and stir the soap in gently.
6. Pour it into a spray bottle and put a label on it. Never keep spray in a cool-drink or milk bottle.
7. Test it on a few leaves and wait one to two days. If the leaves show burnt or yellow spots, add more water.
8. Spray the tops and undersides of the leaves of only the plants that are attacked, late afternoon or evening when bees have stopped flying. Don't spray open flowers, or plants that are wilting in the heat of the day.

Use it once or twice a week, and after heavy rain. Make a fresh batch each time, and wash vegetables that have been sprayed before you eat them.

Nicotine poisons people and animals as well as insects, so we do not make tobacco or snuff spray. It goes through the skin of the person who sprays, and a child who drinks it can die. We also do not make sprays from syringa berries or moonflower (moonflower, *Datura*), because these are also poisonous.

=== SECTION 10-natural-pest-control#8 ===
## Crop rotation

**Crop rotation** means that you move each crop family to a different bed each season, so that pests and diseases do not build up. Grow the same crop year after year in the same place, and the soil loses the same nutrients each time, while soil pests just wait for their food to come back. Crop rotation breaks their life cycle and lets the soil recover.

### Rotation by plant family

Plants of one family need similar nutrition and suffer from the same pests. Wait at least three years, preferably four, before a family returns to a bed. A simple rotation with four beds:

1. **Year 1:** cabbage family (for example cabbage)
2. **Year 2:** carrot family or root crops (for example carrots)
3. **Year 3:** bean family (for example beans)
4. **Year 4:** onion family (for example onions)

This suits growers who plant one crop per bed.

| Family | Crops |
|---|---|
| Cabbage family | Cabbage, cauliflower, broccoli, kale, rape, mustard greens, turnip, radish |
| Tomato family | Tomatoes, potatoes, sweet peppers, chillies, eggplants |
| Carrot family | Carrots, celery, parsley, coriander, dill |
| Beet family | Swiss chard (Swiss chard), beetroot, spinach, amaranth (morogo) |
| Pumpkin family | Cucumbers, pumpkins, butternut, squashes, melons, watermelons |
| Bean family (legumes, legumes) | Peas, beans, cowpeas, groundnuts, jugo beans, soya beans |
| Onion family | Onions, leeks, garlic, spring onions, chives |
| Grass family | Maize, sweetcorn, sorghum, millet |

Potatoes belong to the tomato family. Never plant potatoes after tomatoes, sweet peppers or eggplants, or the other way round. Where bacterial wilt or nematodes are a problem, leave an even longer break.

### Rotation by feeding needs

We can also group crops by how hungry they are:

- **Heavy feeders** need rich soil: cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower, maize, tomatoes, potatoes, pumpkins and butternut.
- **Light and medium feeders** need less: lettuce, Swiss chard, carrots, beetroot, onions, garlic and radishes.
- **Soil builders** are the **legumes**, plants of the bean family: beans, peas, cowpeas, groundnuts and broad beans. Bacteria in small knobs (nodules) on their roots take nitrogen from the air and make it available to plants.

Rotate in three steps:

1. Grow heavy feeders after legumes, or after lots of compost (compost: rotted plant and animal waste) or manure.
2. Follow them with light feeders.
3. Then grow legumes to build the soil up again, and start again from the beginning.

This suits mixed beds: move a group of companions together through the steps.

Most of the nitrogen that a legume fixes goes into its seed and leaves the garden with the harvest. Leave the roots in the soil and put the stems and leaves back on the bed or on the compost heap.

=== SECTION 10-natural-pest-control#10 ===
## Key points

- An outbreak is a sign of imbalance. Treat the cause, not just the symptom.
- Poison kills the predators as well as the pests, and often makes the problem worse.
- Go scouting twice a week, find pests early and remove them by hand, with barriers or with a trap crop.
- Feed the helpers with flowers, water, mulch and stones, and keep the same crop family apart and on the move.
- Spray only as a last resort, in the evening and away from flowers. We never make tobacco spray.

Season after season the list of helpers in your notebook gets longer. Watch it grow, and let the sprayer gather dust.

=== SECTION 11-home-and-appropriate-technology#4 ===
## Drying food with the sun

Summer gives more than a family can eat; winter gives little. Drying carries the surplus over. It works because the moulds and germs that make food spoil cannot grow without water.

### Drying in the sun

Open sun drying is the cheapest method, but it is slow: food can go mouldy before it is dry, and flies, dust and dew get at it. Do it well:

1. Use fresh, clean produce. Wash it, and your hands.
2. Cut it into thin, even slices.
3. Dry it on raised racks or trays, never on the ground.
4. Cover it with clean netting against flies.
5. Bring it in at night, or cover it against dew.
6. Turn the pieces once or twice a day.

### Solar dryer

A **solar dryer** (solar dryer) is faster and cleaner. It can be as simple as a wooden box with a lid of transparent plastic and trays of mesh or shade cloth, or as sturdy as a metal frame with glass. Dryers that let warm air flow through the trays work faster, because moving air carries the moisture away.

Food is dry enough when:

- **fruit** is leathery and bends without wet spots when you press it
- **vegetables** are brittle and break or rattle
- **maize, beans and groundnuts** are hard and rattle, dried to below about 13% moisture within two or three days after harvest

Dry grain and groundnuts quickly, and throw away any that are mouldy, discoloured or shrivelled. Mould on damp grain makes **aflatoxin**, a liver poison that cooking does not destroy. Store dried food in clean, airtight containers in a cool, dark place, and throw away anything that smells musty.

If you dip fruit slices in lemon juice and water, they keep their colour. Blanching vegetables (a quick dip in boiling water) helps them keep longer.

=== SECTION 11-home-and-appropriate-technology#5 ===
## Cooking with heat retention: the wonder bag

A pot of samp and beans can simmer for hours, and each of those hours burns wood, gas or paraffin. A heat-retention cooker lets the pot finish cooking in its own heat.

A **wonder bag** (hittesak) is an insulated bag that keeps a cooking pot hot for hours. The Wonderbag was developed in South Africa in 2008; a home-made hay box, packed with dry grass or old blankets, works the same way. Less fuel burnt means less money spent and less smoke in the house.

1. Bring the food to a full boil and let it simmer for a few minutes. Samp, beans and tough meat must simmer longer. Soak dry beans, and then boil them hard for at least 10 minutes before they go into the bag.
2. With the lid on, move the pot straight into the bag and close it.
3. Use a pot that fits tightly and is well filled. A full pot keeps its heat longer.
4. Don't open the bag to look. Every peek lets heat out.
5. Eat the food while it is still steaming hot. If it has become lukewarm, first bring it to the boil again. Never leave food overnight in the bag.

=== SECTION 11-home-and-appropriate-technology#6 ===
## Rocket stoves

Wood is heavy to carry and grows back slowly. And smoke kills. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that about 2.1 billion people cook on open fires or simple stoves that burn wood, dung, crop waste, coal or paraffin. Smoke in their homes caused an estimated 2.9 million deaths in 2021. Women and young children, who spend the most time at the fire, suffer the most.

A **rocket stove** (vuurpylstoof) burns small sticks in a short, insulated, L-shaped chimney. The fire burns hot and clean, and the heat goes straight up into the pot. Field tests found that rocket stoves use roughly 30 to 50% less wood than an open fire on three stones, with much less smoke and carbon monoxide. Some are portable; others are built in, with a chimney.

1. Burn dry wood. Wet wood smokes and gives little heat.
2. Feed a few thin sticks in at a time and push them in as they burn.
3. Keep the air gap under the sticks open.
4. Let the pot sit tightly, with a small gap through which the hot gases can flow around the pot.
5. Cook outside or in a kitchen with a chimney.

A rocket stove still makes carbon monoxide, a gas you cannot see or smell. Never bring a burning stove, imbawula or coal fire into a closed room to keep warm.

=== SECTION 11-home-and-appropriate-technology#7 ===
## Biogas digesters

Manure is fuel and fertiliser at once, if we handle it right. **Biogas** is a fuel gas that is made when bacteria break down manure, food waste or toilet waste in a sealed tank without air. The tank is a **biogas digester** (biogas digester). Biogas is about 50 to 70% methane, the part that burns. Most of the rest is carbon dioxide, with a little hydrogen sulphide, which smells of rotten eggs. For a household, cooking is the best use. The liquid that flows out, which is called **slurry** (slurry), is a good fertiliser.

You can buy a digester or build one from tanks, pipes and fittings from the hardware store. It must be completely airtight.

1. Feed it fresh cattle, pig or chicken manure mixed with about the same amount of water, plus chopped vegetable waste.
2. Keep woody material, straw, bones, plastic, soap, bleach and other chemicals out. Chemicals kill the bacteria.
3. Feed a little every day rather than a lot at once. A family usually needs the fresh manure of several cattle or pigs every day.
4. Keep it warm. The bacteria work best at about 30 to 38 °C and make very little gas below about 15 °C. On the Highveld and in Lesotho, bury it, insulate it or put a small plastic tunnel over it.
5. Test the couplings for leaks with soapy water, where bubbles show a leak. Keep flames away from the digester and pipes, cook in a well-ventilated kitchen, and turn the gas tap off after cooking.
6. Cover the inlet and outlet pits. Never climb into a digester or pit, not even an empty one: the gases inside can kill you.
7. Let the slurry stand for a few weeks or compost it. Then put it on fruit trees and crops that you cook, never on morogo that you eat raw.
