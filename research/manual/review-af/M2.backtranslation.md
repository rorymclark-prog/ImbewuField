=== SECTION 06-tree-systems#2 ===
## Design of a food forest

Design first on paper. A tree in the wrong place costs you years.

1. **List your needs.** What do your family, your animals and the local wildlife need? Food, firewood, fodder, medicine, shade?
2. **Match plants to your climate.** Choose indigenous and exotic plants that suit your rain, frost and heat. Add a few that are only just hardy where you live. As the climate changes, they give you a buffer.
3. **Mix the kinds:** fruits, nuts, berries, nitrogen fixers, medicinal plants, flowers for bees and birds, and plants for mulch.
4. **Spread the harvest.** In South Africa avocados come mostly from about March to September and citrus through autumn and winter, while grapes and peaches ripen in summer and almonds in late summer. Most fruits come in early, mid-season and late cultivars. Plant of each.
5. **Think about nutrition.** Plan so that the family eats a variety of fruits and leafy greens the whole year through.
6. **Match the rootstock to your soil.** Fruit trees are made by **grafting** (grafting): a good fruit cultivar is attached onto the roots of another plant. The **rootstock** (rootstock) is that root part, and different rootstocks suit different soils. Ask the nursery which rootstock suits yours.
7. **Give preference to indigenous support plants.** For nitrogen fixers, **groundcover** (groundcover), that is low plants that spread over the ground, and plants for wildlife, choose indigenous species first.

> **Tip:** Before you write your plant list, walk slowly through the nearest wild veld or forest. Notice which groundcover grows in the shade and which in the sun, and which pioneer trees come up first on disturbed ground. The veld is the best teacher of what will flourish on your land.

### Match fruit trees to your climate

The biggest question for fruit trees in South Africa is **frost**.

| Climate | Fruits that usually do well | Remarks |
|---|---|---|
| Cold Highveld, Free State, Lesotho, Eastern Cape highlands (hard frost) | Apple, pear, quince, peach, nectarine, plum, apricot, fig, persimmon, grape, pecan nut | These deciduous trees need cold winters. Late frost kills the flowers of apricot, almond and early peach, so plant them on a slope, not in a **frost pocket** (frost pocket): a low place where cold air goes to lie. |
| Western Cape (winter rain, dry summer) | Apple, pear, plum, apricot, peach, fig, grape, almond, carob, citrus, pomegranate | Needs water in summer. Almond and carob suit the dry summers. |
| Frost-free lowveld and coast (Limpopo and Mpumalanga lowveld, KwaZulu-Natal coast) | Mango, banana, papaya (pawpaw), avocado, litchi, citrus, black sapote, jackalberry | Most deciduous fruits do not get enough winter cold here. Choose cultivars with a low chill requirement. |
| Light frost (KwaZulu-Natal midlands, parts of Gauteng) | Citrus, avocado, fig, pecan nut, persimmon, peach and plum with a low chill requirement | Fuerte avocado tolerates cold better than Hass. Protect young citrus and avocado two or three winters long against frost. |

Deciduous fruit cultivars are sold according to their chill requirement: how many cold hours they need in winter. Ask for cultivars that suit your area.

### Planting patterns and spacing

Lay out the forest in groups, hedges, straight lines, or strips of crops between rows of trees. Choose what suits your site. Whatever pattern you choose, every tree needs sun. In Southern Africa the sun is in the **north**, so plant tall evergreen trees on the **south side**, where their shade falls outside the garden, or between trees that lose their leaves in winter. Use the sunny **edge** (edge), the place where two areas meet, for herbs that like sun, as a natural forest does.

1. Measure your site.
2. Find the full **canopy** width (canopy) of each tree: the width of its leaf crown when it is fully grown.
3. Draw a plan on paper, or peg out the site with pegs.
4. Leave space. A tree with a canopy of 4 m needs about 6 to 7 m to the next tree, unless you are planting a hedge. In the space in between go paths, nitrogen fixers and smaller plants.
5. Make your shopping list from the plan.

=== SECTION 06-tree-systems#3 ===
## Planting of a food forest

A natural forest takes decades, even centuries, to grow. We speed it up by planting pioneers first.

Think of how you light a fire. The kindling lights the twigs, the twigs light the branches, and eventually the big logs catch flame. In a food forest the fast pioneers build up fertility first, and the fruit trees take over as they grow.

The best pioneers are **legumes** (legumes), plants of the bean and pea family. Most of them fix nitrogen: their roots work together with bacteria to take nitrogen from the air and add it to the soil. They also make masses of **biomass** (biomass), that is leaves and branches, for mulch.

| Plant | Use | Remarks |
|---|---|---|
| Sweet thorn *Vachellia karroo* | Nitrogen fixer, fodder, firewood, gum | Indigenous and very hardy. It grows up densely in overgrazed veld, so plant it in your food forest, never in healthy grassland. |
| Common hook thorn *Senegalia caffra* | Nitrogen fixer, fodder, firewood, hedge | Indigenous; summer-rainfall areas. |
| Keurboom *Virgilia oroboides* | Fast nitrogen fixer, flowers for bees | Indigenous in the Southern Cape and Western Cape; does not live long. |
| Pigeon pea *Cajanus cajan* | Nitrogen fixer, edible peas, chop-and-drop | Shrub that lives a few years; hard frost kills it. |
| Cowpea (cowpea) *Vigna unguiculata* | Nitrogen-fixing groundcover, food | Annual summer plant. |
| Lablab bean *Lablab purpureus* | Climbing nitrogen fixer, fodder | Summer; frost kills it easily. |
| Lupin *Lupinus angustifolius* | Nitrogen-fixing groundcover for winter | Suits the Western Cape winter. Bitter lupin seed is poisonous, so eat only sweet cultivars, and feed only sweet cultivars to animals. |

Wattles, leucaena and sesbania fill this role in many overseas books, but they are invaders here. The indigenous thorn trees above do the same work.

### Planting steps

1. **Peg out the plants.** Measure the gaps according to the size of the canopy and mark each place with a peg or a little lime. Leave the bigger gaps between the big trees.
2. **Label everything.** Put a name tag by every important plant. You will forget what is where.
3. **Dig all the holes and planting basins.**
4. **Keep compost and mulch ready.** **Compost** (compost) is plant and animal waste that has rotted into dark, crumbly food for the soil.
5. **Plant first the big trees that live long,** then the nitrogen-fixing trees.
6. **Plant medium-size trees** between the big trees.
7. **Plant shrubs and herbs** about 1 m from the main fruit trees.
8. **Plant perennial root crops,** then **perennial groundcover** between the basins.
9. **Sow annual legume groundcover last:** cowpea in summer, lupin in winter. Just before you sow, wet the seed slightly and mix it with the right **inoculant** (inoculant), a powder of the nitrogen-fixing bacteria for that legume, which is sold with the seed or at farm stores. Keep inoculated seed out of the sun. Scatter it thickly and cover it lightly with mulch.
10. **Water everything well.**

=== SECTION 06-tree-systems#7 ===
## Planting of a tree

A young tree's hardest time is its first dry season. A hole that is too small, or water that wets only the surface, is enough to kill it. Plant it right once, and the tree looks after itself for decades.

Plant at the beginning of the rains: October to December after the first good rain in summer-rainfall areas, and about April to July in the Western Cape. Plant trees that do not tolerate frost after the last frost. Deciduous fruit trees can be planted in the middle of winter, while they have no leaves.

1. **Choose a good tree.** Buy a sturdy tree with a thick stem and healthy leaves. Avoid trees with roots that grow round in circles inside the bag. For citrus, buy certified disease-free trees.
2. **Plant it soon** after you bought it.
3. **Clear a circle** about 1 m wide, free of weeds and grass, especially kikuyu, so that they do not steal water.
4. **Dig wide, not deep.** Make the hole two to three times as wide as the root ball and about just as deep. In hard soil, dig about 60 cm wide and deep and loosen the bottom and sides. Put the dark **topsoil** (topsoil), the top, darkest layer, on one side and the light subsoil on the other side.
5. **Test the drainage.** Fill the hole with water. If water is still standing the next day, rather plant the tree on a raised mound.
6. **Put in a stake in windy places.** Drive a stake about 50 cm into the ground on the wind side before you plant, so that you do not pierce the roots.
7. **Mix the fill soil:** about one part compost to three parts topsoil, with a little well-rotted manure. Too much compost keeps the roots inside the hole, as if it is a pot.
8. **Set the height.** Loosen any roots that grow in circles. Put the tree down so that the top of the root ball is level with the soil around, and the graft union (the knob where a fruit tree is grafted) stays well above the soil.
9. **Fill and press firm,** gently with your hands or feet. Do not ram/stamp.
10. **Tie it** loosely to the stake with a soft band in a figure-eight, so that the stem can sway slightly. Remove the band after about a year.
11. **Make a basin** about 1 m wide with a low little wall of soil to hold water. Make it wider as the tree grows.
12. **Put mulch** 5 to 10 cm thick in the basin, and keep the mulch about 10 cm away from the stem so that it does not rot.
13. **Water deeply:** a full bucket of 20 L or more just after planting. If no rain comes, water every two to three days for two weeks, and then give about once per week a deep soaking through the first dry season, more often in sand or great heat. Push a finger into the soil: water when it is dry 5 to 10 cm deep.

A deep soaking now and then makes the roots follow the water downward. A little every day keeps them near the surface, where they dry out in the first hot period, and in clay it can make them rot.

=== SECTION 07-earthworks-and-water#2 ===
## Catching and storing rainwater

Water that is piped from far away can stop any time. Water that you caught yourself is there when you need it. The rule is thus simple: **catch rain as close as possible to where it falls**, and store it.

We store it in drums, tanks, ponds and dams. But the biggest storage place is the soil itself. Soil that is rich in **organic matter** (organic matter; plant and animal material that rots), covered with plants and **mulch** (mulch; a blanket of dry grass, leaves or straw), soaks up rain and holds it much better than bare, hard soil. **Permaculture** (permaculture) calls this soil a **living sponge**.

### Eight principles of rainwater harvesting

Brad Lancaster's book *Rainwater Harvesting for Drylands and Beyond* sets out eight principles. In our own words:

1. **Look for a long time.** Walk over the land in the rain. See where water runs and where it stands. Build on what already works.
2. **Start at the top and work downward.** At the top of a slope there is less water and it runs slower, so it is easier to catch. From there it can flow downhill to where you need it.
3. **Start small and simple.** Many small works that you can build and fix yourself are better than one big one.
4. **Slow it, spread it, let it sink in.** Never let water race off the land.
5. **Plan the overflow, and use it.** Lead extra water to where it can do more good.
6. **Keep the soil covered** with plants and mulch, so that the sponge grows every year.
7. **Let every work do several tasks.** A swale berm can also be a dry path; the trees watered by a basin can shade the house.
8. **Keep watching and adjusting.** Every rain season teaches you something, and it brings you back to principle 1.

### Catching water from roofs

Hard, smooth roofs give the most water. Corrugated iron sheets, IBR sheets and tiles give much more than thatch.

1. Put gutters along the low edges of the roof, sloping slightly toward the tank so that water never stands in them.
2. Put a leaf screen over the gutter or the tank's inlet.
3. Put a **first-flush diverter** (first-flush diverter) before the tank. It is a pipe or container that catches the first, dirtiest water of each rain shower, with its dust, droppings and leaves. Empty it after each rain shower.
4. Put an overflow near the top of the tank and lead it to another tank, a pond, a swale or a tree basin, never against a wall.
5. Connect two or more tanks with a pipe near the bottom, so that they fill together and empty together.

Leaves that land in the tank rot and breed germs, so keep the gutters and screens clean. Keep every tank closed with a tight, screened lid, so that children, animals and mosquitoes cannot get in. Roof water is not clean enough to drink as is: boil it first.

### How much water can you catch?

One millimetre of rain on one square metre gives **1 litre** of water. Thus:

**Water caught (litre) = area (m²) × rainfall (mm) × runoff coefficient**

The **runoff coefficient** is the part of the rain that runs off, as a number between 0 and 1. A coefficient of 0.8 means 80% runs off; the rest wets the roof, splashes past the gutter or goes to the first-flush diverter.

Measure the roof **from above**, with the overhangs included. Rain falls straight down, so a steep roof does not catch more than a flat roof over the same floor.

**Worked example: a roof in an area with 750 mm rainfall**

1. The roof measures 12 m × 10 m from above = **120 m²**.
2. Rain that falls on it in an average year = 120 × 750 = 90 000 litres.
3. A metal roof with good gutters has a coefficient of 0.8 to 0.9. Plan with **0.8**.
4. Water that you can catch = 120 × 750 × 0.8 = **72 000 litres per year**.

A new roof with perfect gutters may reach 0.95 (85 500 litres), but plan with 0.8. A dry year gives much less.

The same formula works for any surface. Only the coefficient changes. Runoff is higher on steep slopes, on clay and in heavy storms.

| Surface | Runoff coefficient |
|---|---|
| Metal roof | 0.8 to 0.9 |
| Tile roof | 0.6 to 0.9 |
| Thatch roof | about 0.2 |
| Concrete or tar | 0.7 to 0.95 |
| Dirt road | 0.5 to 0.7 |
| Bare, hard-trampled soil | 0.3 to 0.6 |
| Grass or veld | 0.05 to 0.35 |

Covered soil lets most rain sink in, and that is what we want on land where we grow plants. On hard surfaces we catch water.

### How big must the tank be?

You use water the whole year, so the tank need not hold a whole year's rain. It need only carry you through the dry months.

**Worked example: a tank for the household**

1. Five people plan on 25 litres each per day for drinking, cooking and washing: **125 litres per day**.
2. In a year that is 125 × 365 = 45 625 litres. The 120 m² roof above gives about 72 000 litres, so the roof is big enough.
3. In the summer-rainfall interior the dry season runs roughly from May to September: about **150 days**. (In the Western Cape the dry season is summer.)
4. Water needed to get through it = 125 × 150 = **18 750 litres**.
5. The household thus needs about **20 000 litres** of storage, for example two tanks of 10 000 litres, full when the rain stops.

For a more accurate answer, write down the 12 months. Work out for each month what the roof gives (area × that month's rain × coefficient) and what you use, and keep a running total of what remains in the tank. The biggest shortfall in the dry season is the storage you need.

Garden water comes on top of that, and a vegetable garden in the dry season drinks much more than a household. That is why we also store water in the soil.

## Infiltration basins

An **infiltration basin** is a shallow hollow with a level bottom that catches runoff and lets it sink in (**infiltration** (infiltration) means that water sinks into the soil). Basins suit gentle slopes. They catch water from roads, paths, tank overflows and **greywater** (greywater; used bath and shower water).

1. Dig a shallow hollow with a level bottom below the place where the water comes from.
2. Give each basin an overflow to the next one, so that a chain of basins passes water down the slope and eventually lets it land in a swale or a safe open area.
3. Plant the sides with fruit, nut or **indigenous** (indigenous) trees (trees that grow naturally in our region).
4. Throw plenty of mulch in the basin. Mulch keeps the soil open so that water sinks in quickly.

Keep basins no deeper than about 50 cm, and make sure that the water sinks away within about 12 hours. Roots need air, and mosquitoes need several days of standing water to breed. If a basin stays wet longer, make it shallower, add mulch or divide the water over more basins.

=== SECTION 07-earthworks-and-water#4 ===
## Ponds and dams

A pond turns a single storm into weeks of water. It gives animals and crops water, slows stormwater, can grow fish, and attracts the frogs, birds and dragonflies that eat our pests.

Plan for these things before you dig:

- **Soil.** Water drains away through sand. A pond holds water only in soil with enough clay, or with a lining of compacted clay or a dam liner.
- **Slope.** The steeper the land, the harder it is to design and build a pond or dam. A dam is work for an experienced designer.
- **Overflow.** Every pond and dam needs a wide overflow (spillway), protected with stone or dense grass, so that water never flows over the wall.
- **Evaporation.** Over most of South Africa an open water surface loses more than 1 400 mm per year, and over large parts of the dry interior 2 000 mm or more. That is often more than the rain. At 5 mm per day a pond with a surface of 20 m² loses 100 litres every day. A deep pond with a small surface, shaded by trees, loses less.
- **People.** Let the sides slope while you dig so that they cannot fall in [ambiguous: "they" = the sides cannot cave in / people cannot fall in], and keep everyone far away from a machine that is working. Fence in the finished pond with a gate that a child cannot open, and make one side gently sloping so that a person or animal that falls in can climb out. Where bilharzia occurs, you must not wash, swim or stand barefoot in pond water.

### Working out the volume of a pond

**Volume (m³) ≈ length × width × average depth.** For a bowl-shaped pond the average depth is about half of the deepest point. One cubic metre holds 1 000 litres.

**Worked example: a small pond that catches runoff**

1. The pond is 5 m long, 4 m wide and 1.2 m deep in the middle. Average depth ≈ 0.6 m.
2. Volume ≈ 5 × 4 × 0.6 = **12 m³**, or 12 000 litres.
3. To fill it in one rain shower of 40 mm from a hard surface (coefficient about 1), you need 12 000 ÷ 40 = **300 m²** of roof or paving.
4. From grazed veld (coefficient about 0.2) each m² gives 40 × 0.2 = 8 litres, so you need 12 000 ÷ 8 = **1 500 m²**.

A new pond needs more than that, because the dry ground around drinks first.

=== SECTION 07-earthworks-and-water#6 ===
## Swales

A **swale** is a ditch that is dug exactly along the **contour**, an imaginary line across a slope that joins points at the same height. Because the ditch is level, water does not run along it. It spreads out and sinks in, and the trees below grow deep roots in that stored water.

A swale has two parts: the ditch, and the **berm** (berm), the bank of dug-out soil that is piled up on the downhill side.

### Connecting swales and ponds

1. Runoff fills a swale and spreads along it to a pond at one end.
2. When the pond is full, water pushes back and fills the swale.
3. Extra water runs away over a **spillway**: a short, level piece of the berm that is lower than the rest, protected with stones or dense grass. Keep the top of the berm at least a hand's breadth higher than the spillway, so that water never pours over the berm.
4. The spillway feeds the next swale lower down, and the next, until the water reaches a lower pond or leaves the land.

Water that leaves such a system is slower and clearer, and carries much less soil. A low **diversion mound** built at an angle across a driveway will also turn the driveway's runoff toward a swale.

### How big and how far apart?

A small garden swale can be about two spade blades deep and one wide. A swale on the field can be much bigger. Every swale must be able to hold the runoff of the land above it in a big storm, and the roof formula tells you how much that is.

**Worked example: spacing of swales**

1. The swale is about 0.5 m wide and holds water 0.3 m deep below the spillway. Each metre holds 0.5 × 0.3 = 0.15 m³ = **150 litres**.
2. Plan for a big storm of **50 mm**.
3. The land above is grazed veld, coefficient about 0.3. Each m² gives 50 × 0.3 = **15 litres**.
4. Each metre of swale can handle the runoff of 150 ÷ 15 = 10 m² of land: a strip 1 m wide and 10 m long up the slope.
5. The swales thus come about **10 m apart**, measured down the slope. In a bigger storm the spillways carry the rest.

Where the rain is lighter or the land is well covered, swales can be further apart. On bare land, steeper slopes or in wetter areas, bring them closer together.

### Where you must not dig swales

- **Steep slopes.** Keep swales to slopes of less than about 15% (a drop of 15 m over 100 m). Wet soil on steep land can slide. Rather use terraces and trees there.
- **Heavy clay or waterlogged soil.** If water does not sink in, a swale becomes a ditch with standing, rotten water. Use a pond or a diversion drain.
- **Near buildings.** Keep swales and basins at least 3 m from walls and foundations, and never above a pit latrine, septic tank or soak-away.
- **Very wet places.** Where the ground is wet most of the year, you need drains that lead water away.

### Digging the swale

First peg out the contour with an A-frame (see "Try it" at the end of this chapter). Then:

1. Dig the ditch along the pegs and keep the bottom level.
2. Pile the soil and stones on the **downhill** side and tramp the berm firm layer by layer.
3. Protect the ends and the spillway with stones or tough grass, so that water cannot cut around them.
4. Plant the berm and put mulch on it the same day, before rain can wash it away.

### Planting the swale and berm

Now the fun begins! Plants that like water go in the ditch, useful trees on and below the berm, and plants that prefer it drier, on top.

- **On the berm:** fast-growing pioneer trees and shrubs, such as indigenous **legume** trees (legume; plants of the bean family, many of which bring nitrogen into the soil) such as *Vachellia* species, to hold the bank and build up fertility.
- **Mulch plants on the berm:** comfrey (comfrey), lemongrass (lemongrass), yarrow (yarrow), nettle (nettle) and sterile vetiver grass, which are often cut for mulch and **compost** (compost; plant waste that has rotted to dark, crumbly soil food). The berm becomes a mulch bank.
- **Soil builders:** clover (clover), lucerne (lucerne) and sunn hemp. Let dandelion (dandelion) and plantain (plantain) grow and cut them for mulch.
- **In the ditch:** cowpeas (cowpea) or lupin (lupin) as cover, and plants that like water, such as amadumbe (taro, *Colocasia esculenta*). Keep amadumbe in garden swales and beds, away from streams and wetlands, where it can spread.
- **Below the swale:** lemons and other citrus, bananas where there is no frost, and tomatoes. Think of the shade they will cast.
- **Under the trees:** **groundcover** (groundcover; low plants that cover the soil) that tolerates shade, such as sweet potatoes and New Zealand spinach (New Zealand spinach).

=== SECTION 07-earthworks-and-water#7 ===
## Preparing the soil

Every time we turn the soil, we break the tunnels, fungi and crumbs that took years to build up. Choose therefore the method that disturbs the soil the least. As the farm matures and makes its own mulch and compost, you need to disturb it less and less.

### Start every time the same way

Whatever tool follows, start like this:

1. Pull out the worst spreading weeds, such as kikuyu runners (kikuyu) and couch grass (couch grass), which digging would multiply.
2. Cut the vegetation as close as possible to the ground.
3. Water well, or wait for rain, and let the soil drain until it is moist but not wet.

### By hand

For a small or medium area, use a pick-mattock, a spade and a garden fork.

1. Remove the cut material for compost or mulch.
2. Loosen the topsoil with a fork.
3. Rake off any debris and level the surface.

### With a tractor

Repeated ploughing is worldwide one of the main causes of soil erosion and loss of soil carbon. Heavy machines compact the soil, and ploughing every year forms a hard layer just below the blade, which is called a **plough pan**. Ploughing gives a quick flare-up of fertility while air streams into the soil, and then fertility drops season after season.

If we thus use a tractor at all, we use it mainly to set up the farm. **Rippers and chisel ploughs** loosen hard soil without turning it, and they are good for restoring damaged soil. Choose the tool that turns the soil the least, and make as few passes as you can.

A **two-wheel tractor** is a machine you walk behind and which pulls a rotavator (rotating blades that break up the soil), a small plough or a mower. It costs much less to buy and run than a big tractor. Use the rotavator sparingly; regular rotavating also breaks down soil structure.

### Animal tractors

An **animal tractor** (animal tractor) uses animals to clear and prepare land (see Chapter 5). Chickens, ducks, sheep, goats and cattle graze it bare; pigs also dig.

1. Cut big plants down to the ground. If you want pigs to dig, water the area first.
2. Put a movable fence around the area. An electric fence on a solar energiser works well.
3. Give the animals shelter inside the fence if they need it, such as a movable coop for chickens.
4. Let chickens come behind cattle or pigs. They spread the manure and eat the fly larvae in it.
5. Loosen the soil with a fork or chisel plough, without turning it, and rake level.

### No-dig gardening and sheet mulching

No-dig gardening imitates the forest floor. Leaves fall, rot and feed the soil, and it becomes loose and spongy without any digging. Earthworms and fungi do the work. Buried weed seed stays in the dark and stays asleep, which saves hours of hoeing. The price is a lot of organic matter.

**Sheet mulching** (sheet mulching, or lasagne beds) builds no-dig layers on top of the soil. It smothers grass and feeds soil life from above, and it is the fastest way to start a small garden on hard, bare ground.

1. Cut the grass and bushes and let them lie where they fall. Water well.
2. Spread animal manure 6 to 10 cm thick. Fresh manure goes only here, at the bottom, to wake the soil life.
3. Cover it with cardboard or newspaper, overlapping so that no light comes through. Wet it.
4. Add 10 to 15 cm straw or dry mulch, then a layer of compost.
5. Add another 10 to 15 cm seed-free straw.
6. Finish with compost or good topsoil. What you plant into must be well-made compost or well-rotted manure, never fresh manure.
7. Water, make small holes through the top layers, plant seedlings in the compost, and put mulch around them.

### Solarisation and tarping

**Solarisation** cooks weeds with the sun. Water the soil, cover it tightly with **transparent** plastic, bury the edges and leave it for at least 4 to 6 weeks in the hottest weather. It also kills some of the useful soil life, so use it only where you must.

**Tarping** (tarping) uses **black** plastic. It heats the soil less and works by cutting off the light. Cover the cut, watered area, hold the tarp down with stones or sandbags and leave it 6 to 10 weeks. Then loosen the topsoil by rocking a fork back and forth, without turning it.

Market gardeners use a tarp to make a **stale seedbed** (stale seedbed): prepare the bed and water, cover it, let the weed seed germinate in the dark and die, then lift the tarp after a few weeks and sow in the clean bed.

=== SECTION 07-earthworks-and-water#8 ===
## Shaping vegetable beds

A bed is where water, soil and your back meet. Shape it well and the soil gets better every year. These are guidelines, not rules.

Raised beds suit moderate and high rainfall. In very dry areas, make **sunken beds**, slightly lower than the paths, so that they catch the rain like basins.

If the slope is more than about 3% (a drop of 3 m over 100 m), lay out beds and paths **along the contour**, so that heavy rain does not run down them. On flat land you are free to make circles, spirals and mandalas.

### Raised double-reach beds

A **double-reach bed** (double-reach bed) is narrow enough that you can reach the middle from the paths on both sides, so that you never stand on it and compact the soil.

- Beds 80 to 120 cm wide. Market gardens often use 75 to 90 cm, which suits hand tools and two-wheel tractors.
- Paths 40 to 60 cm wide.
- Beds about 8 m long in a small garden, up to 30 m in a commercial one.

Raised beds lift roots out of waterlogged soil and deepen the topsoil with soil from the paths, and compost goes only where the crops grow. The soil is never trodden and hardly dug, and gets better every year, until a garden fork sinks in up to the handle.

1. Mark the top contour with an A-frame. If there is space, put a small swale above the top bed.
2. Measure the other beds and paths down the slope, and mark the edges with lime or ash. Lay a garden hose along curved edges.
3. Starting at the top, dig soil out of the path above each bed and throw it on the bed below. Raise an average bed 10 to 15 cm.
4. Level each bed with a rake and make neat edges.
5. Add any lime or gypsum that your soil test shows you need, and about 3 cm compost.
6. Fork the bed through without turning it, put a thick mulch on it and water.
7. Let the bed rest a few weeks before you plant.

In small gardens, edge beds with stones, thick branches or pallet wood. Lay cardboard over the grass inside, fill with good topsoil and put compost on top. This works even on concrete.

### Trench beds

A **trench bed** is a deep bed that is filled with organic matter. It turns stony or clay soil into soil in which plants can grow, and holds water in dry areas. It is heavy to dig, but lasts for years.

1. Mark a bed of about **1 m wide** and 2 to 5 m long, and dig it about **60 cm deep**.
2. Put the dark topsoil on one side and the lighter subsoil on the other side.
3. Lay sticks or coarse material at the bottom.
4. Fill with layers: about 20 cm organic matter (dry leaves, grass, kitchen waste, manure), then about 10 cm subsoil. Water each layer.
5. Finish with the topsoil, and then mulch.

The bed sinks as it rots. Top it up with compost every season.

### Terrace beds

On steep land a terrace is a level step that is cut into the slope. The front bank works like the berm of a swale and holds water and soil. Build the walls of stones, logs or old tyres. Where wattle has invaded, cut stems woven into screens hold soil well; use only dry stems without seed pods, so that no seed spreads.

### Pit beds and banana circles

A **pit bed** is a round pit of about 50 cm deep (up to 1 m) and 1 to 2 m wide, which is filled with organic matter. Plants grow around the rim and feed on the rotting and the water in the middle. A **banana circle** is the best-known kind.

1. Choose a place where water comes: under a tap, near a seep, or where greywater or runoff flows.
2. Lift the sods and the topsoil and put them aside.
3. Dig a small channel to lead water to the middle, then dig the pit and fill it with organic matter.
4. Put the soil back as a low ring around the rim, plant it and mulch well.
5. Keep feeding the middle with organic matter.

Bananas, amadumbe, sweet potatoes, ginger and papayas do well in frost-free areas. You can even put a wooden floor with slats over the pit and shower on it.

### Mandala and keyhole beds

Round beds, **keyhole beds** (keyhole beds; a round bed with a path cut into the middle) and mandalas suit flat land. From one place you can reach a large part of the bed, so put the crops you pick most near the path. Put tall plants on the south side, because the sun is in the north.

=== SECTION 07-earthworks-and-water#10 ===
## Using greywater for irrigation

Every bath and shower sends good water down the drain. On a dry farm that water can keep a row of fruit trees alive through the dry season.

**Greywater** is used water from baths, showers, hand basins and the washing machine. **Blackwater** (blackwater) is water from toilets. Blackwater and nappy water carry germs and never go on the garden. Water from the kitchen sink is the dirtiest greywater, full of fat and food; use it only through a grease trap, and only on trees and flowers.

| Water source | On the garden? |
|---|---|
| Shower, bath, hand basin | Yes, carefully |
| Washing machine | Yes, with mild, low-salt detergent |
| Kitchen sink | Only through a grease trap, on trees and flowers |
| Nappy washing or toilet | No |

### The rules

1. **Into the soil, under mulch.** Lead greywater to a tree basin, swale or pit bed with mulch, through a pipe that ends under the mulch. Never spray it.
2. **Never on food that is eaten raw.** Keep it away from morogo, lettuce and root crops such as carrots. Use it on fruit trees, bananas, shrubs and flowers.
3. **No pools.** It must sink in quickly, where people and animals will not touch it.
4. **Use it the same day.** Stored greywater goes bad and breeds germs and mosquitoes.
5. **Far from drinking water.** Never near, or above, a well, borehole or spring.
6. **Mild soap.** Salts, bleach, borax and strong chemicals build up and damage soil and plants. Use mild, biodegradable, low-salt soap; liquid laundry soap usually carries less salt than powder.
7. **Move it around.** Alternate between basins and let rain wash the soil out. Yellow leaf edges or a white crust mean that salt is building up.

### A simple greywater system

1. Find where the bath, shower and basin drain leaves the house, usually at the bathroom wall.
2. Connect a PVC pipe to the outlet and lead it to the garden, sloping slightly downhill all the way.
3. Put a flexible hose on the end, so that you can move the outlet around between mulched basins.
4. Keep the outlet buried in mulch.

Once greywater has sunk into healthy mulched soil, soil life and roots break down most of what it carries.

=== SECTION 07-earthworks-and-water#12 ===
## Key points

- Design for the biggest storm: start at the top, and give every earthwork a safe spillway.
- Rain caught (litres) = area (m²) × rainfall (mm) × runoff coefficient. A metal roof of 120 m² in an area with 750 mm rain gives about 72 000 litres per year.
- Size the tank for the dry season, not for the whole year's rain.
- Swales follow the contour exactly; space them so that each can hold a big storm.
- Disturb the soil as little as possible, water at the roots, and send greywater under mulch to trees.

Walk over your land in the next heavy rain shower and watch where the water goes. That already shows you where the first swale belongs.

=== SECTION 08-soil#3 ===
## Test your soil

Most smallholders have a feeling about their soil. The feeling is often wrong.

### The feel test (ribbon test)

1. Take a small handful of soil from about 10 cm deep. Remove stones and roots.
2. Add water little by little and knead until the soil is like putty and just stops sticking to your hand.
3. Feel it. Gritty means sand. Smooth and silky means silt. Sticky means clay.
4. Press the ball out between your thumb and forefinger into a flat ribbon, and let it hang until it breaks.
5. Measure the ribbon.

| Length of the ribbon | Your soil |
|---|---|
| No ribbon, falls apart | Sand or loamy sand |
| Less than 2.5 cm | Sandy loam or loam |
| 2.5 to 5 cm | Clay loam |
| More than 5 cm | Clay |

### The jar test

1. Take soil from several places in the bed, from the top 15 cm. Mix it and remove stones, roots and leaves.
2. Fill a transparent jar with straight sides one third full of soil and almost to the rim with water, and add a teaspoon of dishwashing soap to help separate the particles.
3. Shake hard for 3 to 5 minutes and put the jar down where no one will move it.
4. After **1 minute**, mark the top of the bottom layer: that is **sand**.
5. After **2 hours**, mark the top of the next layer: **silt**.
6. After **1 to 2 days**, when the water is mostly clear, mark the top layer: **clay**. Heavy clay can take a week or longer. Anything that floats is organic matter.
7. Compare each layer with the total height of the soil in the jar.

A loam is roughly 40% sand, 40% silt and 20% clay. More than half sand means a sandy soil; a thick clay layer and water that stays cloudy for days means a clay soil.

### Read the land

While you dig, write down what you see. Darker soil usually contains more organic matter. Rushes and reeds show wet soil. Stones such as sandstone, shale or basalt (the dark volcanic rock of Lesotho and the Drakensberg) tell you what the soil is made of. Healthy soil smells sweet and earthy; a sour smell can mean that the soil is waterlogged. Count the earthworms in a spadeful of soil. Look whether rain sinks in or runs off. And ask what the land was used for before: old kraals, fields and rubbish dumps all change the soil.

### Soil pH: sour or sweet?

**pH** measures how acid or alkaline (sweet) the soil is, on a scale of 0 to 14. A pH of 7 is neutral, below 7 is acid and above 7 is alkaline.

- Most vegetables grow best between about pH 6 and 7, measured in water.
- In very acid soil phosphorus gets locked up and aluminium poisons the roots.
- Many soils in the wetter east of South Africa are acid. Soils in the dry west and in low-lying areas can be alkaline or brackish (saline).

A cheap pH kit gives a rough reading; a soil laboratory gives a precise reading and says how much lime you need. South African laboratories often report **pH (KCl)**, which reads about 0.5 to 1 unit lower than pH in water. A pH (KCl) below about 4.5 is too acid for most crops. Spread agricultural lime or dolomitic lime at the amount that is on the report. Organic matter also helps to buffer pH.

=== SECTION 08-soil#4 ===
## Look after your soil

Nature never leaves soil bare. The floor of a forest or of healthy veld is always covered, always fed, and is never dug. We imitate this.

- **Keep it covered** with **mulch** (a blanket of dry grass, straw or leaves) or with living plants. Bare soil bakes, forms a crust and blows away.
- **Feed it.** Crop residues, **compost** (rotted plant and animal waste) and manure go back onto the soil, never onto the fire.
- **Grow many kinds of plants.** Different roots reach to different depths and feed different soil life.
- **Dig less.** Every time you dig or plough, you break the soil's structure and burn up organic matter. Rather loosen with a fork.
- **Stay off the beds**, especially when they are wet. Walk on paths.
- **Let it rest.** Give an exhausted bed a season under green manure or mulch.

Covered, undisturbed soil gets deeper and richer every year. There are five main ways to feed it: green manure, animal manure, mulch, compost and worms.

=== SECTION 08-soil#6 ===
## Way 2: Animal manure

Every animal on the farm is a small fertiliser factory. Manure adds organic matter and nutrients, especially nitrogen, and feeds soil life. Cattle, sheep, goat, horse, rabbit and chicken manure all work; chicken manure is the strongest, and burns roots when it is fresh. An **animal tractor** (animal tractor), a movable coop with chickens that stands on a bed that is resting, spreads manure straight onto the soil (see the chapter on animal systems).

### How to use manure

1. Compost manure before it comes near crops that you eat raw. Composting kills most germs and weed seed, prevents the manure from burning roots, and turns it into **humus**: the dark, stable part of organic matter that holds water and nutrients.
2. If you must use raw manure, dig it in at least 4 months (120 days) before you harvest crops that touch the soil or are eaten raw, such as lettuce, spinach and carrots. For crops that grow above the ground, such as maize and fruit trees, give at least 3 months (90 days). Never put fresh manure around vegetables that are already growing.
3. Never use human faeces, or dog or cat droppings, in the vegetable garden. They carry worms and other parasites that infect people, especially children.
4. Use pig manure only after thorough hot composting and long storage, and never on crops that are eaten raw. Its roundworm eggs survive for years.
5. Wash your hands after you have handled manure, and wash vegetables before you eat them.

Know where your manure comes from. Do not use manure from sick animals, nor manure from animals that were dosed against worms in the last few weeks (for example with ivermectin), because it harms dung beetles and earthworms. Some long-lasting herbicides that are sprayed on pasture pass through animals and survive composting, and small amounts damage tomatoes, beans and potatoes. So ask whether the animals grazed on sprayed pasture.
