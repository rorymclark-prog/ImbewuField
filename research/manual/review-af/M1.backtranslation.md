=== SECTION 01-what-is-permaculture#2 ===
## Why permaculture matters in Southern Africa

Families who live from the soil feel the weather first. Two kinds of pressure are increasing.

- **Climate change** (climate change) is the long-term shift in the weather that is mainly caused by people. Farmers already see warmer days, rain that comes late or all at once, longer droughts and heavier storms. Most smallholders farm with rain, not with irrigation, and therefore they carry the most risk.
- **Erosion** (erosion) is soil that is washed away or blown away. Bare, overgrazed or steeply ploughed soil loses its topsoil (topsoil) with every storm. Soil erosion has affected more than 70% of South Africa's soil. Lesotho, where this manual began, also has serious erosion and deep dongas (ditches/gullies).

South Africa is also a dry country. It gets on average about 450 mm of rain per year, about half of the world average of about 860 mm. Every drop counts.

Land that loses its soil and water stops feeding its people, and they move away. We cannot farm the old way and expect a different result.

Permaculture gives us a map and tools: protect the soil, catch the rain, grow many crops, work together with nature. It pays back in daily life.

- **Food throughout the year.** Vegetables, fruit, grain and **legumes** (legume) (the bean and pea family), grown without poison. A varied plate is a healthier plate.
- **Money saved and money earned.** Fewer bought inputs, and many products, so that one failed harvest does not make the household go under. Surplus can be sold, dried or preserved (bottled).

=== SECTION 01-what-is-permaculture#4 ===
## The design principles of permaculture

The ethics tell us what matters. The principles are thinking tools to design it: to plan a farm, to see how its parts are connected, and to solve problems for good.

Teachers use slightly different lists. The twelve here come from David Holmgren's book *Permaculture: Principles and Pathways Beyond Sustainability* (2002). Holmgren linked each principle to an old proverb, and we give some of those sayings here.

### 1. Observe and exchange [lit. "wissel uit" = exchange/swap]

You cannot design for land you do not know. Where does the water run? Where does the frost lie? What grows well without help? Books and teachers help, but nothing replaces your own time on your own land.

1. Walk over your land, and the veld around it, in every season.
2. Sit still and look at the plants, birds, insects and animals.
3. Keep a notebook of the rain, the first and last frost, and when things flower. Many farmers also write down the phases of the moon.
4. Walk out during and after heavy rain. See where the water runs and where it pools.

After a year of observation you will know your land better than any visitor. Your design will fit it instead of fighting it.

### 2. Catch and store energy

*Make hay while the sun shines.*

Nature moves in cycles: abundance, then shortage. Over most of South Africa's interior the warm, wet summer is the growing season, and the winter is cold, dry and slow. In the Western Cape the rain comes in winter and the summer is dry. We catch what is abundant and store it for the scarce time.

- Catch the summer sun as crops: vegetables, fruit, nuts, fodder (fodder), firewood and meat.
- Catch the rain in tanks, dams and the soil itself, with **mulch** (mulch) (a covering of dry grass or leaves on the soil) and **swales** (swale) (level ditches dug along a slope to let rain sink in).
- Dry, preserve and preserve summer's surplus for the winter.

A farm that stores well does not panic when the rain stops.

### 3. Obtain a yield

*You cannot work on an empty stomach.*

A **yield** is anything useful the land gives you: food, fodder, firewood, shade, medicine, seed or income. A fruit tree may need a few seasons before it bears, so plant fast and slow yields together, and there is always something to show for this season's work. We design for surplus: enough for the family, the soil, the animals, the neighbours and the market.

- Sprouts of mung beans or lentils are ready to eat within about 2 to 5 days. Use clean water, rinse them twice a day, and throw away any that smell bad or feel slimy.
- Fruit trees start slowly, but once they bear, they give a harvest for many years.
- Plant flowers for bees. Many indigenous (indigenous) aloes flower in winter, when few other things flower. The bees then pollinate your crops and can give you honey.

### 4. Apply self-regulation and accept feedback

Natural systems keep themselves in balance. In a healthy veld no single plant or animal takes over for long. People are different: we often take much more than we need, and that overuse drives climate change and the loss of wild plants and animals.

Therefore we **set limits**: we live simply and take what we need, so that our children inherit living soil. And we **accept feedback**, the signs that tell us whether what we do works. Mistakes are part of learning. Listen to the crops, the soil, the animals and other people, and change what does not work.

- Combine errands into one trip, walk, or ride a bicycle.
- Read your plants. Yellow leaves, weak growth or an outbreak of pests is the land telling you that something in the soil, the water or your plant choice must change.

### 5. Use and value renewable resources and services

Sunlight, wind, rain, plants and animals renew themselves. Petrol, diesel and chemical fertiliser are used once and then gone. They cost money, and burning fossil fuels contributes to climate change.

Plants and animals can do much of the work of a tractor or brushcutter. They give fuel and fertiliser, control weeds and insects, prevent erosion, recycle nutrients and loosen the soil. It takes time to build them up, but over time they breed and keep themselves going.

- **Animal tractors.** An **animal tractor** (animal tractor) is a movable pen with chickens or pigs, which scratch and dig for worms, roots and insects. Keep them for a few days on an overgrown patch and they clean it and fertilise it, ready to plant.
- **Manure.** Animals eat plants and give the nutrients back as manure and manure tea. Compost manure before it comes near crops you eat raw.
- **Living hedges.** A hedge of useful plants that are not invaders gives shelter, fodder and firewood without wire or fuel.

### 6. Produce no waste

*Who does not waste, does not want.*

In nature there is no rubbish: the output of one part is the input of the next. On the farm we aim for **closed loops**, where nutrients and energy circulate and come out as a yield. What finally leaves the farm should be clean. A good rule: **rethink, reduce, repair, reuse, recycle.**

- **Kitchen scraps** feed worms, and are a valuable "green" (nitrogen-rich) ingredient in the **compost** heap (compost). Compost is plant and animal waste that has rotted into dark, crumbly food for the soil.
- **Grass clippings** make mulch around seedlings. It keeps the soil cool and moist and holds weeds back. Spread it thin or let it dry first, because a thick layer of fresh grass mats together and gets hot.
- **Greywater** (greywater) is used water from baths, showers and basins. It goes under mulch to fruit trees, never onto morogo you eat raw, and kitchen or "doek" water [nappy water? / cloth water — ambiguous] never goes into it. Use mild soap with little salt.

### 7. Design from patterns to details

Nature is full of patterns: the branching of a river or a tree, the spiral of a shell, water running downhill. We first find the big pattern of the site, and then fill in the details. Mollison thought pattern so important that he devoted a whole chapter to it in his book *Permaculture: A Designers' Manual* (1988).

1. Start with the shape of the land: its slopes and **contours** (contour), the lines across a slope that stay at one height.
2. Find the **catchment** (catchment), the area your water comes from, and follow where it goes.
3. Note the climate and the sun's path. In Southern Africa the sun is in the north, and lower in winter.
4. Only then place the details: which bed, which tree, which crop.

The contour is the pattern; the swale dug along it is the detail. Also borrow nature's shapes: paths that wind like a stream, beds in the shape of a spiral or a keyhole.

### 8. Integrate rather than segregate

*Many hands make light work.*

When every part of the farm helps the others, the whole becomes stronger than its parts. Placing each thing (house, garden, chickens, tank, trees) where it can serve the others is the heart of permaculture design. People too: children and older people have much to give and to learn, and belong in the life of the farm.

- Mix trees, crops and animals (**agroforestry**). Trees give shade and fodder, animals give manure, and crops give food and leftovers for the animals.
- Build **guilds** (guild): groups of plants around a main plant, usually a fruit tree, that help it with pest control, fertility or shade.
- Make safe places where children and grandparents can work and learn in the garden.

### 9. Use small and slow solutions

*Slow but sure wins the race.*

Many farmers start big, with fire in the belly, and lose heart when the plot is too big to manage. A small plot that is well cared for gives more than a big one that goes wild.

1. Start at your back door with a small kitchen garden.
2. Get it working before you start the next step.
3. Bring a little more land into production each year.
4. Build the soil season after season with compost, mulch and cover crops.

A big tractor on a small field is expensive and packs the soil hard; chickens in a movable pen work slowly, but clean the soil and fertilise it for very little. Slow change gives you time to see how the soil responds, and changes made this way last. Have a plan, and expect it to change.

### 10. Use and value diversity

*Don't put all your eggs in one basket.*

Diversity is strength. Think of a rope: one strand breaks, but many strands twisted together hold. A **monoculture**, one crop over a large area and often of nearly identical seed, is poor in life and open to every pest, disease and bad season. A diverse farm bends and recovers.

Diversity only works when the parts are connected. Every crop and animal needs a place and a purpose.

- Grow vegetables, grain, legumes, and fruit and nut trees that give food at different times of the year. If one crop fails, another still feeds you.
- Save seed each season from many of your healthiest plants. The seed adapts to your land, and saving from many plants keeps it diverse.
- Keep animals that help each other. Chickens that walk behind cattle scratch the manure apart and eat the fly larvae in it.

### 11. Use edges and value the marginal

An **edge** (edge) is where two different areas meet: a dam and dry land, forest and grassland. Edges are often the busiest and most productive places, visited by creatures from both sides. As Holmgren put it: "The interface between things is where the most interesting events take place."

The marginal areas (corners, hedges, walls, steep banks and wet patches) are usually ignored. Put them to work.

- Plant hedges of indigenous plants along crop fields. Kei apple (*Dovyalis caffra*) makes a thorny hedge that bears fruit. Cape honeysuckle (*Tecoma capensis*) and plumbago (*Plumbago auriculata*) attract birds and useful insects.
- Lay a path between two growing areas, such as a **food forest** (food forest) (a garden planted in layers like a natural forest) and the vegetable beds, so you tend both equally.
- Put the chicken coop next to the garden. Weeds go in; manure comes out.
- Grow up against walls and fences. Climbing beans, cucumbers and gourds turn a bare edge into food.

### 12. Creatively use and respond to change

*Vision is to see things not as they are, but as they will be.*

Nothing in nature stands still. We cannot stop change, but we can expect it and act at the right time. A caterpillar needs different food than the butterfly it becomes; a farm also needs different care at each stage.

- A food forest grows from young trees and fast-growing plants to a shady, mature forest. What you plant and how you tend it must change along with it.
- Rain and temperature shift from year to year. Let your crop choices shift with them.

## Step 2: Zone planning

How far you walk decides what you look after. A garden at the far end is forgotten; a garden by the kitchen door gets water. Zone planning divides the farm into **zones** (zone) according to how often you visit: daily things near the house, things you rarely visit, further away.

| Zone | What it is | How often you visit it | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | The house | All the time | Kitchen, seed store, tanks at the house |
| 1 | Right next to the house | Every day, often many times | Kitchen garden, herbs, nursery, compost, small animals |
| 2 | Near the house | Most days | Food forest, fruit trees, chickens, staple crops |
| 3 | Main farming area | Weekly, or when needed | Field and market crops, orchard, grazing, dams |
| 4 | Semi-wild, lightly managed | Occasionally | Woodlot, grazing |
| 5 | Wild | Rarely, to observe and learn | Natural veld or forest |

**Zone 1** gives the highest yield from the smallest area. Put here the **annual** vegetables (annual) (those that live one season) and morogo you pick often, kitchen herbs, dwarf fruit trees, small animals such as quails and rabbits, and small water stores. Cover every tank and well, and fence off ponds or cover them, so that no child can fall in.

**Zone 2** is bigger and less intensive. Its **food forest** (food forest), a garden planted in layers like a natural forest, can include trees for **coppicing** (coppicing): cutting a tree back to a low stump so that it grows new poles. For firewood and poles, plant sweet thorn (*Vachellia karroo*), an **indigenous** (indigenous) tree (one that grows naturally here), rather than wattle or bluegum, which spread in veld and streams.

**Zone 3** contains livestock, field crops and farm dams. **Zone 4** gives grazing, firewood and timber. **Zone 5** is natural land left wild, a refuge for the birds and insects that help control pests. Visit it to observe, learn and gather wild food carefully.

Not every site has room for all the zones. A town plot of about 1 000 square metres (a quarter acre) has room for Zone 1 and bits of the others; a corner left for birds and insects is a small Zone 5. On communal land Zones 4 and 5 can be shared grazing or veld. And zones are not neat circles: slope, water and paths bend them into other shapes.

=== SECTION 02-planning-your-farm#4 ===
## Step 3: Slope planning

Water runs downhill for free. Slope planning, following Bill Mollison's model, means looking at your land from the side and asking: where am I on the hill? Use the slope well and gravity does the carrying.

- **Water high up.** Put dams and tanks above the house and garden, and gravity moves the water without a pump. Every 10 m of height gives about 1 bar of pressure.
- **Trees on the steep upper slopes** slow runoff, help water sink in and hold the soil.
- **Beds, paths and swales on the contour** keep water in the soil.
- **Compost heaps and animal shelters open downhill,** so that compost and manure go down to the garden. [ambiguous: "maak afdraand oop" — open towards downhill]
- **Drinking water stays clean.** Keep manure, compost and animal pens below (downhill of), and far away from, boreholes, wells and springs.
- **Greywater down to trees.** **Greywater** (greywater), used water from washing, baths and basins, flows by gravity to mulched tree basins or to swales below the house, never onto morogo or other vegetables you eat raw (chapter 7).

=== SECTION 02-planning-your-farm#6 ===
## Key points

- Observe your land through the seasons, and put what you see on a base map.
- Read your site for water, slope, aspect and access. North- and east-facing slopes are the warmest.
- Zones 0 to 5 place things according to how often you visit them.
- Put water high up and let gravity move it. Keep manure away from drinking water, and greywater away from vegetables eaten raw.

Keep your map on the wall and add to it every season. In a few years it will be the most useful book about your land.

=== SECTION 03-sector-planning#2 ===
## Find north

Every sector depends on north. Here are three simple ways to find it.

### With a compass

A compass points to magnetic north, not true north. In South Africa magnetic north lies about 20 to 28 degrees west of true north: in 2025 about 21 degrees in Johannesburg, 27 in Cape Town and 28 in Durban. True north is therefore a little to the right (east) of the needle. Many compass apps on phones show true north for you.

### With the rising sun

Look at the rising sun: north is on your left, south on your right. But the sunrise moves. It is only around 21 March and 23 September due east. In December it rises about 26 to 29 degrees south of east, and in June about 26 to 29 degrees north of east.

### With your midday shadow

At midday the sun is in the north for almost all of Southern Africa, so your shadow points south. Solar noon falls between about 11:45 and 13:00 on the clock, later in the west. Only in the far north of Limpopo in midsummer can it stand slightly south.

> **Tip:** Once you have found north, mark it in the garden with a painted stone or post, and you will never have to look for it again.

=== SECTION 03-sector-planning#3 ===
## Wind

The sun heats land, sea and rock unevenly. Warm air rises, cool air sinks, and air rushes from high pressure to low pressure. That is wind.

Wind dries out soil and plants, breaks crops and trees, blows **topsoil** (topsoil) (the dark, living top layer of the soil) away and drives fires. It also pumps water, cools a house in summer and carries maize pollen.

### Winds in Southern Africa

| Wind | Where | When | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| Berg wind | Coast and escarpment of the Western, Southern and Eastern Cape and KwaZulu-Natal | Mostly in winter, often a day or two before a cold front | Hot, very dry wind from the interior to the coast. Very high fire danger. |
| Cold-front winds | The Western Cape the strongest, then the rest of the country | Winter | North-westerly winds bring rain to the Western Cape. Behind the front, cold southerly winds bring frost to the interior and snow on high ground. |
| South-easter ("Cape Doctor") | Cape Town and the south-western Cape | Summer | Strong, dry, gusty wind that dries soil, damages crops and spreads fire. |
| August winds | Highveld and the interior | Late winter and early spring | Dry, gusty winds over dry veld. Dust and very high fire danger. |

### Find your main wind directions

1. Observe for a year: the trees that lean, the smoke, the dust, the washing on the line.
2. Put up a wind vane or a windsock, a cloth tube on a pole.
3. Ask the older people in the area which winds are the worst, and when they come.
4. Name each wind after where it comes from: a north-westerly blows from the north-west.
5. Draw each one on your sector map with its season, and plant windbreaks across the harmful ones (chapter 6).

=== SECTION 03-sector-planning#4 ===
## Fire

Grassland, savanna and fynbos need fire at the right intervals. But a veld fire that gets out of control kills people and livestock, and burns grazing, crops, houses and fences.

In summer-rainfall areas the fire season runs from about May to October, the worst in late winter and early spring before the rains. In the winter-rainfall Western Cape it runs from about December to March. In the Southern Cape and KwaZulu-Natal berg-wind days in winter bring sudden, very high fire danger.

### How fire behaves

Fire can come from any direction, but on most land it comes mainly from one: your fire sector. That is usually the direction of the hot, dry wind, and your neighbours will know it.

Fire runs much faster uphill than downhill. Hold a burning match upside down and watch the flame climb. A common rule of thumb is that fire's speed doubles for every 10 degrees of uphill slope, so on a slope of 20 degrees it spreads about four times as fast as on level ground. A house at the top of a steep, grass-covered slope is in the greatest danger. Put your protections below (downhill), and in the fire sector.

Protect people and the house first, then livestock, stores, stored fodder and fruit trees.

### Ways to reduce fire risk

1. **Reduce fuel.** In the fire sector and around buildings, keep grass short and clear up dry leaves and dead wood. Store firewood, hay and thatch away from the house.
2. **Make firebreaks.** Clear a strip of everything that can burn. Roads, ploughed land and dams also work as firebreaks. Burn a firebreak only on a cool, windless day when the fire danger is low, never alone, and with enough people, water and fire beaters to keep it under control.
3. **Plant green, slow-burning plants.** No plant is fireproof, but fleshy, moist leaves with little oil or resin burn slowly: aloes, spekboom (*Portulacaria afra*, which does not like hard frost), *Cotyledon*, vygies (*Delosperma*), sour fig (*Carpobrotus edulis*), bietou (*Osteospermum moniliferum*) and num-num (*Carissa*).
4. **Keep oily trees away from buildings.** Pines, bluegums and wattles burn fiercely.
5. **Keep water ready**: a tank and garden hose near the house, and fire beaters or a knapsack sprayer.
6. **Stack functions.** A watered garden or orchard around the house is a green buffer, and a strip of slow-burning plants slows wind as well as fire.

=== SECTION 03-sector-planning#9 ===
## Climate change

**Climate change** (climate change) is the long-term shift in weather patterns, mainly driven by human activity: more heat, changing rain, wilder weather and less water.

Southern Africa is warming faster than the world average, parts of the interior about twice as fast. Heatwaves occur more, and more droughts and fire-danger days are expected. Climate change made the Cape Town drought of 2015 to 2017 about three times more likely.

### Design for a changing climate

We can no longer rely on a normal year. Therefore we design for the bad year, and we build **resilience**: the ability to endure a shock and recover.

1. Plant many different crops, so that one failure does not ruin you.
2. Choose tough plants, including **indigenous** (indigenous) plants that grow naturally here, and **perennial plants** (perennial), which live for many seasons.
3. Keep hardy local breeds: indigenous chickens, goats and Nguni cattle.
4. Plant windbreaks and store water to soften extreme weather.
5. Keep the soil covered with cover crops and mulch, and help rain sink in with **swales** (swale), level ditches dug across the slope.
6. Preserve your surplus. Dry it, pickle it in plenty of vinegar, or make jam and chutney. Home-bottling low-acid food, such as beans, pumpkin or meat, without a tested method can cause botulism, a deadly food poisoning.
7. Save your own seed, so that it adapts to your land, and share it through a community seed bank.
8. Move grazing animals regularly, and give the veld time to rest.
9. Build outside the floodplain. Keep firebreaks and water ready, and store food, seed and fodder for hard times.

=== SECTION 04-vegetable-and-staple-crops#1 ===
## Growing food in Southern African climates

Here there is not just one planting calendar. Know your climate:

- **Summer-rainfall interior** (Highveld, Free State, large parts of the Eastern Cape, the KwaZulu-Natal Midlands, Lesotho): rain falls from about October to March; winters are dry and cold, often with frost.
- **Winter-rainfall Western Cape**: rain falls from about May to September. Summer crops need irrigation.
- **Frost-free lowveld and coast** (the KwaZulu-Natal coast, the Limpopo and Mpumalanga lowveld): with water you can plant most of the year.

In cold areas frost is the greatest danger. Cold air is heavy. On a still, clear night it flows downhill like water and collects in low places called **frost pockets (frost pocket)**. Work with it:

1. Leave a gap low down in walls and hedges on the uphill side, so that cold air keeps flowing past and flows away.
2. Plant frost-sensitive crops higher up the slope, never at the bottom of the valley.
3. Use north slopes for early and late crops. They get the most winter sun.
4. Extend the season with a plastic tunnel; it is cheaper than glass. If you build it against the north wall of the house, it also helps to warm the house. Some farmers keep a few chickens in a fenced corner of it in winter for warmth and manure; give them plenty of fresh air, because the manure gives off ammonia.

## 4. The staple garden (Zone 3)

**Staple crops** are the foods that fill the family's plate most days. Grain, potatoes and other roots give energy. Beans and other **legumes (legume)**, the bean family, give protein.

Staple crops need a lot of space for a moderate harvest, take months to ripen and come in all at once, so they go in Zone 3. They need less work and grow mostly on rain, so choose crops that suit your climate and plant them so that they catch the rain.

| Group | Crops |
|---|---|
| Grain | maize, sorghum, pearl millet (pearl millet); wheat, oats and barley (mainly winter crops) |
| Beans and other legumes | sugar beans and other dry beans, cowpeas (cowpeas), jugo beans, groundnuts, soya, pigeon peas (pigeon peas), mung beans, chickpeas |
| Roots and tubers | potatoes, sweet potatoes, amadumbe, cassava (only frost-free areas) |
| Vegetables that take a lot of space | pumpkins, butternuts, watermelons, sweet melons and gourds |

### Maize

1. In summer-rainfall areas, plant after good rains have wetted the soil and the frost is over, usually October to December.
2. Plant in rows about 90 cm apart. In dry areas, leave about 50 cm between plants in the row, so each has enough water. With good rain, irrigation or rich soil, plant them about 30 cm apart.
3. Choose **open-pollinated** seed if you want to save your own seed. Hybrid seed gives a good harvest the first time, but seed saved from a hybrid crop gives about a third less.

### Sorghum

Sorghum handles heat and drought better than maize. Where water is scarce, plant it in wide rows, about 0.9 m to 2.3 m apart, depending on rain and soil, so each plant has more soil to drink from.

### Dry beans, cowpeas and jugo beans

1. Plant dry beans after the first good rains, once the soil is warmer than about 15 °C: mid-October to December in most summer-rainfall areas, and until about mid-January where there is frost.
2. Plant them beside or between the maize. They feed the soil with nitrogen.
3. In hot, dry places, plant cowpeas and jugo beans, which handle drought well.

### Sweet potatoes

1. Cut pieces of vine 20 to 30 cm long from healthy plants.
2. Plant them on ridges about 1 m apart, 30 cm apart along the ridge.
3. Where there is frost, plant from mid-November to early December. In frost-free areas, plant from August to March.

Plant the orange-fleshed kind. It is rich in vitamin A and good for children.

### Cassava

Cassava grows only in warm, frost-free areas, such as the KwaZulu-Natal coast and the lowveld, and withstands drought well once established. Its roots release cyanide, a poison, so we never eat cassava raw: peel the roots and cook them well, and soak bitter kinds first for a few days in water and throw the water away.

### Principles for the staple garden

1. **Mix crops.** Plant maize together with beans and pumpkins, not one crop alone.
2. **Disturb the soil as little as possible.**
3. **Leave stalks and leaves on the soil as mulch.** It protects the soil and feeds it as it rots.
4. **Plant cover crops** between main crops.
5. **Plant on the contour** so that rain sinks in and the soil stays where it is.
6. **Do crop rotation (crop rotation)** so that no crop comes back year after year in the same place.
7. **Let the soil rest** under a cover crop or grazing after a few seasons.

=== SECTION 04-vegetable-and-staple-crops#8 ===
## Key points

- Know your climate, and let cold air drain away from sensitive crops.
- Make your gardens as big as the time, money and interest you really have.
- Kitchen garden near and dense; vegetable garden on the contour; market garden in beds with one crop each, sold direct.
- Plant staple crops to catch the rain, and never eat cassava raw.
- Mix crops, disturb the soil little, mulch, rotate crops and let the soil rest.

Start with the garden by the kitchen door. When it feeds you every day, you will know when you are ready for the next.

=== SECTION 05-animal-systems#3 ===
## Chickens

Chickens are usually a family's first animals. They are small, cheap and gentle on the soil, and give eggs, meat, feathers, pest control, cleaned soil and some of the richest manure on the farm. They belong in **Zone (zone)** 1 and 2, the parts nearest the house that you visit every day.

### Feeding chickens

Chickens are **omnivores**: they eat plants and animals. A laying hen needs:

- **Protein**, from insects, worms, cooked beans and pigeon-pea seed (pigeon pea)
- **Energy**, from grain such as maize, sorghum and wheat
- **Greens**, from grass, weeds and garden waste
- **Calcium** for strong shells, from crushed oyster shell, limestone grit or dried, crushed eggshells
- **Grit**, small hard stones or coarse sand that she keeps in her crop to grind food fine
- **Vitamins**, especially A from green leaves and yellow maize, and D from sunlight
- **Clean water** all the time

A laying hen eats about 110 to 140 g of feed per day and drinks about twice that weight in water, more in hot weather.

Bought laying mash is balanced, but costs money. Most South African maize and almost all its soya is genetically modified (GM), and bought feed usually contains both, so some permaculture farmers choose to grow their own. Home-grown feed must still be balanced: hens that get only grain lay fewer eggs. If laying drops, add protein or a little bought laying mash.

Free food is everywhere on the farm: kitchen scraps and old bread; snails, slugs and larvae; leaves full of aphids; raked-up leaves full of insects and seeds; the outside of the **compost (compost)** heap, where waste is rotting; cattle dung full of larvae; and sunflower heads hung up ripe so the birds can peck at them.

> **Tip:** Dry eggshells and crush them before you feed them back. Hens that recognise a whole shell can start to eat their own eggs.

Give them a sheltered patch of dry, loose soil, sand or wood ash for a **dust bath**. When they roll in it, their feathers stay clean.

### Housing, water and predators (predator)

Snakes, mongooses, genets, wildcats, dogs, rats and birds of prey all catch chickens. In the countryside a good coop is most of the battle.

1. **Space.** Allow 3 to 4 birds per square metre of floor. Birds that are too crowded fight, get sick and lay less.
2. **Perches.** Chickens sleep high, as in a tree. Give each bird 15 to 20 cm of perch, all at the same height, otherwise they fight over the top one. Put the perches above the nest boxes.
3. **Nest boxes.** About 30 cm wide, deep and high, one for every four or five hens. Old fruit crates work. Line them with dry grass or shavings.
4. **Air.** Let air flow through the coop, without cold draught on the birds at night.
5. **Mesh.** Use strong wire mesh with small holes. Bury it, or fold it outward along the ground, so nothing can dig under it.
6. **Night.** Shut the birds in every night. Keep small chicks in a pen with a roof.
7. **Water.** A cold-drink bottle standing upside down in a shallow dish makes a simple drinker. Keep it in the shade and clean it regularly.

Chickens also scratch out seedlings and **mulch (mulch)**, the dry grass and leaves that cover and protect the soil, so rotate them: a bed after the harvest, then back to their yard until the next bed is ready.

### Which chickens?

**Local breeds** have been kept for generations in Southern Africa. They are hardy, forage well for themselves and are good mothers, but grow slower and lay less than commercial birds. Well-known South African breeds are the **Potchefstroom Koekoek**, **Venda**, **Ovambo**, **Naked Neck** and **Boschveld**, a newer breed developed in Limpopo from Venda, Matabele and Ovambo chickens.

In ARC (ARC) trials with good feed and housing these breeds began laying at about 24 to 26 weeks old. Koekoek hens laid about 196 eggs in a year, Venda about 154, Naked Neck about 139 and Ovambo about 126. A village hen that finds all her own food lays much less, often three clutches of about 10 eggs per year, because she stops laying to hatch and raise her chicks.

**Commercial hybrids**, **layers** for eggs and **broilers** for meat, produce more, but need good feed and care, and are less hardy if they are free-range.

### Keeping chickens healthy

**Newcastle disease** is the big killer of village chickens in Southern Africa. It can wipe out most of an unvaccinated flock within days.

1. **Vaccinate.** Newcastle vaccine for small flocks is sold at co-ops and farm stores. Eye drops protect best; vaccine in the drinking water also works. Follow the instructions on the pack.
2. **Repeat.** Protection wears off, so village flocks are usually revaccinated about every three months.

**Biosecurity (biosecurity)** means the everyday habits that keep disease away from your farm:

- Keep new birds apart from your flock for about a month, and watch them for disease.
- Keep wild birds away from your chickens' feed and water.
- Keep coops clean and dry, and replace nest material regularly.
- Wash your hands after you have handled birds or manure.
- Bury or burn birds that die, where dogs cannot dig them up.

When new birds join, expect a day or two of fighting while the **pecking order** sorts itself out. Add them in the evening, after the flock has gone to sleep, two or more at a time so that no single bird is targeted, with a place where a bullied bird can escape to.

=== SECTION 05-animal-systems#4 ===
## Pigs

Pigs are the great cleaners. Put them on rough, overgrown land and within a week they have turned it over better than any pick or fork. They fit in Zones 2, 3 and 4, and they work well in orchards and in the **food forest (food forest)**, a garden planted in layers like a natural forest.

### Feeding pigs

Pigs eat grass, vines, fallen fruit, nuts and any roots they can dig up. Always give them clean water and shade. Feed them vegetable and garden waste from your own farm, plus grain or bought feed. Never give them meat scraps or kitchen waste from other homes, restaurants or hotels. That food can carry **African swine fever**, which kills pigs and has no cure or vaccine.

### Pig tractors

1. Fence off the plot with a movable electric fence: one or two wires at snout height.
2. Let the pigs first eat most of the plants above the ground. They will not dig as long as there is plenty of food on top.
3. Make sure the soil is moist. Pigs root in soft soil, not in hard, dry soil.
4. After about a week the plot is dug over. Move the fence on.
5. Follow the pigs with a chicken tractor to spread their manure.

In the orchard pigs eat fallen fruit and the fruit fly larvae in it, which helps break the fruit fly's cycle.

> **Tip:** Train young piglets to the electric fence while they are small, in a strong pen with the electric wire on the inside. A pig that has never felt the wire will simply push through.

### Keeping pigs in

A loose pig can destroy young trees and a whole vegetable garden in one night. Fence them in well, and choose carefully where they may go.

Pigs are social, so keep at least two. If you do not want to breed, keep only females or castrated males. Give them a dry shelter without draught for winter and shade for summer.

=== SECTION 05-animal-systems#6 ===
## Bees

Bees are **pollinators (pollinator)**: they carry pollen from flower to flower so that fruit and seed can form. Worldwide about three quarters of the main food crops benefit from pollination by animals. Maize, wheat and sorghum are wind-pollinated, but most of our fruit and vegetables need pollinators.

### Feed the bees

Aim to have something in flower in every season.

- Plant flowering hedges of indigenous trees, trees that grow naturally here, such as karee (*Searsia lancea*) and sweet thorn (soetdoring, *Vachellia karroo*).
- Plant aloes. They flower in winter, when few other things flower.
- Plant lavender, rosemary, borage (borage), rocket, fruit trees and berry bushes.
- Let a patch of wild grass and weeds flower.
- Put out a shallow dish of water with stones in it, so bees can drink without drowning.
- Stop spraying insecticide. If you must spray, use the mildest option, late in the evening when bees are not flying, and never spray on open flowers.

Worldwide honeybees are dying from pests, diseases, hunger and pesticides. South Africa's story is more hopeful. The **varroa mite**, a parasite, arrived here in 1997, and our honeybees became tolerant of it within a few years, so beekeepers here usually do not treat for it. The bigger threat is **American foulbrood (American foulbrood)**, a bacterial disease that kills the young bees. It was first confirmed in 2009 in the Western Cape, and its spores survive for decades in honey, wax and old hive equipment.

### South Africa's honeybees

We have two honeybees: the African or savanna honeybee (*Apis mellifera scutellata*) over most of the country, and the Cape honeybee (*Apis mellifera capensis*) in the Western Cape and surroundings. Both are defensive. Disturb the hive and they sting in large numbers, and can chase a person or animal for hundreds of metres. Learn at a course and from an experienced local beekeeper before you open your first hive.

### Placing hives

Natural beekeepers open a hive only a few times a year, so it can stand at the edge of Zone 2, or in Zone 3 or 4.

1. **Away from people and animals.** Keep hives far away from houses, paths, roads, schools, children, and animals that are tied up or in pens. A hedge or fence in front of the hives makes the bees fly up and go over people's heads.
2. **Level and raised.** Put each hive level on a sturdy stand, so it cannot fall over and the combs do not break. Lift it off the ground against mice and damp. A stack of old tyres works.
3. **Ants.** Put the legs of the hive stand in tins of water or oil, or smear them with grease.
4. **Sun and wind.** Shelter the hive from strong wind and harsh midday sun, so the bees use less energy cooling and warming it. Let the entrance face east, towards the morning sun.
5. **Security.** Honey attracts thieves and animals. Fence off a group of hives, or lock a single hive with a chain.

### Natural beekeeping

Natural beekeeping disturbs the colony as little as possible. Treat the colony as one living whole. Open it rarely, so the nest keeps its scent and warmth. Let the bees build their own combs and multiply by their own swarming. Keep local bees, and only as many hives as the local flowers can feed. Put nothing in the hive that does not come from bees, take only the honey they can spare, and let what the bees do guide you.

Leave them enough honey and pollen for winter and dry times, rather than feeding sugar water. Never feed bees honey, and never use second-hand hives, frames or wax unless you know where they come from: both can carry foulbrood spores.

### Stings

Many stings at once, or one sting in a person who is allergic, can kill.

1. Wear a veil, a bee suit or long sleeves and long trousers, and gloves.
2. Work calmly, with a smoker. Do not open hives in cold, wet or windy weather, or at night.
3. If someone is stung, walk away from the hive. Scrape the sting out sideways with a fingernail or knife blade; do not squeeze it out. Wash the place and cool it.
4. If the face, lips, tongue or throat swell, if breathing or swallowing is difficult, or if the person is dizzy, faints or is covered with a spreading rash, call an ambulance at once: 10177, or 112 from a cellphone. Do the same for anyone who has been stung many times.

Anyone who has previously had a bad reaction to a sting must not work with bees.

## Key points

- Look at each animal's needs, products and behaviour, and link them to the rest of the farm.
- Animal tractors move animals over the land so that their manure, scratching and digging do useful work.
- Chickens need balanced feed, grit, calcium, clean water and a coop against predators, and every few months a Newcastle vaccine.
- Pigs clean land and ducks clean up snails, but both must be well behind a fence.

Start with one animal and one job. The second job will show itself, and then the third.

=== SECTION 06-tree-systems#0 ===
# Tree systems

Trees are work for the long term. A vegetable bed feeds you for one season. A tree that is planted well feeds your children and their children, with fruit, firewood, **fodder** (fodder), that is food for animals, shade and medicine, and you never have to dig or sow it again.

Trees also do quiet work. Their roots hold the soil and reach water that vegetables cannot reach. Their leaves fall and feed the soil. Their branches break the wind and give shelter to the birds that eat your pests. A forest is built on a forest: each plant makes the soil better for the next one.

Three tree systems do most of this work on a smallholding: the **food forest** (food forest), a garden planted in layers like a natural forest; the **windbreak** (windbreak), a strip of trees and shrubs that slows the wind; and the single tree, planted well. Every plant recommended here is **indigenous** (indigenous), that is, it grows naturally in Southern Africa, or it is not a declared invader in South Africa. Many trees praised in overseas books on permaculture (permaculture) are **invasive species** (invasive species) here: plants from elsewhere that spread out of control. Buy by the Latin name.

**By the end of this chapter you will be able to:**

- Choose a place for a food forest and choose fruit trees that suit your frost and winter cold
- Plant a food forest in the right order, and start with nitrogen-fixing pioneers
- Plan a windbreak across your harmful wind, with indigenous trees and shrubs
- Plant a single tree so that it roots deep and survives its first dry season

=== SECTION 06-tree-systems#1 ===
## Food forests

Vegetables and staple crops ask a lot of us: digging, sowing and watering, season after season. Because the soil is dug over and replanted every year, it never grows past the first stage of **succession** (succession): the natural step-by-step change from bare soil to grass, shrubs and trees. Annual (annual) crops also suffer a lot from pests and dry times.

A food forest is a garden planted like a natural forest, in layers, from tall trees down to low plants and roots. It is not an orchard of trees standing in bare soil. Most of its plants are **perennial plants** (perennials), that is plants that live longer than two years. Once a food forest is established:

- it does not need to be replanted every season
- it grows deeper roots that reach water and nutrients that shallow vegetables cannot reach
- it keeps the soil covered all year
- it feeds itself with its own fallen leaves and prunings
- it gives a home to birds and insects that keep pests in check

From one food forest you can harvest fruit, nuts, berries, herbs, spices, firewood, poles, fodder, root crops and **mulch** (mulch). Mulch is material spread over the soil to keep it moist and protected. The work that remains is seasonal pruning, **chop and drop** (chop and drop), that is cutting plants down and leaving them on the soil as mulch, propagating new plants, and now and then moving an **animal tractor** (animal tractor), a movable chicken coop, through it. Young trees still need water through their first one or two dry seasons, and in dry areas most fruit trees need a little water every year.

People have farmed this way for a very long time. In the eastern Amazon people have tended the forest for food plants for at least 4 500 years, and the forest there is still full of the edible trees they favoured.

### Where to plant a food forest

In large parts of South Africa forest is not the natural end point of succession. Grassland, fynbos, Karoo shrubland and savanna are old, complete systems in their own right, not forest still to come. Trees planted in healthy grassland or fynbos crowd out rare plants and use more water.

1. Plant on land that is **already disturbed**: old fields, bare yards, eroded land, land around the homestead, or land cleared of invasive plants.
2. Leave healthy veld, grassland, fynbos and wetland as they are.
3. Keep trees away from streams and wetlands, unless they are indigenous riverbank trees.

### Two kinds of food forest

Permaculture plans a farm in **zones** (zones), according to how often you visit each part.

**Homestead food forest (Zone 2).** Near the house, where you walk past once or twice a day. It feeds the household: fruit, nuts, berries, cooking and medicinal herbs, and annual plants that self-seed. It contains many species. Where space is short, plant only a few big trees.

**Larger food forest or orchard (Zone 3).** Further away, with bigger trees, to sell. A **permaculture orchard** is a simpler food forest with fewer species, planted on a larger scale and often irrigated. It still has **nitrogen fixers** (nitrogen fixers), plants that add nitrogen from the air to the soil, and flowers for insects. Plant it in strips with grazing in between, which also makes harvesting easier.

### Coppicing

**Coppicing** (coppicing) means cutting a tree off near the ground so that it pushes out many new shoots. You cut the shoots off again every few years for firewood, fence posts, pegs and handles for tools, and the tree lives on. The shoots grow back much faster than a new tree from seed, because the roots are already big. Not every tree resprouts. Wild olive (*Olea europaea* subsp. *cuspidata*) is an indigenous tree that resprouts well after coppicing, and a coppice patch of it in Zone 2 gives firewood year after year.
