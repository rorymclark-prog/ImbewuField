# ImbewuField — expanded field programme

English authoring edition · 7 September 2026

This is the written practical companion to the existing studies. It contains 36 core weekly cycles and four elective pathways, with three activities per pathway. These are learning cycles, not 36 additional app lessons. All 33 existing lessons retain their IDs and have linked practical activities.

The scope comes from Rory’s **ImbewuField Training Audit and 36 Week Plan**, recovered from the July 2026 document. The new activities below were authored for this continuation. Facilitator and language review remain outstanding; this document does not certify completed bilingual media or change graduation requirements.

Use the week numbers as a learning sequence. The facilitator adapts actual field work to local weather, water, crop stage and resources. Keep evidence on paper or in the field journal, and use the existing assignment submission when requested. No photograph, survey response, planting or harvest should be claimed before it exists.

## Core programme

### Week 1 — Start with your household and your phone

**Read alongside:** The Three Ethics: Earth Care, People Care, Fair Share.

**Why this matters.** Your starting point gives the course a purpose. A useful baseline describes what you have today, including the work you already know how to do. You do not need to make the farm look better for a photograph.

**Look closely.** Compare a note saying “the garden is poor” with a dated note naming the bed, what is growing and the problem you can actually see.

**Try it in the field**

1. Name one change your household wants from the course and one strength you bring.
2. Practise opening a lesson and making a dated note. Use paper or a voice note if writing on a phone is difficult.
3. Choose what you are comfortable sharing. Ask before including another person in a photograph.

**Keep a record.** Keep your starting note, chosen goal and a picture or sketch of one growing area. Keep personal household details private.

**Think it through.** What could you show at the end of the course that would tell you something has improved?

**Facilitator check.** The learner can find the lesson, describe their starting point and explain what they consent to share.

**If the timing is wrong.** Practise with paper and a borrowed demonstration phone; lack of a personal phone does not prevent the baseline activity.


### Week 2 — Turn household goals into a farm brief

**Read alongside:** The Three Ethics: Earth Care, People Care, Fair Share; Record-Keeping: Knowing What Your Farm Is Actually Producing.

**Why this matters.** A farm plan makes choices about food, money, water and time. A brief keeps those choices connected to the people who will live with them.

**Look closely.** A household may want more food and more sales, while the same person does most of the watering. Show where those aims compete for labour.

**Try it in the field**

1. Discuss food needs, available work time and the jobs people can realistically share.
2. List resources you have, resources you can borrow with permission and resources still missing.
3. Write a short brief: what you want, who will do the work and the limits the first plan must respect.

**Keep a record.** Keep the brief and mark which statements are agreed facts and which still need checking.

**Think it through.** Which attractive idea would put too much pressure on the household this season?

**Facilitator check.** The proposed work fits the learner’s stated resources and includes the household’s own priorities.

**If the timing is wrong.** Write your own provisional brief and leave shared commitments undecided until the people concerned can discuss them.


### Week 3 — Use ethics and principles to make a decision

**Read alongside:** The Three Ethics: Earth Care, People Care, Fair Share; Twelve Principles: Designing with Nature.

**Why this matters.** Knowing the names of the ethics is only a beginning. Their value appears when a decision helps one part of the farm but creates a cost somewhere else.

**Look closely.** Use the lesson’s surplus example: food can support household meals, sales, seed saving and returning material to the soil. Each destination serves a purpose.

**Try it in the field**

1. Choose a real decision you face, such as where a harvest or a limited supply of mulch will go.
2. Describe who and what benefits from each choice, and who carries the cost.
3. Choose an action and explain it using the three ethics and a relevant design principle.

**Keep a record.** Write the decision, the alternatives you considered and the reason for your choice.

**Think it through.** What new observation would make you change your mind?

**Facilitator check.** The learner applies an ethic to a concrete trade-off instead of only reciting its name.

**If the timing is wrong.** Use a situation from the lesson, clearly labelled as an example, then revisit the exercise with a real decision.


### Week 4 — Walk, observe and keep a useful journal

**Read alongside:** Understanding Water Flow: Where Rain Goes on Your Land; Making a Simple Site Map: Your Design Starts on Paper.

**Why this matters.** A journal becomes useful when you can return to the same place and compare what changed. “I saw this” and “I think this caused it” belong in different parts of the note.

**Look closely.** Compare a close picture of a damaged leaf with a wider picture that shows where the plant sits in the bed. Together they tell a fuller story.

**Try it in the field**

1. Choose a safe walking route and name the places you will revisit.
2. At each place record what you can see: crop condition, ground cover, water marks or recent disturbance.
3. Add a separate question or possible explanation, then choose something to check on the next visit.

**Keep a record.** A dated observation with a location, sketch or photo, your question and the next visit’s purpose.

**Think it through.** Which part of your note is evidence, and which part is still a guess?

**Facilitator check.** Another person can locate the observation and distinguish a finding from an explanation.

**If the timing is wrong.** Observe an accessible garden edge or container. Do not enter flooded, unstable or otherwise unsafe ground.

Supporting reading: [FAO: field observation and decision making](https://www.fao.org/4/x7925m/X7925M07.htm).

### Week 5 — Draw the land you actually have

**Read alongside:** Making a Simple Site Map: Your Design Starts on Paper.

**Why this matters.** A base map records the site before you add a design. If the starting features are wrong, later choices about beds, access and water can be wrong too.

**Look closely.** Look at the same site as a photograph and a simple plan. Find the house, paths, existing trees, water points and places you cannot use.

**Try it in the field**

1. Walk the accessible boundary with the person who knows the site and note any uncertainty about access or permission.
2. Draw existing features on paper or in the mapping tool. Mark orientation and label measurements as measured or estimated.
3. Ask someone familiar with the site to follow your map and point out what is missing.

**Keep a record.** Keep the first base map and a list of corrections. Date later versions so the original observation is not lost.

**Think it through.** Which missing feature would cause the biggest mistake in your planting plan?

**Facilitator check.** The map is recognisable, distinguishes existing features from proposals and identifies uncertain boundaries.

**If the timing is wrong.** Map only the accessible area and mark the rest as unverified. A learning map does not establish legal boundaries.


### Week 6 — Map sun, wind, slope and movement

**Read alongside:** Sun Angles, Shade, and Aspect: Getting the Most from Sunlight; Wind, Frost, and Topography: Reading the Invisible Forces; Zones and Sectors: Organising Your Farm by Energy.

**Why this matters.** The same crop or structure can behave differently in different places. Sectors describe influences crossing the site; they help you ask where a proposal will be exposed or sheltered.

**Look closely.** Use one fixed viewpoint to compare shade at different times. Add observed wind exposure and the paths people and animals already use on a separate map layer.

**Try it in the field**

1. Mark the shadows you observe and note the time and date.
2. Ask about local frost, strong wind and fire experience, recording who provided the account rather than treating memory as a measurement.
3. Add those observations to the base map and identify a place that needs more observation before a design decision.

**Keep a record.** A sector overlay with dated observations and clearly labelled local accounts.

**Think it through.** What can today’s visit tell you, and what still needs another season?

**Facilitator check.** The learner can connect a proposed location to site evidence and name the limits of that evidence.

**If the timing is wrong.** Use existing observations and mark seasonal gaps. Do not stage a fire or enter hazardous weather to complete the activity.


### Week 7 — Audit water before placing the garden

**Read alongside:** Swales and Berms: Slowing Water on the Slope; Farm Dams and Ponds: Storing Water for the Dry Season; Rainwater Tanks and Roof Catchment: Harvesting Clean Water; Greywater Recycling: A Free Daily Water Source.

**Why this matters.** A water point on a map does not tell you whether it will serve the garden when needed. Source, reliability, access and intended use all matter.

**Look closely.** Trace the route from source to storage to use. Include where overflow or used water goes. Keep observations about quantity separate from evidence about water quality.

**Try it in the field**

1. List each existing water source, who may use it and when it has failed or run short.
2. Walk the visible supply route safely and mark leaks, difficult carrying routes and unknown connections.
3. Place a provisional growing area on the map and explain how it would receive water. List any permissions or technical checks still needed.

**Keep a record.** A water map and an unanswered-questions list for the owner, facilitator or relevant local specialist.

**Think it through.** What happens to the proposed garden when its usual source is unavailable?

**Facilitator check.** The learner can trace the water route and does not treat clear-looking water as proof of safety.

**If the timing is wrong.** Inspect visible components and use a paper route. Do not enter tanks, dams or excavations, or build waterworks for this mapping exercise.


### Week 8 — Make a first whole-farm concept

**Read alongside:** Zones and Sectors: Organising Your Farm by Energy; Making a Simple Site Map: Your Design Starts on Paper; Establishing a Food Forest: Planting Sequence and Timeline.

**Why this matters.** A first concept connects household needs with the site you observed. It should make daily work easier and leave room to learn before committing to expensive changes.

**Look closely.** Compare a layout that puts frequently visited work near convenient access with one that makes the same person walk repeatedly across the property.

**Try it in the field**

1. Place proposed activities on a copy of the base map, using the lesson’s zone guidance and the visits each activity needs.
2. Trace carrying routes, daily work and access for maintenance. Check them against water and sector observations.
3. Choose what can start now, what comes later and what must wait for advice or permission.

**Keep a record.** Your first concept, the household brief and a short explanation of the first phase.

**Think it through.** Which connection between activities saves work, and which might create a conflict?

**Facilitator check.** Review point 1: the concept responds to the brief, water, access and known site constraints.

**If the timing is wrong.** Keep uncertain areas as alternatives on paper. A concept is a proposal, not authority to carry out construction.


### Week 9 — Compare soils in the field

**Read alongside:** Understanding Your Soil: The Foundation of Everything.

**Why this matters.** Soils can differ within one garden. A comparison helps you decide what to investigate instead of assuming every bed needs the same treatment.

**Look closely.** Compare samples and ground surfaces from named places. Observe texture by the lesson’s method, visible structure, roots, cover and signs of water movement.

**Try it in the field**

1. Choose contrasting accessible places and describe their recent use and conditions.
2. Use the same observation method at each place so the comparison is fair. Record the conditions as well as the result.
3. Identify a question that needs a repeat observation, a laboratory result or help from a facilitator.

**Keep a record.** A comparison sheet with locations, method, conditions, observations and remaining questions.

**Think it through.** Could moisture, recent cultivation or a different method explain part of the difference?

**Facilitator check.** The learner describes evidence about soil without claiming a visual observation is a nutrient or pH test.

**If the timing is wrong.** Use clean, accessible soil samples and photographs of their locations. Leave unsafe or contaminated areas alone.


### Week 10 — Build a compost and soil-cover routine

**Read alongside:** Making and Using Compost; Mulching and Cover Crops: Protecting and Building Soil; Mulch Plants and Pest Management.

**Why this matters.** Composting and cover are ongoing jobs. A plan works when suitable materials are available and someone knows what to check after the first day.

**Look closely.** Use the compost and mulch readings to identify materials and actions. Compare a covered patch with a nearby exposed patch without treating one visit as proof of a long-term result.

**Try it in the field**

1. List suitable materials actually available and check uncertain materials with the facilitator.
2. Prepare a small demonstration using the authored lesson method and record the starting condition.
3. Agree who will revisit it, what they will observe and how changes will be recorded.

**Keep a record.** Materials used, the location, a starting photo or sketch and a follow-up observation.

**Think it through.** What would you change if materials become scarce or the first attempt does not behave as expected?

**Facilitator check.** The learner can explain the chosen materials and show a maintenance observation, not just an opening photograph.

**If the timing is wrong.** Make a materials and maintenance plan now, then return for the practical work when suitable materials are available.


### Week 11 — Ask better soil-fertility questions

**Read alongside:** Understanding Your Soil: The Foundation of Everything; Nitrogen Fixers: Plants That Feed the Soil.

**Why this matters.** A weak or discoloured plant is a clue, not a complete diagnosis. Soil conditions, water and crop history help you decide what evidence to collect next.

**Look closely.** Compare affected and less-affected plants of the same crop where possible. Look at where the pattern occurs rather than naming a deficiency from one leaf.

**Try it in the field**

1. Record the pattern, crop stage, watering and recent inputs without guessing missing dates.
2. Ask a local laboratory or extension adviser what sample and background information they need for the question you have.
3. Prepare a labelled sampling plan using those instructions. Discuss a result with the facilitator before choosing an amendment.

**Keep a record.** A crop-history note, a sampling question and any actual result, clearly separated from assumptions.

**Think it through.** What else could explain the symptom, and what observation would help distinguish the possibilities?

**Facilitator check.** The learner asks an answerable question and does not select a treatment solely from leaf colour.

**If the timing is wrong.** Keep the question and observation record; do not fabricate a laboratory result or substitute an unverified application rate.


### Week 12 — Check irrigation and keep it working

**Read alongside:** Rainwater Tanks and Roof Catchment: Harvesting Clean Water; Greywater Recycling: A Free Daily Water Source; Mulching and Cover Crops: Protecting and Building Soil.

**Why this matters.** An irrigation plan must be checked on the ground. A blocked outlet or uneven delivery can make the plan on the phone different from what the crop receives.

**Look closely.** Trace an existing system during normal safe operation with its operator. Compare places receiving water and note visible faults or differences.

**Try it in the field**

1. Identify the source, controls, outlets and the person responsible for maintenance.
2. Record a normal watering event, weather, soil observations and visible delivery problems using the operator’s safe procedure.
3. Review the observations with the facilitator and agree a maintenance task and a follow-up check.

**Keep a record.** A dated watering log, fault list and the result after a permitted repair or adjustment.

**Think it through.** Which decision needs a crop-specific recommendation before you change the schedule?

**Facilitator check.** Review point 2: the learner can explain the supply route, detect a problem and name an appropriate next action.

**If the timing is wrong.** Use a labelled system sketch and maintenance discussion. Do not work on pressurised or electrical equipment without competent help.


### Week 13 — Plan food for home and a realistic surplus

**Read alongside:** Staple Crops: Maize, Beans, and Root Vegetables; Selling Surplus: Where to Sell and How to Price.

**Why this matters.** A production target should start with what the household will use and what buyers actually want. A large harvest is not automatically a useful harvest.

**Look closely.** Lay out a household food-use calendar beside a buyer’s requested product, quantity and collection time. Notice where supply and demand may fail to meet.

**Try it in the field**

1. Record foods the household grows, buys and finds difficult to obtain, without requiring anyone to disclose private health information.
2. Ask a willing potential buyer about product, quality, unit and timing; distinguish an enquiry from an order.
3. Choose a provisional crop purpose: household use, a confirmed need, or a small market trial.

**Keep a record.** A household-use calendar and a market enquiry with its date, unit and unanswered questions.

**Think it through.** What will you do with a surplus if the hoped-for buyer does not buy it?

**Facilitator check.** The learner separates household needs, observed demand and hoped-for sales.

**If the timing is wrong.** Use your own food-use record and mark market assumptions as unconfirmed until a buyer can be consulted.


### Week 14 — Turn bed measurements into a planting plan

**Read alongside:** Preparing and Planting Your Beds.

**Why this matters.** The whole site is not plantable area. Paths, access and unused corners must be accounted for before a planting plan can describe what fits.

**Look closely.** Mark the edges of an existing bed and its paths. Compare the gross area with the space actually available for planting.

**Try it in the field**

1. Measure the usable bed and record the units and any uncertain measurements.
2. Use the crop spacing from the authored lesson or a checked local recommendation, recording its source.
3. Mark rows and positions on paper first, then count the places that fit with access. Compare the result with the app’s plan.

**Keep a record.** A measured bed sketch, spacing source and counted planting positions.

**Think it through.** Where would treating a path as growing space overstate your planned production?

**Facilitator check.** The learner can explain their units, usable area and plant count without inventing a yield.

**If the timing is wrong.** Use a measured demonstration bed or a labelled paper example. Keep it separate from the learner’s unmeasured site.


### Week 15 — Choose crops for the local season

**Read alongside:** Staple Crops: Maize, Beans, and Root Vegetables; Species Selection for South African Food Forests.

**Why this matters.** A planting date copied from another place may not fit your site. Crop choice needs local season information, water availability and a clear purpose.

**Look closely.** Compare the site observations with the crop and climate guidance in the readings. Separate an annual crop’s planting window from a tree’s longer-term suitability.

**Try it in the field**

1. List candidate crops already covered by the course and the purpose each would serve.
2. Check local planting guidance with a reliable local source and record where it came from.
3. Choose a first crop and an alternative, explaining the water, season, space and household or market reasons.

**Keep a record.** A crop-choice note with local evidence, source dates and any conditions still unverified.

**Think it through.** What change in water availability or planting conditions would make the alternative more suitable?

**Facilitator check.** The learner can justify the choice for their location and avoids treating a national calendar as a site guarantee.

**If the timing is wrong.** Keep a shortlist and arrange a local check before committing seed, money or a planting date.


### Week 16 — Plan crops through space and time

**Read alongside:** Succession Planting and Intercropping; Building a Plant Guild: A Practical Example.

**Why this matters.** A bed has a history and a next use. Succession, rotation and mixed planting need enough space and a workable sequence, not just a list of crops.

**Look closely.** Draw the same bed at successive stages: what is there now, the next planting and what follows harvest. Mark any period when the bed is still occupied.

**Try it in the field**

1. Record the bed’s known crop history and label gaps in that history.
2. Place proposed sowings and harvest windows using the chosen crop guidance, marking estimates as estimates.
3. Check water, access and labour where tasks overlap. Revise the plan if two crops need the same space at the same time.

**Keep a record.** A bed sequence with sources for timing, expected overlaps and a contingency if the first crop remains longer.

**Think it through.** How would a delayed harvest affect the next planting?

**Facilitator check.** Review point 3: the learner explains a feasible crop sequence connected to measured space and local conditions.

**If the timing is wrong.** Plan a trial bed on paper and wait for the appropriate planting window before field implementation.


### Week 17 — Track seed and nursery batches

**Read alongside:** Why Seed Saving Matters; Drying, Storing, and Sharing Seed; Preparing and Planting Your Beds.

**Why this matters.** A nursery label connects a seed source with what happens next. Without it, a successful batch cannot easily be repeated and a failed batch is difficult to investigate.

**Look closely.** Compare an unlabelled tray with one carrying the crop, variety where known, seed source and sowing date. The second tray has a history you can follow.

**Try it in the field**

1. Choose a suitable course crop and check its propagation method with the facilitator.
2. Create a batch label and sow using the checked method, recording seed quantity and starting conditions.
3. Keep observations of emergence and care. Count what happened instead of describing the whole batch as simply good or bad.

**Keep a record.** A batch record linking source, sowing, observations and the number of usable seedlings when assessed.

**Think it through.** Which missing record would make it hardest to understand poor emergence?

**Facilitator check.** The learner can trace the batch and distinguish seed sown, seedlings emerged and seedlings suitable for the next step.

**If the timing is wrong.** Prepare labels and a batch sheet, then observe an existing nursery with permission until sowing is appropriate.


### Week 18 — Prepare, sow and establish a bed

**Read alongside:** Preparing and Planting Your Beds; Making and Using Compost.

**Why this matters.** A planting plan becomes useful when the actual bed matches it. Recording what was planted gives later scouting and harvest records a reliable starting point.

**Look closely.** Place the measured plan beside the bed. Identify the crop, positions, preparation and initial care described in the authored lesson.

**Try it in the field**

1. Confirm the planting window, available water, seed or seedlings and the agreed method before starting.
2. Prepare and plant the bed following the lesson and locally checked crop guidance.
3. Count and record the actual planting, including any difference from the plan. Agree who will check establishment.

**Keep a record.** Planting date, bed, crop and batch, quantity planted, method and a dated starting picture or sketch.

**Think it through.** Which difference between the plan and actual planting should change the next tasks?

**Facilitator check.** The learner demonstrates the method and can connect planted material to its bed and batch record.

**If the timing is wrong.** Practise layout and tool handling in a demonstration area; record planting as pending rather than claiming a crop is in the ground.


### Week 19 — Make a task calendar someone can follow

**Read alongside:** Succession Planting and Intercropping; Record-Keeping: Knowing What Your Farm Is Actually Producing.

**Why this matters.** A task is useful when somebody knows where to go, what to do and what would count as done. A long list without responsibility can leave essential care undone.

**Look closely.** Compare “look after vegetables” with a task naming the bed, the check, the responsible person and the observation to bring back.

**Try it in the field**

1. List upcoming work from the actual planted bed and the agreed crop plan.
2. Give each task a location, purpose, responsible person and suitable timing or trigger.
3. Walk through the calendar with the people doing the work and adjust clashes with household responsibilities.

**Keep a record.** A practical calendar and one completed task note stating what actually happened.

**Think it through.** Which task must change when rain, labour or crop stage changes?

**Facilitator check.** Another person can understand a task and report its result without guessing the intended action.

**If the timing is wrong.** Create a calendar for a demonstration bed and mark it as practice. Do not create fictional completion records.


### Week 20 — Review establishment and correct the plan

**Read alongside:** Preparing and Planting Your Beds; Understanding Your Soil: The Foundation of Everything.

**Why this matters.** The planting date is not the end of establishment. Missing plants and uneven growth change what the bed can do, and they deserve an explanation before you spend more seed.

**Look closely.** Compare the original planting sketch with the bed now. Mark gaps and differences without hiding the places that did poorly.

**Try it in the field**

1. Revisit the same bed and record established plants, gaps and visible patterns.
2. Review watering, weather, handling and batch records with the facilitator to identify what needs checking.
3. Agree a suitable correction or further observation, then update tasks and planned quantities to match the field.

**Keep a record.** A dated establishment map, possible causes, the agreed action and a follow-up observation.

**Think it through.** What evidence would show that your correction helped?

**Facilitator check.** Review point 4: the learner compares plan with actual establishment and can justify the next step.

**If the timing is wrong.** Use a previous real bed record or a supervised example; return to the learner’s crop when the stage is appropriate.


### Week 21 — Scout the crop and choose a next step

**Read alongside:** Pest and Disease Management Without Chemicals.

**Why this matters.** Regular scouting connects a crop observation to a decision. Looking only at the worst plant can miss the pattern across a bed.

**Look closely.** Compare crop condition, soil moisture observations, weeds, visible insects and damage across a repeatable route. Record helpful organisms as well as suspected pests.

**Try it in the field**

1. Choose a repeatable observation route with the facilitator and identify the crop stage.
2. Record the pattern and extent of what you see, including places without the problem.
3. Discuss the findings and choose a next observation, a checked action or an escalation to someone qualified to advise.

**Keep a record.** A dated scouting sheet with bed, observations, decision, responsible person and follow-up.

**Think it through.** What changed since the previous visit, and how did that change your decision?

**Facilitator check.** The learner bases the next step on recorded field observations and returns to check the outcome.

**If the timing is wrong.** Practise the observation method in an accessible demonstration plot; do not invent pest counts for an absent crop.

Supporting reading: [FAO: field observation and decision making](https://www.fao.org/4/x7925m/X7925M07.htm).

### Week 22 — Use crop photos and Lima Vision carefully

**Read alongside:** Pest and Disease Management Without Chemicals; Making a Simple Site Map: Your Design Starts on Paper.

**Why this matters.** A useful crop photograph gives context. An image-based suggestion still needs checking against the crop history and what is happening around the plant.

**Look closely.** Take a wider view, a whole-plant view and a clear detail of the symptom. Compare what can and cannot be inferred from each.

**Try it in the field**

1. Photograph the crop with permission and avoid including people or private information unnecessarily.
2. Add bed, crop, date, affected part and relevant recent care. If using Lima Vision, keep its suggestion separate from your own observation.
3. Discuss uncertainty with the facilitator before any treatment decision. Retain the original evidence for follow-up.

**Keep a record.** A photo set, context note, any tool suggestion and the person or source used to check it.

**Think it through.** What important fact could the photograph fail to show?

**Facilitator check.** The learner explains the limits of an image suggestion and can identify the next verification step.

**If the timing is wrong.** Use the same photo-and-context method with a facilitator. The learning activity does not require an AI service or a paid request.


### Week 23 — Make an integrated pest-management decision

**Read alongside:** Pest and Disease Management Without Chemicals; Mulch Plants and Pest Management.

**Why this matters.** Pest management begins with understanding the crop situation. A decision should consider the problem, helpful organisms and what happened after earlier action.

**Look closely.** Use the scouting record to compare the affected crop with nearby less-affected plants. Discuss prevention and the options already taught in the course.

**Try it in the field**

1. Describe the problem using observed evidence and list what remains unidentified.
2. With the facilitator, compare suitable options from checked guidance and select an action or a reason to continue observing.
3. Set a follow-up observation and record the result, including any unwanted effects.

**Keep a record.** The problem, evidence, checked advice, chosen action and outcome in the same crop record.

**Think it through.** What result would make you stop, change the approach or seek specialist help?

**Facilitator check.** The learner explains the choice from evidence rather than treating every insect as a reason to spray.

**If the timing is wrong.** Work through an actual past scouting record. Do not apply a product merely to demonstrate activity.

Supporting reading: [FAO: field observation and decision making](https://www.fao.org/4/x7925m/X7925M07.htm).

### Week 24 — Know when crop protection needs more help

**Read alongside:** Pest and Disease Management Without Chemicals; Understanding Your Soil: The Foundation of Everything.

**Why this matters.** Similar symptoms can have different causes. The useful skill is to recognise the limits of your evidence and give an adviser the information needed to help.

**Look closely.** Put the crop history, symptom photos and scouting map together. Ask which possible causes the evidence supports and which remain untested.

**Try it in the field**

1. Prepare a clear problem report with crop, location, timing, pattern and recent care.
2. Ask an extension adviser or other qualified local specialist what further information is needed.
3. Record the advice and its source. Before any crop-protection product is used, have a qualified facilitator check the current label and applicable local requirements.

**Keep a record.** A complete problem report and advice record, including unresolved questions and the follow-up plan.

**Think it through.** What could go wrong if you confidently named a disease from a single picture?

**Facilitator check.** Review point 5: the learner recognises uncertainty, knows where to seek help and does not invent a treatment or dose.

**If the timing is wrong.** Practise preparing the report. Treatment stays pending until identification and safe, locally appropriate advice are available.


### Week 25 — Record a harvest and account for losses

**Read alongside:** Record-Keeping: Knowing What Your Farm Is Actually Producing; Staple Crops: Maize, Beans, and Root Vegetables.

**Why this matters.** Harvest records should describe what left the bed and where it went. Household use and damaged produce matter even when no money changes hands.

**Look closely.** Follow one real harvest from bed to sorting and destination. Use the same clearly named unit throughout or record an actual conversion.

**Try it in the field**

1. Check readiness for the chosen crop using the lesson and locally appropriate guidance.
2. Harvest with the agreed method and record the quantity, unit, date and bed.
3. Account for household use, sale, sharing, retained produce and losses without counting the same produce twice.

**Keep a record.** A harvest record whose destinations reconcile with the measured total, plus a note on any loss.

**Think it through.** At which point did produce lose quality or become difficult to account for?

**Facilitator check.** The learner can trace the harvest to a bed and explain where the recorded quantity went.

**If the timing is wrong.** Use a real harvest from a demonstration plot with permission, or defer the field assessment until a crop is ready. Do not force a harvest to fit the week number.


### Week 26 — Handle food carefully and keep its history

**Read alongside:** Record-Keeping: Knowing What Your Farm Is Actually Producing; Selling Surplus: Where to Sell and How to Price.

**Why this matters.** The work after picking affects the food people receive. A batch record also helps you trace produce back to its harvest if a problem is reported.

**Look closely.** Walk the route from harvest container to sorting place, storage and handover. Identify where food contacts hands, tools, surfaces and water.

**Try it in the field**

1. Use WHO’s food-hygiene guidance to discuss cleanliness, separation of raw and cooked food, thorough cooking, safe temperatures, and safe water and ingredients.
2. Check the handling method for the actual crop with the facilitator and identify a practical improvement.
3. Label the harvest batch and record its origin, handling and destination.

**Keep a record.** A batch trail and a before-and-after note on the handling improvement.

**Think it through.** Could you identify the source and recipients of this batch if someone reported a problem?

**Facilitator check.** The learner can trace a real batch and explain the hygiene practices relevant to its handling.

**If the timing is wrong.** Practise the batch trail on paper and inspect the handling area; do not use doubtful food for a tasting demonstration.

Supporting reading: [WHO: Five keys to safer food](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241594639).

### Week 27 — Use the harvest well at home

**Read alongside:** Staple Crops: Maize, Beans, and Root Vegetables; Selling Surplus: Where to Sell and How to Price.

**Why this matters.** Farm success includes food used by the household. A plan for meals, sharing and sales can make the harvest more useful than treating everything as market stock.

**Look closely.** Compare the household’s earlier food-use calendar with what is actually being harvested. Discuss gaps and preferences without assuming every household eats the same meals.

**Try it in the field**

1. Choose a current harvest and discuss how the household would like to use it.
2. Record what is kept, shared or sold and connect those choices to the original household brief.
3. If considering processing for sale, list the handling skills, equipment, costs and local requirements that must be checked before starting.

**Keep a record.** A household-use note and a realistic next action for a gap or surplus.

**Think it through.** Did the crop improve food access at home, and what evidence supports that answer?

**Facilitator check.** The learner includes household use in the farm record and separates a processing idea from a verified production method.

**If the timing is wrong.** Use an actual earlier harvest or plan for the next one. This activity does not require a new recipe or preservation trial.


### Week 28 — Save seed with a traceable method

**Read alongside:** Why Seed Saving Matters; How to Save Seed: Dry and Wet Methods; Drying, Storing, and Sharing Seed.

**Why this matters.** Saving seed is a chain of decisions from choosing a plant to labelling and storing the seed. A record makes the next sowing and any sharing more informed.

**Look closely.** Return to the Seeds module’s dry and wet demonstrations. Explain why the chosen crop needs its particular method rather than using one method for all seed.

**Try it in the field**

1. Choose a suitable seed-saving opportunity using the reviewed Seeds lessons and identify the parent plant or source.
2. Follow the appropriate saving, drying and storage method when the crop is ready.
3. Label the batch and record what you know and what remains uncertain before storing or sharing it.

**Keep a record.** A seed-batch history from source through method to storage, plus the checks taught in the Seeds module.

**Think it through.** What information would you want if a neighbour gave you this seed?

**Facilitator check.** Review point 6: the learner demonstrates the appropriate method and can trace the batch. A label alone is not proof of seed quality.

**If the timing is wrong.** Prepare the batch record and practise identification with the facilitator. Complete collection when suitable seed is mature, not on an arbitrary calendar date.


### Week 29 — Keep farm and household money understandable

**Read alongside:** Record-Keeping: Knowing What Your Farm Is Actually Producing.

**Why this matters.** A money record should let you explain what happened. Household spending, a farm input and a long-lived tool are different events even if they were paid for from the same pocket.

**Look closely.** Review actual receipts or a clearly labelled practice set. Identify the date, purpose, amount and the farm activity, if any, that the payment belongs to.

**Try it in the field**

1. Gather available records and enter actual income and spending with a clear description.
2. Separate farm transactions, household use and equipment purchases using the existing record system.
3. Reconcile the entries with receipts and cash movements; mark missing evidence instead of making it up.

**Keep a record.** A dated money record and a list of entries that still need clarification.

**Think it through.** Which transaction would make the crop look more profitable if it were left out?

**Facilitator check.** The learner can explain a sample of entries and distinguish recorded facts from reconstructed estimates.

**If the timing is wrong.** Use a labelled practice record with the facilitator. Do not present invented receipts or figures as the learner’s farm history.


### Week 30 — Find the work and costs behind a crop

**Read alongside:** Record-Keeping: Knowing What Your Farm Is Actually Producing; Selling Surplus: Where to Sell and How to Price.

**Why this matters.** A sale has work and costs behind it. Recording those inputs helps you compare ways of growing and selling without making unpaid household work disappear.

**Look closely.** Trace one crop from seed to buyer and list the inputs, transport, packaging and work actually used at each stage.

**Try it in the field**

1. Choose a real crop batch and collect its recorded costs.
2. Record labour time and who did the work, keeping paid cash costs distinct from an explicitly valued estimate of unpaid work.
3. Identify shared items and explain how you allocated them, or leave them visibly unallocated for discussion.

**Keep a record.** A crop cost sheet with units, receipts or sources, labour time and labelled estimates.

**Think it through.** Which cost changes when you sell through a different route?

**Facilitator check.** The learner accounts for relevant work and costs without counting a shared expense twice.

**If the timing is wrong.** Build the sheet with the costs known so far and mark the rest pending; an incomplete honest record is useful for the next visit.


### Week 31 — Compare margin, price and cash timing

**Read alongside:** Selling Surplus: Where to Sell and How to Price.

**Why this matters.** Money from sales is not the same as profit, and a promising season can still run short of cash before harvest. Keep the definitions clear when comparing options.

**Look closely.** Gross margin is enterprise income less its variable costs. It does not by itself account for all fixed costs. Put expected receipts and payments on their actual or expected dates to see cash timing.

**Try it in the field**

1. Use one crop’s real income and variable-cost record to calculate its gross margin, naming the period and units.
2. With the facilitator, identify other costs before discussing the price needed to cover the chosen cost basis.
3. Place expected payments and receipts on a calendar; change a clearly labelled sales assumption and discuss what happens to the cash gap.

**Keep a record.** A checked calculation, its cost basis and a cash calendar separating actual transactions from estimates.

**Think it through.** Could the crop show a positive gross margin while the household still cannot pay an earlier bill?

**Facilitator check.** The learner distinguishes revenue, gross margin, wider costs and cash availability.

**If the timing is wrong.** Use a facilitator’s clearly labelled worked example, then return to the farm’s records when available.

Supporting reading: [Department of Primary Industries: Using gross margins](https://library.dpird.wa.gov.au/bulletins/313/).

### Week 32 — Test a route to market

**Read alongside:** Selling Surplus: Where to Sell and How to Price.

**Why this matters.** A buyer’s price is only part of a selling decision. Quantity, quality, packaging, travel, payment timing and unsold produce affect whether the route works for you.

**Look closely.** Compare two possible selling routes using the same product and unit. Include the work and costs needed to reach each buyer.

**Try it in the field**

1. Ask willing buyers about their requirements and record the date and status of each enquiry.
2. Compare routes against the crop’s likely availability and the household’s transport and work capacity.
3. Plan a small feasible sale or a documented market enquiry, then compare the result with the expectation.

**Keep a record.** A buyer-requirements note, route comparison and any actual sale, cost or unsold quantity.

**Think it through.** Which buyer requirement would be hardest to meet consistently?

**Facilitator check.** Review point 7: the learner connects costs and production evidence to a realistic market decision.

**If the timing is wrong.** Complete the enquiry and comparison first. A market interview is not a purchase agreement or a completed sale.

Supporting reading: [FAO: Entrepreneurship in farming](https://www.fao.org/uploads/media/5-EntrepreneurshipInternLores.pdf).

### Week 33 — Make a clear agreement with neighbours

**Read alongside:** Building Community Food Networks: Strength in Numbers.

**Why this matters.** Shared work depends on people understanding the same agreement. Clear quantities, responsibilities and a way to handle changes can prevent a useful exchange becoming a dispute.

**Look closely.** Compare “we will help each other at harvest” with an agreement naming the work, people, date or trigger, contribution and what happens if plans change.

**Try it in the field**

1. Choose a possible exchange, group task or shared transport arrangement with willing participants.
2. Discuss contributions, responsibilities, records and a fair way to raise disagreements.
3. Write the agreed points in plain language and ask each participant to explain their understanding before acting.

**Keep a record.** An agreement or a clearly labelled practice draft, plus any agreed change after discussion.

**Think it through.** Which unspoken assumption could cause a disagreement later?

**Facilitator check.** The learner can explain both their own responsibility and the other participants’ understanding.

**If the timing is wrong.** Role-play the discussion with a facilitator; do not commit another person’s labour, produce or money without their agreement.


### Week 34 — Collect evidence that tells the truth

**Read alongside:** Record-Keeping: Knowing What Your Farm Is Actually Producing; Building Community Food Networks: Strength in Numbers; Making a Simple Site Map: Your Design Starts on Paper.

**Why this matters.** An impact story is stronger when someone can trace it to real evidence. A photograph, a survey answer and a measured harvest show different things.

**Look closely.** Compare a claim such as “food access improved” with the dated records that might support it. Identify what those records cannot establish on their own.

**Try it in the field**

1. Choose a course outcome and gather only the information needed to examine it.
2. Explain the purpose before asking someone for information or a photograph, and respect their choice about sharing.
3. Check dates, units and duplicated entries. Keep missing answers missing and separate observations from conclusions.

**Keep a record.** An evidence index naming each item, its date, what it supports and any sharing limits.

**Think it through.** What would be misleading if this evidence were shown without its context?

**Facilitator check.** The learner can trace a claim to evidence, explain its limits and respect the contributor’s choices.

**If the timing is wrong.** Use the learner’s own records. Do not fabricate survey answers or use another household’s information without permission.


### Week 35 — Bring the farm plan together in phases

**Read alongside:** Zones and Sectors: Organising Your Farm by Energy; Establishing a Food Forest: Planting Sequence and Timeline; Record-Keeping: Knowing What Your Farm Is Actually Producing.

**Why this matters.** The integrated plan should reflect what the season taught you. A phased plan shows which actions are feasible now and which depend on money, water, advice or earlier work.

**Look closely.** Lay the first concept beside the current map, crop records and household brief. Identify a change supported by experience rather than a more attractive drawing.

**Try it in the field**

1. Update the map with actual work and clearly distinguish proposed additions.
2. Connect crops, water, soil care, access, labour and costs in the next phase.
3. Choose dependencies and review points: what must be checked or completed before each later action begins?

**Keep a record.** An updated concept, phased work list, resource needs and evidence for the main revisions.

**Think it through.** Which proposed investment should wait until its assumption has been checked?

**Facilitator check.** Review point 8: the integrated plan is traceable to site observations, household priorities and realistic resources.

**If the timing is wrong.** Leave unresolved technical or permission-dependent work clearly pending and develop the parts that can be planned from current evidence.


### Week 36 — Review your portfolio and plan next season

**Read alongside:** Record-Keeping: Knowing What Your Farm Is Actually Producing; Drying, Storing, and Sharing Seed; The Three Ethics: Earth Care, People Care, Fair Share.

**Why this matters.** The final review is a chance to explain what you learned, including attempts that did not work. A useful next-season plan grows from that evidence.

**Look closely.** Follow one chain through the portfolio: starting goal, plan, field action, observation, result and revision. Check that the dates and records tell the same story.

**Try it in the field**

1. Select the records that best demonstrate your work and identify any missing field evidence.
2. Explain a success and a difficulty to the facilitator, using the records rather than memory alone.
3. Write the next-season priorities, the first action, the support needed and the conditions you will check before starting.

**Keep a record.** A portfolio index, review feedback and a next-season action plan. Keep deferred seasonal activities explicitly open.

**Think it through.** What can you now decide or demonstrate that you could not at the start?

**Facilitator check.** Review point 9: the learner explains a coherent evidence trail and a realistic next step. Certification follows the programme’s existing assessment rules.

**If the timing is wrong.** Review the evidence available now and schedule the outstanding field observation for the right crop stage; do not substitute a fictional result.
