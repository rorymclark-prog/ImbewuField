# Design curriculum review — 21 September 2026

Rory asks whether the design aspect of a permaculture plan has been treated properly. This is a review of the curriculum currently present, not a claim that a new course has been authored or published.

## Finding

Partly. The core course teaches important components, but does not yet sufficiently teach and assess the complete process of making, comparing, justifying, implementing and revising a site design. This is the specific gap already recorded in `docs/COURSE-GAP-AUDIT-2026-08-10.md`, item 13. More separate topic modules alone will not close it.

Evidence reviewed:

- `lib/course-modules.ts`: ethics and principles; zones and sectors; four Reading the Landscape lessons including a base map; water, soil, planting and livestock topics.
- `lib/course-assignment-content.ts`: the existing capstone asks for boundary, water, zones, planting, structures and an exported plan. Its self-checks establish that features are present, rather than whether their placement and connections are justified and feasible.
- `docs/studies-review-2026-09-20/reserve/app-companions/design.md`: the app companion teaches Design Studio operations. Operating the software is a different learning outcome from making a sound design decision.
- `docs/studies-review-2026-09-20/reserve/facilitator-and-capstone.md`: the unpublished reserve capstone adds household brief, evidence, connected decisions, feasibility, phasing and revision. This is a useful assessment framework, but not yet a full worked teaching sequence.

## Proposed design pathway — not yet authored or integrated

Use one coherent worked site throughout the pathway, followed by a different independent practice site or a learner's consenting household site. Keep the known facts, assumptions, unknowns and proposed changes visibly distinct throughout. Offer paper, oral and app-based submissions. No purchase, paid render or construction should be required to show understanding.

1. **Agree the brief and read the site.** Household priorities, food needs, access and tenure, available time and resources, existing features, observations and measurement sources. Produce a brief and a labelled base map; identify facts still needing verification.
2. **Analyse before placing things.** Use the site's water, slope, sun, wind, access and seasonal observations; distinguish zones from sectors. Show constraints, opportunities and competing needs on analysis overlays. Any technical or legal thresholds must come from verified sources.
3. **Connect functions and compare concepts.** Explain why elements belong together, what each needs and supplies, and how people and materials move between them. Compare at least two plausible arrangements against the same brief, including maintenance and trade-offs. Avoid treating every site as needing every system.
4. **Develop and check the plan.** Move from concepts to a legible scaled layout, showing actual and proposed features, routes, access and relationships. Check that dimensions and labels agree. Identify water, earthworks or other decisions needing qualified assessment before implementation. Keep concept illustrations separate from measured plans.
5. **Make implementation feasible.** Set dependencies and achievable phases, connect the design to the finance course, and identify materials, quotations or unknown costs, labour, care responsibilities and permissions. Explain what can begin now and what must wait for evidence or advice.
6. **Review, explain and revise.** Present the plan, explain placement and rejected alternatives, receive a structured critique, revise with reasons, and specify what to observe later. Assess reasoning, feasibility and evidence as well as completeness and readability.

Each stage needs a short lesson sequence, a demonstrated worked example, learner practice, feedback criteria and a matching app companion. Topic modules supply the relevant technical detail; the pathway teaches how to use it together.
