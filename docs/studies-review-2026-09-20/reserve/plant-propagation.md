# Plant propagation and a small nursery

Status: reserve teaching draft, not in the public module registry. English teaching text drafted; local horticultural and fluent isiZulu review, media production and learner trial outstanding. No crop-specific timings, chemical doses or new species are prescribed.

This develops the nursery, seeds, stem cuttings, division and growing-medium topics in Rory's five-day course, and the propagation/enterprise day in the RVCC outline. It follows the established pattern: a visible question, a short explanation, a demonstration, a practical task and a check of understanding.

## Outcomes

The learner can choose a suitable propagation method with a mentor, set up a labelled nursery batch, handle young plants without damaging their roots, and use a survival record to improve the next batch.

## Lesson 1 — Choose how to make the next plant

**Teaching text.** Propagation means making new plants. Seed is one way. Some plants can also grow from a cutting or a divided clump. A cutting is a piece taken from a parent plant to form a new plant. Division separates an established clump into smaller plants with roots and shoots. The right method depends on the plant. A method that works for one plant may fail for another.

Before taking material, identify the plant with your mentor. Use the course's checked plant list. Check permission to collect and any restrictions on propagating or selling it. A healthy-looking parent can still carry disease. Ask where nursery material came from, and keep its name with the batch.

**Demonstration.** Lay out a seed packet, a rooted nursery plant and a suitable division. Learners point to the seed, stem, roots and growing point. Use plants already approved for the local course; the trainer confirms the method before cutting anything.

**Check.** A neighbour says any branch will grow if it is put in soil. Should you copy the method immediately? **Answer:** identify the plant and check its propagation method first. **Reason:** rooting ability and suitable methods differ.

## Lesson 2 — Make a place where seedlings can be cared for

**Teaching text.** Choose a nursery place you can visit easily. Plan access to suitable water, shelter from damaging weather and protection from livestock. Keep paths clear so you can inspect each batch. Clean containers and tools between batches using the appropriate local nursery hygiene procedure. Use growing medium recommended for the chosen crop. A container needs drainage; a tray standing in dirty water is not a healthy nursery.

Label the batch when you start. Record the plant and variety, source, date and method. Keep different batches separate. A lost label can turn a good plant into an unknown plant that cannot be sold honestly.

**Practical.** Draw the nursery on the farm map. Mark water, access, shade and where new or suspect plants will be kept apart. Make a label and a matching notebook row before filling a tray.

**Check.** Two trays look the same but their labels have gone. Can you sell them under the variety name you remember buying? **Answer:** do not guess. **Reason:** appearance alone may not recover the identity of a mixed batch.

## Lesson 3 — Sow and look after a seed batch

**Teaching text.** Read the packet or the local crop guide before sowing. It tells you the suitable season and sowing method. Use your batch label to keep the instructions with the seed. Water gently so the seed and medium stay in place. Check the moisture and drainage instead of watering by habit.

Record what you put in and what emerges. If little emerges, look back at the seed source, storage, sowing method, moisture and weather. Do not decide that more fertiliser is the answer before you know the problem. Ask for help when seedlings collapse or disease seems to spread.

**Practical.** Start a small batch suited to the available space and care. Count the seeds actually sown and record the emerging seedlings on later visits. Photograph the label and tray together, without people or private details unless they have agreed.

**Check.** A batch has not emerged. What is the useful next step? **Answer:** inspect the conditions and the batch record. **Reason:** a record helps find the cause; guessing a treatment can waste seed and money.

## Lesson 4 — Try a cutting or a division with a mentor

**Teaching text.** For a suitable stem cutting, the trainer shows where the nodes are and which end was nearest the roots. Use a clean, sharp tool. Prepare and place the cutting according to that plant's method; remove leaves that would be buried. Keep it in the advised light and moisture conditions while it forms roots. Do not pull it out every day to check.

For division, keep healthy roots and shoots on each new part. Protect the roots from drying while you replant. A division already has roots; a fresh stem cutting still needs to make them. Grafting is a further skill in which compatible plant parts are joined. It needs a separate supervised demonstration; this introductory lesson does not qualify someone to graft saleable fruit trees.

**Practical.** The trainer demonstrates one locally suitable, approved plant. Each learner repeats the safe handling and labelling. Keep the trial separate from sale stock until its health and identity are checked.

**Check.** A cutting has new leaves. Does that alone prove it has enough roots to plant out? **Answer:** no. **Reason:** the trainer must check establishment; a brief flush of growth is not the same as a sound root system.

## Lesson 5 — Prepare for planting out and learn from the result

**Teaching text.** Nursery plants need preparation for the conditions outside. Gradually adjust protection with advice for the crop and weather. Hardening means helping the plant adjust; it does not mean letting it wilt badly. Prepare the destination and water supply before moving plants.

Moisten the root plug before removal. Support it as it leaves the tray. Do not tear roots by yanking the stem. Use the correct planting depth for the crop; for the demonstrated plug seedling, keep the top of the plug level with the surrounding soil. Water after planting and check establishment. Avoid planting into a waterlogged hole.

Keep the batch record after planting or sale. Record losses as well as surviving plants. Compare what happened with what you expected, then change one part of your next trial deliberately.

**Field assignment.** Submit a nursery sketch, a readable batch label, a record of the method and observations, and an explanation of one improvement. A drawing or spoken explanation can replace writing. No purchase or particular survival percentage is required to demonstrate learning.

**Assessment criteria.** The learner identifies the method, keeps identity with the batch, demonstrates careful handling, records what actually happened and explains when local advice is needed. Attendance alone does not pass the practical skill. If a batch fails, use its evidence to diagnose and repeat; failure is not hidden by replacing the photograph.

## Images and animation brief

Use the warm South African homestead style already approved by Rory. Keep the same farmer, tools, plant and tray across a sequence. Show readable labels as HTML or deterministic overlays, never invented AI lettering.

| Scene | Best treatment | Acceptance check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Seed, cutting and rooted division side by side | Still photograph plus a labelled diagram | The methods remain distinct; no root appears on an unrooted cutting. |
| Seed placed into prepared medium | Flow close-up from an approved start frame | Seed stays the same object; natural hand anatomy; no instant germination. |
| A node and the direction of a cutting | Deterministic botanical animation | Node, cut and orientation remain precise; no decorative time-lapse substitutes for the method. |
| Seedling root plug lowered into a hole | Existing reviewed Flow scene as a candidate | Verify crop-appropriate depth and root plug integrity; do not label it a universal depth rule. |
| A batch followed through time | Dated photographs or an explicitly illustrative sequence | No eight-second shot implies that rooting or establishment takes eight seconds. |

Audio should describe the action before the learner needs to notice it, then leave time to observe. Short clips are optional downloads with size visible. The full text must be readable with sound off. Translation and narration must use the same approved lesson revision.
