# Land, water and community responsibilities

Status: reserve teaching drafts for two modules. These are practical decision and record lessons, not a legal opinion or authority to undertake works. Current local procedures and examples need review before publication.

## Module A — Check permissions before changing a site

### Lesson 1 — A map is not permission

A boundary drawn in an app can help planning, but it does not prove ownership, shared rights or permission to change a place. Identify the people who use and manage the land. Record what is agreed and what is still disputed or uncertain. Discuss fences, access, grazing, gardens and water with the relevant people before implementation. Do not upload identity documents or private agreements into a public classroom exercise.

**Activity:** annotate a sample map with “confirmed”, “needs checking” and “proposed”. **Check:** does placing a dam symbol authorise construction? **Answer:** no; the map shows an idea and separate rights/design/approval checks are needed.

### Lesson 2 — Follow the water and check the use

Describe the water source, proposed use and works clearly. Storage, abstraction and changes to watercourses can fall under water-use regulation. The authorisation route depends on the actual circumstances. Ask DWS or the relevant catchment authority before proceeding; a roof-water example does not authorise a stream diversion.

Municipal rules also matter for plumbing and greywater. Keep untreated greywater away from edible crops and human contact, and drinking-water pipes separate. The course's concept drawings are not construction plans.

**Activity:** prepare a short enquiry with a source description, intended use and an honest map. Mark unmeasured dimensions as unknown. **Check:** can a universal tank or dam size quoted online settle every permission question? **Answer:** no; confirm the applicable requirements for the specific proposal.

### Lesson 3 — Keep decisions traceable

Save the dated advice, its source, conditions and the plan revision it applies to. If the design changes, check whether earlier advice still applies. An estimate is not a measurement, and an attractive rendered image is not an engineer's approval. Name who will inspect and maintain a system after installation.

**Field task:** a sample decision record linking one proposed change to the information, permissions and competent design still needed. **Pass criteria:** separates observation, proposal and approval; identifies the relevant authority; keeps uncertainty visible. No permit application or private land record must be published for assessment.

**Sources:** [DWS water-use authorisation](https://www.dws.gov.za/ewulaas/WUA.aspx), [National Water Act chapter 4](https://www.dws.gov.za/iwqs/nwa/tmp_Chapter_4.html), [WRC greywater guidance](https://wrc.org.za/?mdocs-file=65954), and Rory's site-design curriculum. Current legal thresholds are deliberately not restated from old documents. Site-report, earthworks and crop-plan audits supply the distinction between visual proposal and verified evidence.

**Media:** deterministic source→use→works→questions diagram, with a warm still of a farmer and adviser reading a map. Flow may show the discussion; it must not fabricate an official stamp, permit, surveyed boundary or approval.

## Module B — Shared tools, shared markets and fair agreements

### Lesson 1 — Make the shared task specific

A group works better when people know what they have agreed to do. Choose one manageable activity: sharing a tool, combining transport, swapping seed or hosting a learning visit. Record who participates, who does the work and what each person receives. Make it possible to explain the agreement aloud. Membership alone is not a guarantee of income or equal access.

**Activity:** write a tool-booking example with return, cleaning, repair and loss responsibilities. **Check:** does a shared purchase explain who pays when the tool breaks? **Answer:** no; that needs an agreed arrangement.

### Lesson 2 — Keep produce, money and responsibility visible

For a shared delivery, record each grower's contribution and its unit. Agree quality standards, fees, payment timing, unsold produce and losses before collection. Keep a receipt trail and let members check their own entries. Do not merge kilograms and bunches or divide money using a guessed contribution.

A group may choose a formal structure, but this introductory lesson does not tell every group to register as a cooperative. Seek appropriate local advice on the structure and its obligations.

**Scenario:** a buyer pays for a combined load. **Check:** can the organiser simply divide the payment equally without checking the agreement? **Answer:** no; reconcile the payment, costs and each member's agreed entitlement.

### Lesson 3 — Review the arrangement and resolve problems

Set a time to review what worked, what cost more than expected and who could not participate. Agree how concerns are raised and who helps resolve a dispute. Share learning records with permission. Do not post another household's debts, identity documents or health information in a group chat to settle an argument.

**Field task:** prepare a sample shared-activity agreement and reconcile one example delivery. **Pass criteria:** clear responsibilities, consistent units, transparent costs/payment and a workable review route. The assessor checks reasoning rather than whether a group has bought equipment or registered an entity.

**Sources:** Rory's enterprise and seed-sharing workshops; the farmer-money, training-quality and production-metrics audits; [FAO Farm Business School](https://www.fao.org/4/i2136e/i2136e00.htm) for collective learning and business planning. Local governance and registration requirements need a current, separate review.

**Media:** reuse the reviewed seed-sharing Flow scene as atmosphere. Accounting, tool booking and payment splits belong in deterministic diagrams with checked exercise inputs. No generated money or supposed legal document serves as evidence.

Publication gates for both modules: current local legal/community-practice review, privacy review of sample documents, learner trial of spoken/pictorial activities, fluent isiZulu and audio review, phone readability and full-motion inspection.
