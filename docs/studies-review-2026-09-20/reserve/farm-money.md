# Farm money: records, prices and decisions

Status: reserve teaching draft. Not registered as a public module. English text, activities and assessment drafted; local bookkeeping review, isiZulu review and media remain outstanding. This is elementary farm record literacy, not tax or investment advice.

Built from Rory's enterprise/cashbook workshops, the ASAP handbook's record-keeping section, and the farmer-money and production-metrics audits. Use real local records with private details removed, or clearly labelled practice cards. No fabricated market price, income promise or statutory threshold is a lesson fact.

## Outcomes

The learner can separate a harvest from a cash receipt, keep a usable transaction record, distinguish cash from profit, compare costs and selling prices in matching units, and plan for a period when costs arrive before sales.

## Lesson 1 — Follow the produce and the money separately

**Teaching text.** A harvest can go home, be sold, be shared, remain in storage or be lost. Record where it went. Food taken home has value to the household, but no buyer paid cash for it. An order is not yet a payment. A sale on credit means the buyer still owes you.

When payment arrives, connect it to the sale. Do not count the sale again as new produce or count the same payment twice. Keep the harvest date, sale date and payment date where they are needed; these may be different days.

**Activity.** Sort cards headed “harvest”, “home food”, “sale”, “money received”, “gift” and “loss”. Move one imagined harvest through the cards. Explain which movements change the cash balance. The facilitator supplies quantities from the exercise record, not from a yield assumption.

**Check.** A customer collects produce today and agrees to pay later. Is that cash available for tomorrow's feed? **Answer:** no. **Reason:** money owed is not money received.

## Lesson 2 — Keep a record you can check

**Teaching text.** Record the date, what happened, the amount and unit, and the money received or paid. Keep a receipt or another reference when available. Say which part of the farm the transaction belongs to. Record small costs too; transport, packaging and losses can change the result.

Check the saved entry. A message saying it is waiting to sync is different from confirmation that it reached the server. Keep the receipt until you can reconcile the record. If a price is missing, mark it as unknown. Entering zero says it cost nothing, which is a different statement.

**Activity.** Enter a practice transaction in a notebook or sample app. Find it again. Compare date, decimal amount, unit and description with the source card. Correct a deliberate unit mistake and explain why kilograms and bunches cannot simply be added.

**Check.** The price box is empty. Should you enter zero to finish the record? **Answer:** no; find the price or record it as unknown. **Reason:** a missing value must not become a claim that the item was free.

## Lesson 3 — Cash remaining is not the whole profit

**Teaching text.** Cash shows money coming in and going out. Profit asks whether the income from the work covers its costs for the period. The two questions need different information. A loan increases cash but is not a crop sale. Money paid for equipment is not the same as using up all that equipment in one season.

Look beyond the costs already entered. Include relevant materials, transport, labour and other costs when judging an enterprise. Make family labour visible even when no wage was paid. Keep household withdrawals separate from farm operating costs. Ask a bookkeeper how to treat equipment, loans and stock in the final accounts.

The app may show sales minus recorded or assigned costs. Read that label carefully. A positive contribution does not prove full profit when costs are incomplete.

**Activity.** Circle missing costs on a practice enterprise sheet. Show a mentor which result is cash, which is a partial margin, and which cannot yet be calculated. The correct answer may be “we do not have enough information”.

**Check.** There is cash in the account because the farm borrowed money. Does that prove the enterprise is profitable? **Answer:** no. **Reason:** the source of cash and the cost of earning income matter.

## Lesson 4 — Price a product in the unit you sell

**Teaching text.** Know what the buyer is receiving: a kilogram, a bunch, a tray or another clearly defined unit. Use that same unit when comparing your selling price with your cost. Weigh or count honestly. Include preparation, packaging, delivery and expected unsold produce in the decision.

Ask actual buyers about quality, quantity, price and payment terms. Record the place and date of a quote. An old price in a handbook is not today's price in your market. A higher selling price may come with higher delivery costs or slower payment. Compare the whole arrangement.

**Activity.** Compare two real offers using the same quantity and unit. Write down what each buyer requires and when each pays. If a cost is missing, name it before recommending an option.

**Check.** One buyer offers more per kilogram but requires costly delivery. Is it automatically the better sale? **Answer:** no. **Reason:** compare the money retained, the work required and payment reliability.

## Lesson 5 — Plan before the cash runs short

**Teaching text.** List the work you plan to do and when you must pay for it. Put expected receipts beside the dates when buyers are likely to pay. Keep expected figures separate from completed sales. Mark uncertain quantities and prices. Consider what changes if sales are smaller, a buyer pays late or an input costs more.

Check what you already own before budgeting for new equipment. Existing infrastructure may still need maintenance; it is not automatically free to use. Start at a scale you can supply and manage. Do not borrow just because a plan shows an attractive total without explaining its assumptions.

**Activity.** Make a simple season calendar with costs, expected payments and known commitments. Identify a possible cash gap and a practical response: phase a purchase, reduce the batch, agree different payment terms or seek qualified advice. Do not record hoped-for funding as confirmed money.

**Check.** The season may end with a surplus, but inputs must be paid for before the first sale. Can the farm still face a cash shortage? **Answer:** yes. **Reason:** timing matters as well as the final total.

## Field assignment and assessment

Bring a redacted record or use the supplied practice set. Reconcile one harvest with its destinations, connect one payment to its sale, explain one missing cost, compare offers in the same unit, and identify one possible cash gap. The learner may explain aloud while a facilitator records the reasoning. Do not require learners to disclose debts, bank details, identity numbers or household income to a group.

Pass criteria: accurate distinction between produce and cash; no duplicate payment; dates/units checked; missing values remain unknown; contribution not called full profit; planned figures labelled; a reasoned next action. Correct misunderstandings with a fresh example and reassess. Completion does not certify a business profitable or compliant with tax law.

## Media plan

Use the existing warm harvest-record, buyer and seed-sharing photographs. Add a full-scene picture of a grower checking a receipt with a mentor, using blank illustrative paper rather than invented figures. Flow can animate handing over a produce basket or placing a receipt beside a notebook. Keep numbers and accounting logic in deterministic overlays: harvest → destinations; sale → amount owed → payment received; costs and receipts on a calendar. Do not use generated banknotes, fabricated app screenshots or “instant profit” imagery. All calculations must be independently checked against the exercise inputs.

## Sources and boundaries

- Rory's course outlines: live cashbook exercise, enterprise comparison and a practical take-home plan. ASAP handbook: production record and cashbook structure. The new draft corrects the manuals' tendency to equate cash remaining with profit and does not reuse old prices, tax thresholds or percentage saving rules.
- `AUDIT-FARMER-MONEY-2026-08-02.md`: 27 concrete app-record hazards, including units, cents, missing values, dated prices and double counting. Historical bugs are teaching test cases, not claims about current production defects.
- `PRODUCTION-METRICS-AUDIT-2026-09-05.md`: contribution, mapped area, missing costs and destination assumptions.
- [FAO Farm Business School manual](https://www.fao.org/4/i2136e/i2136e00.htm): farm-business learning through records, market investigation, planning and review. Its regional context and examples are not South African prices.
- [FAO record keeping](https://www.fao.org/4/w6864e/w6864e0f.htm): cash, sales, stock and amounts owed are distinct records; invoice and payment dates can differ.
- [FAO small-scale dairy record keeping](https://www.fao.org/4/t1265e/t1290e01.htm): preserve supporting documents and distinguish loan movements when considering cash flow and profit.

Publication gates: local bookkeeping review of worked exercises, arithmetic checks, phone-readable diagrams, fluent isiZulu review, narration aligned with the final script, and app screenshots verified against the released interface. A separate current-source lesson is needed before teaching South African registration, tax or lending obligations.
