# F3 - Know costs and set prices

Production status: three English teaching drafts with original practice cards, a learner workbook and checked answers. **Reserve only.** Not a published, narrated, translated or accredited finance course. South African bookkeeping and learner review remain open. This is Unit F3 of the [24-lesson pathway](../financial-course.md), following F1 and F2.

## Before teaching

Bring paper, a calculator and the learner workbook. Read the cards aloud if needed. Learners may point, sort cards or explain their answer to a partner; fluent reading is not the assessment. Begin by asking what it costs to get one product from the garden into a buyer's hands. Keep quantities, time and money on separate lines.

All amounts, weights, work hours and quotes below are **invented classroom inputs**. They are not recommended prices, wages, productivity rates or yields. The R25.00 hourly value is a comparison assumption, not a legal wage or payment instruction. No area, real growing season or tax status is supplied. Do not infer these. A, B and C are separate exercises; do not add their totals or carry forward F1/F2 balances.

Outcomes: assign a cost once; explain the basis for sharing it; keep unpaid work visible; use the right denominator; distinguish markup from margin; test a bounded break-even calculation; and compare buyer terms without equating the highest price with the best outcome.

## F3.1 - Direct costs, shared costs and family labour

**Narration draft:** “Start with a product and a period. A direct cost belongs to that product. A shared cost serves more than one product. Use a record of how it was used to divide it, and explain your choice. Divide the cost; do not copy the whole bill into both crops. Keep the family's work hours visible too. No wage paid does not mean no work done. An amount used to value that time is different from cash paid out.”

**Demonstration:** Place A01-A04 beneath their own crop headings. A05 is one hire bill for R120.00. The equipment record shows two hours for carrots and four for the second crop: carrots receive 2/6 of the bill, the second crop 4/6. Together the shares must still equal the one bill. A06 is a separate R90.00 market trip. Its agreed practice basis is occupied equal-size crate spaces, two for carrots and one for the other crop. Explain why this bill uses a different record.

There is no universal rule that hours or crate spaces always give the fairest allocation. Ask what caused the shared cost and whether the record describes it. The chosen basis must fit the decision and period. If the usage record is missing, show an unallocated amount and investigate. Do not quietly assign it all to the crop that looks most profitable.

**Two questions, not two interchangeable labels:** “Which product uses it?” gives direct or shared. “What changes when this activity changes?” gives fixed or variable. Hiring a tool for an agreed day may be direct to one crop but unchanged if that day's output is a little smaller. Extra packaging may vary with packs sold. A shared bill can contain variable items. Always state the period and the change being considered.

**Guided practice:** Allocate both shared bills, add each crop's direct costs and check the combined amount. Then add the separately stated family-time comparison: four hours and six hours at the hypothetical R25.00 per hour. Keep the cash column intact. No payment has been invented, and no family time should be added twice if it is already paid and included elsewhere.

**Independent attempt:** A new R75.00 hire bill was used one hour by one activity and two hours by another. Divide it and check the sum. Then explain why “shared” does not automatically mean “fixed”. Classify an owner withdrawal and an equipment purchase separately; neither should be hidden inside materials used. Equipment use and its cash purchase may fall in different periods; ask for bookkeeping help on the appropriate treatment.

**Feedback:** If both crops receive R120.00, place the single hire receipt between them and ask how the farm obtained two bills. If the learner divides every shared cost equally, ask which source supports the equal split. If family time becomes a cash payment, ask for the payment evidence. Retry with the new hire bill rather than repeating the original numbers.

**App connection:** Use the published [expense guide](https://imbewufield.vercel.app/student/guides/expenses) to record an actual paid cost and find its receipt in the sample workflow. Use this workbook for the allocation exercise. Do not create a second expense merely to assign one existing bill to another crop, or enter an unpaid comparison value as money spent. The app's current records are not proof that every cost has been included.

**Field task:** Choose one small practice enterprise. Name its product, unit and period. Collect three redacted cost references, identify a shared cost and record a possible allocation basis. List missing evidence. Do not require learners to disclose private household finances.

## F3.2 - Unit cost, contribution and break-even

**Narration draft:** “A cost per unit needs two things: the costs you included and the quantity you divided by. Say both. Produce available for sale is not always produce sold. A selling price may cover some costs and still leave others unpaid. Contribution is what remains after the included variable costs. It must still cover fixed costs. A break-even answer is useful only when its assumptions fit and the required sales can be achieved.”

**Demonstration A - choose the denominator:** The carrot exercise has R220.00 of assigned paid costs and 40 kg of marketable produce. That is R5.50 per marketable kg for those included costs. With the separate R100.00 family-time comparison, the total becomes R320.00, or R8.00 per marketable kg. These are two labelled views of one budget, not two bills to add.

If only 32 kg is expected to sell, recovering that same R320.00 entirely through those sales requires R10.00 per kg sold. This is a **sales-recovery planning target**, not the accounting cost of each item sold or a promise that a buyer will pay it. The other 8 kg is not automatically lost or household food; investigate its destination and value. Do not write off closing stock simply because it has not sold. Missing land, equipment, administration, finance and tax information means full profit is still unknown.

**Demonstration B - a separate simplified model:** B01 supplies included fixed costs of R105.00 per practice cycle and variable costs of R7.50 per pack. Each pack is 1 kg and sells for R12.00. For this model only, every pack prepared is sold, price and variable cost stay constant, and fixed costs stay unchanged up to the stated capacity of 30 packs. It is not a forecast derived from the carrot harvest.

Contribution per pack is R12.00 minus R7.50 = R4.50. R105.00 divided by R4.50 is 23 and one-third packs. Whole packs require rounding **up** to 24. Verify on either side: at 23 packs the model is R1.50 short; at 24 it has R3.00 left after its included costs. Call that the model surplus, not full farm profit. This model does not account for prepared-but-unsold packs, waste, changing rates, bad debts or omitted costs. A real plan with those features needs them added explicitly.

**Guided practice:** At 30 packs, calculate revenue and both kinds of included cost. Repeat for 18 packs prepared and sold. Then keep the original prices but reduce capacity to 20. The numerical break-even point does not change, but it is outside that capacity. Never recommend “sell more” without testing production capacity and evidence of demand. Expected sales are assumptions, not confirmed orders.

If price equals or is below variable cost while fixed costs remain positive, more units do not cover those fixed costs under this model. Report no feasible break-even quantity under these assumptions, not zero units. If a cost is missing, the result is unknown; an empty value is not a free input.

**Markup and margin:** A separate card gives included cost R8.00 and selling price R10.00. The difference is R2.00. As a share of cost it is 25% markup; as a share of selling price it is 20% margin on those included costs. These are different denominators. Neither is a complete net-profit margin if costs are missing.

**Independent attempt:** Use fixed costs R80.00, price R10.00, variable cost R6.00, and capacity 18 whole packs. Find the break-even quantity and explain the capacity problem. Then compare a R6.00 included cost with a R7.50 selling price: calculate markup and margin, naming the denominator each time.

**Feedback:** For an answer of 23 packs, ask the learner to multiply 23 by R4.50 and compare with R105.00. For “25% margin”, ask “25% of which amount?” For a missing variable cost treated as zero, restore the blank source card and ask what evidence is missing. For “profit”, have the learner name an excluded cost and relabel the result.

**App connection:** Keep these what-if calculations in the workbook. Do not enter forecast sales as actual sales in My Records. Use the existing expense and harvest guides to find the underlying real records. A chart based on saved rows does not supply a missing stock valuation, family-time value or depreciation policy.

**Field task:** Prepare one labelled cost-per-unit estimate from agreed practice data. Write the period, costs included, exclusions, unit, expected quantity sold, and source date. Test a smaller sales quantity. Explain what evidence is needed before using the estimate to negotiate a real price.

## F3.3 - Compare buyers and agree a price

**Narration draft:** “Compare the whole offer. Check the weight, quality, price, packaging, delivery and payment date. Use the same unit for both buyers. A higher price can leave less after delivery. Money promised for next week is not money available today. Write down what is agreed, and check what is still unknown before choosing.”

**Demonstration:** Decision C offers the same 20 kg of accepted produce to either Buyer A or Buyer B. A offers R10.00/kg, collects it, requires no extra packaging and pays on Day 0. B offers R12.00/kg, but the grower pays R35.00 delivery and R10.00 additional packaging; B promises payment on Day 7. These are alternatives, not two simultaneous sales of the same produce.

A leaves R200.00 after the specified offer-specific cash costs. B leaves R195.00. A's lower headline price leaves R5.00 more at that stage. Both options also relate to R140.00 of already-incurred common costs; subtracting those gives R60.00 and R55.00, respectively. Those are partial results, not full profit. Do not charge the common cost twice or add the two offers together.

The cards separately record additional unpaid selling work: half an hour for A, one and a half for B. At the same hypothetical comparison value, that adds R12.50 or R37.50 to the management view, leaving R47.50 or R17.50 after the specified common costs and additional family-time value. The farm has not paid those wages. Other omitted work or costs could still change the full result.

**Guided decision:** State what the supplied evidence favours: A retains more after these specified costs, requires less additional time and pays earlier. Do not claim the buyer is reliable merely because the card promises payment. Ask about confirmed quantities, acceptance standards, rejections, collection arrangements and how the agreement will be recorded. A real buyer relationship may have advantages the cards do not describe; name those separately rather than inventing a monetary score.

**Independent attempt:** For a fresh 10 kg, a collecting buyer offers R10.00/kg and a delivery buyer R12.00/kg. The delivery charge is missing. Find the charge at which their cash results would be equal, and explain why no final ranking is justified yet. Another buyer quotes per crate without a crate weight: explain what measurement is needed before comparing it with a per-kg price.

**Feedback:** If the learner chooses B just because 12 is greater than 10, lay the delivery and packaging cards beside that offer. If Day 7 money is treated as today's cash, move its payment card along a paper calendar. If the missing delivery charge becomes zero, keep the result blank and write the next question to ask.

**App connection:** The [invoice guide](https://imbewufield.vercel.app/student/guides/invoices) covers checking the buyer, units, amounts and payment status after an agreement. An invoice is not evidence that payment arrived. Link an existing recorded sale rather than recording it again. These alternative quotes belong in the workbook; do not create two sample sales and present both as completed business. Partial payments still need a separate balance record; do not mark an invoice paid in full after a deposit.

**Field task:** Obtain two redacted or facilitator-supplied offers for a clearly specified product. Record the quote date and all conditions. Compare the supported costs and payment timing. Give a reasoned choice or say precisely which missing fact prevents one.

## Independent answers and assessment

The new R75.00 hire allocation is **R25.00 and R50.00**. The new break-even case needs **20 packs**, beyond capacity 18; at 18 its model result is **-R8.00**. R6.00 cost and R7.50 price give **R1.50 difference, 25% markup and 20% margin**. The fresh buyer comparison gives collection value R100.00 versus delivery value R120.00 before the unknown charge: the cash results tie at **R20.00 delivery cost**. Below that charge the delivery offer retains more; above it collection retains more, considering only these stated cash items. With the charge missing, no final ranking follows.

To pass, the learner must allocate each bill once, check the total, name the basis, keep time value separate from cash, use and label a correct denominator, round whole-unit break-even upward, reject a capacity or evidence gap, and compare an offer beyond its price. One correct total with an incorrect explanation is insufficient. Supply a fresh case after feedback; record what the learner actually demonstrates. Completion is evidence of these practice skills, not certification of a business's profitability.
