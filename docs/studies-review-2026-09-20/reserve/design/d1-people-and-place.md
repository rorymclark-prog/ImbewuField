# D1 — Understand the people and the place

Status: three new English reserve lessons. Not published, translated, narrated or learner-tested. Sources and exclusions are in SOURCES.md. Read CASEBOOK.md before using the worked examples.

## D1.1 — Agree what the household needs

**Question to show.** A drawing contains a pond, orchard, poultry area and many beds. The household asked for food near home and less carrying. Has the drawing answered the brief?

**Teaching text.** A brief says what the design should help people do. Begin by listening. Ask who uses the place, what is already working and what is difficult. Ask about food, movement, care work and what people want to protect. People may value privacy, a familiar gathering place or a clear route as much as another growing area.

Include the people who do the work. Do not ask only the person who speaks first. A proposal that relies on another person's daily labour needs their agreement. Ask what happens when the usual carer is away. Keep private household information out of a public course example.

Separate a need from an idea. “We need a reliable way to water the existing garden” states a need. “We need a new tank” suggests one possible response. The response still needs information about supply, demand, location, cost and care. Drawing it does not answer those questions.

Write the priorities in plain language, or record them with consent as an oral explanation. Say what is outside this first phase. Record available resources as known, reported or still unknown. Read the brief back to the household and invite corrections. Disagreement is useful information; do not hide it to finish the sheet.

**Worked example.** BRIEF-A in the busy-yard case prioritises food, clear access and manageable care. The household has not asked for animals. RESOURCES-A has no budget or care-time figures. The learner writes “confirm care time and costs”, not an invented amount, and keeps the route in the brief.

**Learner task.** Make a brief with spaces for people and uses, priorities, things to protect, agreed scope, available resources, and questions still open. Use the practice case or obtain consent for a real household exercise. A partner reads it back. Change anything that misrepresents the household.

**Check.** Does agreement on the brief establish permission to alter a neighbour's drainage route? **Answer:** no. **Reason:** the brief records household aims; permissions and effects beyond the site need their own checks.

**Evidence for feedback.** The learner distinguishes needs from proposed objects, includes the people doing the work, and leaves missing resources visible. A list of attractive features alone does not pass this task.

**App companion.** Getting started and evidence guides. A paper brief is valid; do not put private household details into a public sample.

## D1.2 — Observe, ask and keep the source

**Question to show.** A resident describes water crossing the yard during heavy rain. The visit is dry. How should that information appear in the notes?

**Teaching text.** Observation starts with what is there. Walk safely with someone who knows the place. Look at routes, water points, ground cover, shade, buildings, growing areas and signs of past use. Listen to the people who use the site. A short visit and a long memory offer different evidence.

Keep those differences visible. “I saw this” is an observation. “The resident described this” is a report. “I measured this” needs the method and unit. “I think this may happen” is an interpretation. “I want to put this here” is a proposal. All can be useful, but they are not interchangeable.

Record when and under what conditions you observed. A shaded patch during one visit is not a full seasonal sunlight record. A dry water point on one day does not by itself explain the cause or the whole year's supply. Look again when it is safe and useful. Do not wait for perfect knowledge before keeping a record, and do not pretend a short visit supplies it.

Use a notebook, labelled drawing or consented photograph. Give each piece of evidence a short reference you can put on the map. If a plant, pipe or boundary is uncertain, label the uncertainty. Do not identify a plant or underground service from an attractive illustration.

**Worked example.** WALK-A records the route and carried containers as seen. TALK-A records the resident's rain account as reported. The learner does not turn that account into a measured contour or flood depth. Further investigation becomes an action in the evidence register.

**Learner task.** For each note, record the feature, what is known, how it is known, source/conditions, and what needs checking. Ask a partner to point out any sentence where a guess sounds like a measured fact. Correct the label, not the evidence.

**Check.** Is “water always comes from this direction” justified by one dry-day visit? **Answer:** no. **Reason:** the statement exceeds the evidence. Record the account and seek appropriate observations or assessment.

**Evidence for feedback.** The learner distinguishes observed, reported, measured, inferred and proposed information, and names a useful next check. More photographs do not automatically mean better evidence.

**App companion.** Evidence guide. Preserve the source with the feature; exporting a picture does not verify the information in it.

## D1.3 — Make a base map you can trust

**Question to show.** A learner moves an existing path on a base map to make room for a proposed bed. What information has been lost?

**Teaching text.** A base map shows the place before the proposed changes. It helps everyone discuss the same site. Show the existing buildings, access routes, water points, growing areas and other features that matter to the brief. Mark north when you have checked it, and keep the source of the boundary visible.

A first sketch may help you understand relationships. Label it “not to scale” if that is what it is. Before using a plan to judge fit, check the measurements and use a consistent scale. Keep units with dimensions. Measure relevant widths and distances safely, and record whether a value was measured, taken from a source or estimated. A precise-looking line is not proof of a precise measurement.

Keep the existing map and the proposal separate. A transparent sheet over paper, a saved copy or a clearly labelled proposal layer lets you try ideas while preserving what is actually there. Do not silently shift the house, path or boundary to make an idea fit. A traced satellite picture can be a starting reference; it does not by itself establish legal ownership or a surveyed boundary.

Give the map a title, date or revision, legend and source notes. Use words as well as colour so a printed or colour-blind reader can distinguish existing features, proposed changes and uncertain information. Leave enough space for labels to be read.

**Worked example.** MAP-A is useful for discussion but lacks dimensions and verified orientation. The learner keeps its “not to scale” label and lists the measurements needed. They cannot claim a new bed fits simply because a small rectangle fits on the sketch.

**Learner task.** Make the existing-features map and a separate blank proposal sheet. Match each important feature to a note from D1.2. If measurement is unavailable, identify the missing measurement and the decision it prevents. For a real site, retain the original measurements when trying a new layout.

**Check.** Can a neat exported map turn an estimated boundary into a surveyed one? **Answer:** no. **Reason:** export changes presentation, not the source or certainty of the boundary.

**Evidence for feedback.** Existing and proposed features remain distinct; scale status, orientation status, units and source notes are explicit. Fit is not asserted without the relevant measurements.

**App companion.** Mapping and design guides. Practise on a copy or sample first. Verify the saved feature and its measurements after editing; use Undo for an accidental edit rather than accepting a convenient distortion.
