# D3 — Connect functions and compare options

Status: three English reserve drafts. D1–D2 supply the household brief, base map and analysis. The worked case is illustrative; final measured layouts and visual demonstrations remain to be produced.

## D3.1 — Ask what each part needs and gives

**Question to show.** Compost is placed beside a garden on a drawing. What would make that relationship useful in daily work?

**Teaching text.** A useful connection is more than a line between two objects. Ask what each part needs, what it produces, who cares for it and how things move between the parts. A compost area may receive suitable material and supply finished compost, but someone must sort, carry and manage it. Its position must allow that work and suit local hygiene and site conditions.

Write needs and outputs beside the elements you are considering. Include water, suitable materials, space, access, time and skills. Do not count an output before it is available and suitable for its intended use. A proposed animal system does not provide free fertility: animals also require care, feed, water, welfare and management of wastes.

Look for useful connections, but also conflicts. A convenient position might obstruct a route. One person's useful output might create another person's handling burden. A connection that needs daily attention can fail if nobody agrees to provide it. Keep bought inputs and materials leaving the site in the picture; a neat cycle diagram does not prove that everything is supplied on site.

**Worked example.** The busy yard already connects the home, water point and growing patch through a carrying route. Before adding another system, the learner asks whether the current care journey can be improved. BRIEF-A excludes animals from this phase, so livestock are not added to complete a decorative cycle.

**Learner task.** Draw the needs, outputs, journeys and responsible people for the elements in your brief. Circle an assumed connection and name the evidence needed to confirm it. Use the appropriate technical module before proposing a material's use.

**Check.** Does an arrow from animals to a garden prove that the garden needs no other fertility inputs? **Answer:** no. **Reason:** amounts, suitability, losses, other inputs and crop requirements have not been established.

**Evidence for feedback.** The learner explains a real connection, its care requirement and a possible conflict, without promising a closed or maintenance-free system.

## D3.2 — Make alternatives before choosing

**Question to show.** A learner has drawn one beautiful arrangement. What can they compare it with to decide whether it answers the brief well?

**Teaching text.** A first idea is a starting point. Make different concept arrangements while changes are still easy. Keep the same brief, base map and evidence for each. A comparison is unfair if one option receives an imaginary water supply or unpaid labour while the other must use the recorded resources.

A concept shows the main relationships and intended uses. It does not yet settle every dimension or technical detail. Use a proposal overlay so existing features remain visible. Label what is retained, changed, added or deferred. Keep unresolved matters beside the affected part of each option.

Make the alternatives genuinely useful. They might compare improving an existing growing area with relocating it, or a modest first phase with later expansion. Do not make one option deliberately foolish so your favourite wins. Keeping the present arrangement while gathering evidence can also be a reasonable option.

**Worked example.** Busy-yard concept A improves care of the existing patch while preserving access. Concept B investigates a different location, also preserving access. B must still answer the water report, shade, soil, measurement and care questions; A must still answer whether the existing patch can meet the household's priorities. Neither receives a promised harvest or invented cost advantage.

**Learner task.** Draw alternative concepts on copies of the same base. Under each, write its intended benefit, likely burden, important unknown and what would rule it out. Ask a partner whether both options use the same evidence.

**Check.** Can a concept be called cheaper when its material quantities and costs are missing? **Answer:** no. **Reason:** “cost unknown” must stay visible until a comparable estimate is supported.

**Evidence for feedback.** Alternatives differ meaningfully, preserve existing facts and are compared on equal assumptions. Sketches remain labelled as concepts rather than build-ready plans.

## D3.3 — Choose with reasons, not decoration

**Question to show.** One option looks better in a rendered picture. The other better preserves daily access. What should the decision record explain?

**Teaching text.** Compare the options against the criteria you wrote from the brief. Start with needs that cannot be traded away: usable access, agreed care, required permissions and a proposal's basic suitability. Then consider benefits, burdens, costs and what may change over time.

Use words and evidence references. “Possible, but water evidence is missing” is more honest than a precise-looking score with no basis. If you use a scoring exercise, agree what each score means and who chooses the priorities. Adding scores must not cancel an unresolved safety or permission requirement.

Ask the household to explain the choice in its own words. A designer's preference should not quietly replace the household's priorities. Record why an option was chosen, why another was set aside and what new information could change the decision. A conditional choice is valid when its condition is clear and the affected work waits.

**Worked example.** The busy-yard household provisionally prefers investigating improvements to the existing patch because the brief values manageable care and keeping access. This is not proof of lower cost or higher yield. Relocation remains available if later measurements or site assessment show the existing patch cannot meet the brief.

**Learner task.** Complete a comparison sheet: criterion, supporting evidence, implication for each option, remaining check and decision. Present the strongest argument for the option you did not choose. Then record the household's response.

**Check.** Can several attractive benefits compensate for blocking the only agreed access route? **Answer:** not without resolving that access requirement with the people affected. **Reason:** a numerical total or attractive illustration cannot erase a necessary condition.

**Evidence for feedback.** The choice follows the brief and evidence, acknowledges a trade-off and specifies a reason to reconsider. A principle's name alone is not a justification.

**App companion for D3.** Design and evidence guides. Keep versions of alternative concepts; do not overwrite the existing base or silently change measured features when comparing layouts.
