# D4 — Develop and check the plan

Status: three English reserve drafts. The busy-yard case has no measured source pack yet. A worked scaled layout and its visual checks must be supplied before these lessons can be released as a complete demonstrated sequence. The text below does not certify that missing deliverable.

## D4.1 — Move from a concept to a measured layout

**Question to show.** A proposed bed fits on a sketch. What must be known before saying it fits on the ground?

**Teaching text.** A concept explains an arrangement. A developed layout must also show whether its parts fit the available space. Return to the base-map measurements. Check the boundaries relevant to the work, the existing features, access and the distances needed to place the proposal. Keep the measurement method and units with the record.

Use a consistent scale for the plan. Check a known distance against that scale rather than trusting how large the picture looks on a screen. Resizing an image can change its printed scale. A dimension label and the drawn geometry should agree; if they disagree, find the source of the error before using either.

Allow for working access and the needs of the selected system. Do not borrow a universal spacing from a decorative drawing. Use the approved technical source for the actual crop, structure or system, including growth and maintenance needs. If the required dimension is unknown, mark that part incomplete.

**Worked example.** MAP-A is not to scale. The busy-yard learner cannot yet prove that a new bed fits beside the route. They list the measurements needed and preserve the existing path. The correct next step is to obtain a suitable measured base, not shrink the path until the picture looks finished.

**Learner task.** On a measured practice site supplied by the facilitator, reconcile the written dimensions, units and scaled drawing. Point to the information that establishes fit. On your own design, mark any proposal still awaiting measurement or technical dimensions. Do not conduct unsafe or specialist surveying as an unsupervised course task.

**Check.** Does zooming in on the app make an estimated distance more accurate? **Answer:** no. **Reason:** zoom changes the view, not the measurement source.

**Evidence for feedback.** The learner uses consistent units, checks fit against evidence and refuses to claim missing dimensions. This task cannot be passed from the unmeasured busy-yard sketch alone.

## D4.2 — Check the connections before approving work

**Question to show.** Every planned feature fits within the boundary. Is the whole plan ready to implement?

**Teaching text.** Fitting the objects is only one check. Follow the people, water, materials and maintenance journeys between them. Check that a route remains usable when people carry tools or produce. Ask whether a mature planting or a stored material could obstruct access that is clear in today's drawing.

Look beyond the selected area. A water change can affect places downstream; a fence can affect shared access. Record the people and resources affected. The design folder should identify relevant permissions and technical advice, not assume them from a boundary line or software tool.

Ask how the system behaves when something goes wrong. If supply is interrupted, who responds? If a component blocks or overflows, where could the problem go? Technical designs need appropriate assessment; do not invent a safe outlet or drainage capacity. When evidence is incomplete, hold the affected work and explain the dependency.

**Worked example.** The busy-yard proposal keeps the route visible, but the learner still checks it with its users. A new tank remains only an idea: water demand, supply, siting, support, cost and overflow are not established. The design can progress in other areas while that proposal remains unresolved.

**Learner task.** Walk through the proposed daily routine using the map. Mark a possible conflict, the evidence needed to resolve it and who should help. Check both normal use and a relevant disruption. Refer to the technical modules rather than supplying a guessed dimension or capacity.

**Check.** Does the presence of a Water or Earthworks tool mean any placement it accepts is suitable? **Answer:** no. **Reason:** drawing software does not replace site-specific assessment or permission.

**Evidence for feedback.** The learner checks relationships and consequences, identifies an unresolved dependency and keeps the corresponding work conditional.

## D4.3 — Make a plan another person can read

**Question to show.** A map has beautiful plants but no distinction between existing and proposed features. What might the person doing the work misunderstand?

**Teaching text.** A plan communicates decisions. Give it a clear title, revision and purpose. Show orientation, scale status, units, legend and sources. Distinguish existing features from proposed changes and unresolved information using labels or patterns as well as colour. Keep labels near the correct features without covering essential details.

Separate information when a single sheet becomes crowded. A base map, analysis overlay, proposal and phase sheet can tell different parts of the same story. Keep feature names and references consistent between them. The cost record and care plan should refer to the same items shown on the map.

Inspect the actual export at the size it will be used. Can someone read it on a phone or printed sheet? Are labels clipped? Does the legend describe things that are really present? Does decorative artwork hide a path or imply features that were never recorded? Correct the output and check again.

**Worked example.** MAP-A keeps its “not to scale” status in every export. A proposal view must not lose that limitation merely because it receives a polished background. The learner uses separate labels for the retained patch and any future idea.

**Learner task.** Give the plan to a partner without explaining it first. Ask them to identify what exists, what is proposed, where access runs and which work is still on hold. Record the confusion, revise the sheet and repeat the reading task.

**Check.** Is an AI illustration evidence that the saved plan contains the same geometry? **Answer:** no. **Reason:** the illustration and measured plan have different evidential roles; inspect the actual plan and its sources.

**Evidence for feedback.** Another reader can correctly interpret the plan, and the learner has checked a real export. An export button being clicked is not sufficient.

**App companion for D4.** Mapping, design and exports guides. Compare the saved dimensions and exported view; preserve the original geometry when improving presentation. No paid render is required.
