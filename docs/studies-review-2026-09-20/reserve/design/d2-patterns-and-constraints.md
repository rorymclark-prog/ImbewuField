# D2 — Read patterns and constraints

Status: three new English reserve lessons. D1 supplies the brief, evidence register and base map. These lessons analyse that evidence; they do not provide engineering, fire-management or water-permission instructions.

## D2.1 — Use zones and sectors for different questions

**Question to show.** One arrow follows a daily watering journey. Another marks a reported wind direction. Do they describe the same kind of information?

**Teaching text.** Zone planning considers how people use and care for things. Something needing frequent attention should be considered in relation to the routes people already take. Look at the actual work: visiting, watering, harvesting, feeding, carrying and checking. A short distance on paper may still be awkward if there is a steep step, a locked gate or an obstructed path.

Zones are a thinking tool, not compulsory rings. A small site does not need to contain every zone. Do not assign a fixed zone to every animal or crop without considering its care and the household. The placement must also suit the element's other needs.

Sector analysis considers influences such as sun, wind, incoming water and neighbouring activities. Use the evidence for this site. Label a direction as observed, reported or still needing confirmation. A regional description is not proof that the wind at this particular home always comes from one direction.

Put the layers beside each other. Convenience may suggest a place near the home; shade, access or other constraints may make that place unsuitable. This is a question to resolve, not a reason to ignore one of the layers. Explain both the benefit and the limitation of a proposed position.

**Worked example.** WALK-A shows containers carried to the existing patch. That supports investigating the care journey. The eastern shade snapshot and TALK-A's water report remain separate evidence. Neither gives enough information to declare a new growing position suitable.

**Learner task.** On one overlay, draw actual care journeys and note their purpose and frequency from the household record. On another, show the relevant external influences and evidence sources. Name a place where two needs may conflict. Keep uncertain information visibly uncertain.

**Check.** Must a small homestead contain all the zones to be a valid design? **Answer:** no. **Reason:** the tool helps organise real activities and care; the site and household determine what belongs there.

**Evidence for feedback.** The learner explains the difference between a care journey and an external influence, uses site evidence, and identifies a placement trade-off.

**App companion.** Design guide, Base and Sector stages. Adding an arrow is a way to record a claim, not a way to verify it.

## D2.2 — Read water, access and seasons together

**Question to show.** The most convenient route to a proposed garden crosses a place where water is reported during heavy rain. What needs checking before choosing that layout?

**Teaching text.** A design brings different observations together. Trace how people reach the places they use, where water is observed or reported, and where shade changes. Consider how the same arrangement might work in different conditions. Keep a useful access route in view while thinking about a new growing area.

The position of a water point does not tell you how much suitable water is reliably available. A tank drawn higher on a map is not necessarily higher on the ground. A flow arrow does not supply levels, soil information, a safe outlet or permission to redirect water. Those questions need appropriate evidence and, where relevant, qualified help. Keep drainage and construction proposals separate from what is already known.

A photograph of a green site does not prove year-round supply. Ask what changes during dry periods, rain, cold or household absence. Use local records and the existing seasonal-planning lessons to decide what else to observe. Do not copy a calendar or a fixed compass rule from another place without checking its relevance.

Some constraints change the scope of a proposal. If water, access or care is unresolved, improving a smaller existing area may be worth comparing with expansion. Deferring a feature can be a sound design decision. Explain the missing evidence and the next action so “later” does not mean forgotten.

**Worked example.** The busy-yard learner keeps the used route clear on every concept. They mark the eastern water report for further investigation rather than drawing a trench. They ask about the reliability and suitability of the existing supply before promising a larger garden.

**Learner task.** Choose one proposed use. Trace the person's journey, the water it would need and the evidence about its location. List what is known, what could change seasonally, and what must be checked before implementation. Refer to the water, soil and seasonal modules for the technical questions.

**Check.** Does drawing a swale settle whether it is suitable or safe to build? **Answer:** no. **Reason:** a drawing cannot replace terrain, soil, water, outlet and permission checks or relevant qualified assessment.

**Evidence for feedback.** The learner connects access, care and environmental information without inventing a design threshold or treating an unresolved technical proposal as ready to build.

**App companion.** Mapping, design and crop-planning guides. Keep a proposed water feature labelled as proposed; do not mistake software availability for site suitability.

## D2.3 — Turn observations into decisions worth testing

**Question to show.** “Put a new bed here” gives a location. “How can we grow food without blocking access or adding more care than we can manage?” gives a design question. Which helps compare options?

**Teaching text.** Analysis turns the brief and evidence into questions that can guide a design. For each important observation, explain why it matters to the household. Then identify a possible response and what would need to be true for that response to work.

Keep the chain visible: need, evidence, implication, possible response, missing check. If a step is missing, return to it. A learner may notice that a route is used frequently, but still need to ask who uses it and what they carry before changing its shape.

Use the ethics in a practical way. Who gains from a proposal? Who takes on more work? What existing life, soil, water or shared resource could be harmed? A benefit for one element may create a problem elsewhere. Do not call a system sustainable simply because it has many features or recycles something.

Write criteria before choosing your favourite drawing. Criteria come from the brief and site evidence: access remains usable, care is agreed, water needs can be met, and important unknowns are resolved or the affected work is deferred. A criterion needs evidence. “Looks natural” alone does not demonstrate that the household can maintain it.

**Worked example.** The question becomes: “Can the existing patch work better while keeping the route usable?” The learner also keeps relocation and deferring expansion available for comparison. BRIEF-A supports manageable care; WALK-A supports the access concern; RESOURCES-A prevents a claim that expansion is affordable. None of these facts yet chooses the final layout.

**Learner task.** Make a decision sheet with columns for the need, evidence reference, implication, possible responses and missing checks. Ask a partner to challenge a response: “What evidence would make you change your mind?” Keep the answer for the concept comparison in D3.

**Check.** Is choosing the design with the most components a fair test of quality? **Answer:** no. **Reason:** the components must answer the brief and work together within the site's and household's limits.

**Evidence for feedback.** The learner traces a decision back to evidence, names a trade-off and a reason to revise. The facilitator asks for a fresh explanation if the learner only repeats a principle's name.

**App companion.** Evidence and design guides. The decision sheet can stay on paper with an exported map. It is the explanation of the plan, not an automatic result produced by the app.
