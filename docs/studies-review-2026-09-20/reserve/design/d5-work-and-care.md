# D5 — Plan the work and ongoing care

Status: three English reserve drafts. These connect design reasoning to the separate finance course. No local price, work rate, construction specification or completion date is supplied as a recommendation.

## D5.1 — Decide what must happen first

**Question to show.** Plants have been bought, but the proposed area has no agreed care or reliable water arrangement. Which dependency was missed?

**Teaching text.** A phase is a manageable part of the work. Its place in the sequence depends on what must be ready first. Start with the design decision, then list its prerequisites: permission, measurements, advice, suitable materials, resources, access and the people who will care for it.

Distinguish investigating from implementing. Obtaining a quotation does not commit you to buying. Marking a proposed area for discussion does not authorise digging. A design may have useful information-gathering steps even while construction is on hold.

Choose a first phase that answers the household brief and fits the resources actually agreed. Keep access and existing useful systems working during the change. Describe what would make the phase ready to begin, what would stop it and how you will check its result. Do not begin every component simply because all of them appear in the final vision.

**Worked example.** The busy-yard first phase begins with checking care capacity, access and the existing patch's conditions. Buying animals is outside scope. A new drainage intervention stays on hold. Improving the design record is real progress even before a physical change is justified.

**Learner task.** Make an ordered list with an action, prerequisites, responsible person, readiness evidence and next review point. Ask a partner to find something that has been placed before its prerequisite. Correct the sequence and explain why.

**Check.** Is a proposed planting phase ready because the seedlings are available? **Answer:** not from that fact alone. **Reason:** the destination, suitable conditions, resources and continuing care also need to be ready.

**Evidence for feedback.** The learner identifies dependencies and a feasible first phase, rather than a calendar of unsupported promises.

## D5.2 — Connect the drawing to quantities and money

**Question to show.** The drawing includes a tank and fence, but the budget has only a plant purchase. Does the budget describe the plan?

**Teaching text.** Give proposed items consistent references across the plan and cost record. Derive quantities from a checked design or record their source. Keep unknown quantities unknown. Ask for suitable quotations when a purchase is being considered; do not turn a remembered price into a current supplier offer.

Include the work and supporting materials needed for the proposal, not only its most visible object. Separate one-time purchases from ongoing care costs. Record household contributions honestly. A donated material can still require transport, preparation or maintenance; a volunteer's time still needs agreement.

The date money is needed matters. A future sale does not pay today's bill unless funding is actually available. Use the finance lessons to keep budgets, dated cash forecasts, actual payments and amounts owed distinct. Do not count a hoped-for harvest as confirmed cash or a device-generated plan as proof of profitability.

**Worked example.** RESOURCES-A contains no prices, quantities or committed labour. The busy-yard learner creates an unknown-cost register with evidence needed for each proposed change. They cannot claim that concept A is affordable merely because it retains the old patch.

**Learner task.** Choose a proposed item and trace it from the plan to its quantity source, quotation or unknown, payment timing and care cost. State what the current record includes and excludes. Compare the first phase with available resources before committing to it.

**Check.** Should an unpriced item be entered as zero to make the total work? **Answer:** no. **Reason:** zero states a known value; missing information requires a separate unknown and a follow-up action.

**Evidence for feedback.** The plan and budget describe the same scope; the learner preserves exclusions, unknowns and cash timing. No purchase is needed to demonstrate the method.

## D5.3 — Design for care after installation

**Question to show.** A project has an installation team but no agreed person to look after it afterwards. What is incomplete?

**Teaching text.** A design continues after installation. Ask what must be checked, maintained, cleaned, repaired, harvested or recorded. Use the relevant technical lesson and local advice to establish the actual task. Do not assign a generic weekly schedule to living systems with different care needs.

Agree who will do each task and what they need. Ask about normal commitments and absences. A task is not allocated just because a name appears beside it; the person must understand and accept the responsibility. Plan how they will ask for help and what can be reduced if the workload is too much.

Identify relevant disruptions. Water interruptions, damaged access, a missing carer or unavailable materials may change what is feasible. Define a response that preserves welfare and safety. Where the answer depends on specialist advice, record that dependency instead of inventing an emergency procedure.

**Worked example.** The person carrying water in the busy-yard case helps review the care plan. The design does not assume they can take on extra work. If care capacity remains unknown, expansion stays conditional while the household tests its existing routine.

**Learner task.** Make a care sheet with task, reason, responsible person, required resources, evidence-based timing or trigger, backup arrangement and record. Discuss a disruption with the household or practice partner and revise the proposal if its care cannot be supported.

**Check.** Can an installation photograph show that ongoing care has been arranged? **Answer:** no. **Reason:** it records appearance at a moment; care requires responsibilities, resources and follow-up evidence.

**Evidence for feedback.** The learner distinguishes installation from maintenance, records agreement and shows how care capacity affects scope.

**App companion for D5.** Finance, expenses and crop-planning guides. Use classroom/sample records for practice and keep unknowns visible where an app field cannot represent them safely. The guide is not a substitute for the design's care plan.
