# Planned grazing and land recovery

Status: reserve English teaching draft. Not in the public course. The approach Rory described is commonly called planned rotational or adaptive grazing; cell grazing uses subdivided areas, while holistic planned grazing also places grazing decisions within wider household and land goals. These related names are not a guarantee of ecological recovery. A local veld specialist must review the field examples, forage assessment and monitoring method before release.

Prerequisites: Reading the Landscape, Soil Health and the shared animal-care module. Learners can complete planning and observation without purchasing stock or fencing. No universal stock density, grazing height, occupation period or recovery interval is prescribed.

## Lesson 1 — Define what you are trying to improve

**Teaching text.** Moving animals is an action. Better ground cover, less damaging runoff, healthy stock or a more reliable feed supply are outcomes. Name the outcome before choosing a grazing system. Keep the household's food, labour, money and animal-care needs in the same discussion.

Walk the land with the people who use it. Mark boundaries, shared rights, gates, water, steep ground, erosion, wet areas and valuable natural habitat. A map can help the conversation, but it does not grant permission to fence communal land or divert water. Agree who may make changes and who must be involved.

Healthy grassland is not an unfinished forest. Ask what vegetation belongs in this place and what damage you are trying to repair. More trees or more livestock are not automatic measures of restoration.

**Activity.** Write or record one observed problem, one desired change and one way to observe that change. Example: bare ground around a water point → less exposed ground → photographs from marked viewpoints plus a locally agreed ground-cover measure. The example is a monitoring design, not a promised result.

**Check.** A farm installs many small camps. Has it demonstrated regeneration? **Answer:** no. **Reason:** infrastructure shows an action; changes in land and animals still need evidence.

## Lesson 2 — Match animal demand to the feed available

**Teaching text.** Stocking rate describes the animals carried over an area and period. Stock density describes how concentrated they are in the area being used at a particular time. Putting the same herd into a smaller cell changes its density; it does not create more feed for the season.

Ask a trained adviser to assess veld condition and grazing capacity. Count the animals and distinguish their sizes and production needs. Record other grazing animals and shared access that affect the same feed supply. Check what is actually grazeable; paths, rocks, protected areas and unusable steep land do not all provide forage.

Make a fodder-flow plan: feed available through the year compared with animal needs. Use measured or locally assessed quantities and state units. Keep uncertain values marked as uncertain. A green photograph, map area or a neighbour's herd size cannot supply the missing assessment.

**Activity.** On the same farm map, draw the whole grazing area and the current occupied camp. Explain which changes when a herd moves. List the information still needed before recommending herd size.

**Check.** Does doubling the number of cells double carrying capacity? **Answer:** no. **Reason:** forage supply, condition, season and animal demand still set limits.

## Lesson 3 — Allow recovery and respond to conditions

**Teaching text.** A grazing plan separates use from recovery. It must leave plants able to recover and animals able to eat enough. Calendar days alone cannot show that recovery has happened. Growth changes with rain, temperature, plant type and past use.

Before moving stock into a camp, inspect the plants and soil. Is the planned feed actually present? Is the ground too wet or vulnerable? Is water available? Check stock during use and inspect the camp after leaving. With your adviser, define signs that mean “move”, “rest longer”, “use another area” or “reduce demand”.

If growth slows, do not force the same circuit faster just to keep the timetable. Review available feed, welfare and the drought plan. Supplementing, changing numbers or using an agreed alternative area may be necessary; each option has costs and practical limits.

**Demonstration.** Use three observation cards for the same camp: before use, after use and later recovery. Learners explain which observations would change the next move. The facilitator supplies a real local sequence; an animation must not invent a fixed recovery rate.

**Check.** The calendar says return tomorrow, but the plants have not recovered. What should happen? **Answer:** reassess and adjust the plan. **Reason:** following a date despite contrary observations can repeat damage.

## Lesson 4 — Protect vulnerable places and animal health

**Teaching text.** Repeated traffic can concentrate damage around gates, shade and water. Include these places in monitoring. Plan access so animals do not repeatedly break a stream bank or stand in drinking-water sources. Wetlands, erosion gullies and unstable slopes may need exclusion or specialist restoration, not heavier trampling.

Use safe fencing and handling suitable for the species and local conditions. Keep access to clean water and protection from severe weather. No restoration objective justifies leaving animals hungry or without water.

Mixed species may use forage differently, but they can also share diseases or parasites. Sheep and goats are not a parasite-free rotation for one another. Grazing plans and animal-health plans need to work together. Do not stop checks or treatment because a camp rested or poultry followed the herd.

**Activity.** Identify one pressure point on the map. Propose an action, who is responsible, and the observation that would show whether it helped. Check land rights, water permissions and access before proposing works.

**Check.** A wet bank is becoming bare and churned. Is increasing stock concentration automatically a repair? **Answer:** no. **Reason:** the cause and site must be assessed; more traffic can worsen damage.

## Lesson 5 — Monitor honestly and change the plan

**Teaching text.** Keep records that let you compare like with like: date, location, weather, animal group, time of use, ground observations and photographs from the same viewpoint. Use a simple repeatable local vegetation method taught by the adviser. Record animal condition and feed costs alongside land observations.

A photograph after rain may look greener even without a management improvement. Compare similar seasons where possible and record rainfall and other changes. Include places that did badly. One attractive before-and-after pair cannot establish a whole-farm trend or prove the cause.

Review the evidence with the people managing the land. Keep what helps, change what does not, and state what is still uncertain. Do not promise a fixed carbon gain, yield increase or restored landscape simply because the system has a regenerative name.

**Field assignment.** Submit a rights-aware map, goal and baseline, a feed-information checklist, one planned grazing/recovery decision and an observation record. Revisit the decision after conditions change. Spoken explanation and annotated drawings are acceptable.

**Pass criteria.** The learner distinguishes density from seasonal demand, identifies missing capacity information, gives recovery priority over a fixed calendar, protects vulnerable places and animal welfare, and changes a decision using evidence. No particular herd size or claimed restoration result is required. A failed plan with honest observations can support learning and a justified revision.

## Visual and animation treatment

Use the approved warm South African visual style with the same landscape and herd across scenes. Flow is well suited to a calm herd moving through one open gate, a farmer inspecting grass, or a repeated ground-level observation. Keep animal count, feet, fence geometry and terrain coherent. Do not generate a brown landscape turning lush within seconds as if that proves recovery.

Use a deterministic overhead diagram for the actual teaching: one occupied area, clearly distinguished resting areas, water/access and the next decision. Animate the movement only after the learner sees why it is proposed. Label all areas illustrative; do not infer a user's real stocking plan from a decorative grid. A separate timeline can show observation dates without specifying how many days every camp must rest.

A monitoring animation should reveal evidence gradually: fixed viewpoint → date/rain note → comparable second observation → decision. It must allow “insufficient evidence” as an outcome. Do not depict a carbon meter increasing unless it is tied to a valid measured dataset and method.

## Source record

- Rory's homestead handbook and curriculum outlines supply the integrated land-use and practical observation approach. Unsupported stocking, recovery, carbon and output promises are not copied.
- [ARC veld and pasture management](https://www.arc.agric.za/arc-iscw/CSA-Toolbox/Pages/CSA-Veld-Management.aspx): condition, stocking/capacity assessment and seasonal fodder planning. [ARC module 13](https://www.arc.agric.za/arc-iscw/CSA-Toolbox/Pages/assets/modules/13.pdf) supplies the local teaching scope for grazing systems, herding, condition and resting; site-specific numerical recommendations require specialist interpretation.
- [SANBI Savanna ecosystem guidelines](https://www.sanbi.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/2021_Ecosystem-Guidelines-for-the-Savanna-Biome.pdf): local plant-community capacity, professional assessment and adaptive monitoring. Do not transfer savanna advice unchanged to every biome.
- [SANBI Grassland Biome](https://pza.sanbi.org/vegetation/grassland-biome): grassland is valuable in its own right; frost, fire and grazing shape it. This lesson does not teach an unsupervised burn prescription.
- [FAO grassland monitoring](https://www.fao.org/4/x9137e/x9137e08.htm): forage supply, livestock demand, condition and trend need evidence. [FAO variability and system comparison](https://www.fao.org/4/X9137E/x9137e06.htm): avoid assuming rotation alone proves improvement or that a short observation separates management from rainfall.
- [UMN multi-species grazing](https://extension.umn.edu/agriculture/animals-and-livestock/livestock-operations/multi-species-grazing): sheep/goat parasite overlap; no foreign interval copied.

Publication gates: South African veld and livestock review, a real local monitoring example with permission, communal-land governance review where relevant, checked mathematical examples using actual units, fluent isiZulu/audio review and full motion inspection. Holistic planned grazing may become a deeper elective with an appropriately qualified facilitator; this introductory module does not claim certification in that system.
