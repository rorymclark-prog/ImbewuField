**Slide 1 — Water Harvesting**

Swales, berms, dams, rainwater tanks and greywater. Choose where to slow water, where it can soak in, and where excess water can leave safely.

---

**Slide 2 — Learning Outcomes**

Find a level contour line before planning a swale.

Explain why a dam needs a designed spillway.

Follow roof water through a first-flush diverter and into a covered tank.

Keep greywater away from people and edible plant parts.

**Slide 3 — Swales Slow Water on the Slope**

A swale is a level trench dug exactly on contour — not angled, perfectly level end to end — so water fills it evenly and soaks in rather than running off.

The excavated soil forms a berm on the downhill side.

Trees planted there draw on stored water long after the rain has stopped.

**Slide 6 — Storms Need a Safe Overflow**

A storm can bring more water than a swale can hold. Plan a stable overflow route before digging.

Trace it to a place that can receive the excess without erosion or harm. Do not simply send it towards a neighbour, road or building.

A second swale or dam is only an option if its capacity and overflow have also been checked.

**Slide 7 — Watch: The Overflow Point**

Watch the upper swale fill. Excess water then follows the protected overflow route.

The diagram shows a connection to another basin. It does not give construction sizes. The receiving basin also needs a safe way to deal with excess water.

---

**Slide 8 — Know When Swales Fit the Slope**

Slope alone does not tell you whether a swale is safe. Check soil depth, drainage, ground stability and what lies downhill.

Do not dig into steep, unstable or waterlogged ground on the strength of this diagram. Get a competent local practitioner to assess the site.

A small soil observation cannot prove that a large earthwork is safe.

---

**Slide 9 — Watch: Living Contour Barriers**

This side view shows established grass barriers across a slope. Flow slows at the barriers and some soil settles above them.

[pause]

Grass barriers and terraces need their own site assessment. They are not an automatic fix for every steep slope. First mark and observe; agree on a suitable design before disturbing the ground.

---

**Slide 10 — Store Rain for the Dry Season**

Stored water can help through dry periods. How long it lasts depends on rainfall, losses and how much you use.

Record when rain actually arrives on your site. A yearly rainfall total does not tell you how much water will be available in the driest month.

---

**Slide 11 — Design the Spillway Before the Wall**

A dam wall holds water back. A spillway gives excess water a planned way around it.

Have a competent dam designer assess the catchment, soil, flood flows and downstream consequences before any wall is built. Check the approvals needed locally.

A bigger catchment also brings a bigger flood. Never build first and work out the spillway later.

---

**Slide 12 — Watch: Dam and Spillway**

Look down on the dam. Inflow raises the water level. Excess leaves through the side spillway, clear of the earthen wall.

[pause]

This explains the flow route. It is not a plan for constructing a dam. The spillway and its outlet must be designed for the site.

---

**Slide 13 — Keep the Dam Wall Clear**

Keep trees and deep-rooted shrubs away from the dam wall, spillway and outlet. Their roots can damage these structures.

Maintain the protective grass cover and keep the spillway clear. Report erosion, cracks or unexpected seepage to a competent dam practitioner.

Do not dig out established roots from a dam wall yourself. Repairs need an assessed plan.

---

**Slide 14 — Your Roof Is a Harvesting Surface**

Roof area, rainfall and collection losses determine potential harvest. Tank overflow and water use reduce what remains available.

For a calculation exercise, assume a 100 square metre roof, 800 millimetres of rain and a collection factor of 0.9. Multiply these to get 72,000 litres.

Those are example inputs, not a rainfall forecast or a promise that a garden will have enough water.

---

**Slide 15 — Divert the Dirty First Flush**

Roof runoff can carry droppings, dust and other contamination. Divert the first runoff before it enters the tank.

The amount to divert depends on roof area and local conditions. Follow a suitable design and the device instructions; there is no single volume for every roof.

A first-flush diverter does not make water safe to drink.

---

**Slide 16 — Watch: First Flush to Tank**

The first runoff fills the separate chamber. The float rises and closes it. Later runoff travels to the tank.

[pause]

Later runoff is not necessarily safe drinking water. After the storm, drain and reset the diverter as its instructions require, away from edible crops.

---

**Slide 17 — Match Tank Size to the Dry Season**

List the uses you want the tank to supply. Measure or estimate their daily demand.

Compare that demand with local rainfall records, usable storage and the dry period you need to cover.

A tank size on its own cannot tell you how many weeks it will last.

---

**Slide 18 — Keep Stored Water Protected**

Keep the tank covered and screen openings against insects. Maintain the roof, gutters and diverter.

A filter alone does not guarantee safe drinking water. Drinking use needs suitable treatment, disinfection and water-quality checks with qualified advice.

Keep untreated water away from edible plant parts. Keep any alternative supply separate from drinking-water plumbing.

---

**Slide 19 — Greywater Is Washwater, Not Toilet Water**

Greywater is used water from washing. Toilet water is different and must never enter this garden system.

Washwater can still carry germs, salts and cleaning products. Reuse depends on its source, quality and where it will go. Do not assume that clear-looking water is safe.

---

**Slide 20 — Keep Greywater Safe for Soil**

Avoid water containing bleach, harsh cleaners or nappy waste. Do not reuse it when someone in the household is ill. Plain soap is not a guarantee of suitability.

Use suitable greywater promptly, within 24 hours. Stop if it ponds, smells or runs off.

Keep it below mulch, away from people and animals. Mulch does not disinfect water.

---

**Slide 21 — Watch: Greywater Under Mulch**

Used basin water travels through a pipe to soil beneath mulch, away from the tree trunk. It soaks into the root zone.

[pause]

There is no spray and no contact with fruit. The water stays grey in the drawing because mulch does not make it safe to drink.

---

**Slide 22 — Use Greywater Only Where It Is Safe**

Do not use greywater on leafy vegetables, root vegetables or seedlings.

A suitable system may supply established trees or non-edible plants while keeping water off edible parts. Get advice on water quality and stop if plants or soil show damage.

Check municipal requirements before fitting a permanent system. This lesson does not approve an installation.
