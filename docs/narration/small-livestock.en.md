**Slide 1 — Small Livestock Integration**

Small livestock integration turns chickens, ducks, and bees into working parts of the farm.

Learn how animals can control pests, build fertility, support pollination, and return value to the soil.

Use the system you already have: food, manure, flowers, grazing, and careful movement across the land.

---

**Slide 2 — Why This Matters**

Small livestock can provide food and useful work, but every animal also needs daily care.

Chickens scratch, ducks forage, and bees visit flowers. These activities may help the farm, but animals can damage crops and contaminate food-growing areas.

Plan feed, clean water, shelter, health care and safe separation from harvested food before adding animals.

---

**Slide 3 — Learning Outcomes**

By the end of this module, you will understand how chickens, ducks, bees, and guinea fowl fit into a working system.

You will know when chickens help a bed, and when their scratching can cause damage.

You will understand pollination, hive placement, and the value of closing nutrient loops.

---

**Slide 4 — Watch: A Chicken Tractor Moving Across a Bed**

Watch the chicken tractor move across an empty bed, with feed, water and shelter kept with the birds.

Scratching disturbs old material and manure stays behind. The bed is not immediately ready for food crops: agree a safe manure and planting plan before the next crop.

---

**Slide 5 — Chickens Turn Scratching Into Useful Work**

Chickens forage and scratch through suitable areas, eating some insects and weed seeds. This does not replace balanced feed or health care.

Manure and bedding can become compost material when handled and composted properly. Fresh manure can carry disease-causing organisms.

Keep birds, manure and dirty equipment away from harvested produce, packing surfaces and growing crops eaten raw. Wash hands after handling birds or manure.

---

**Slide 6 — Use Chickens at the Right Time**

Never put chickens around seedlings: scratching can uproot them.

An empty bed after harvest may be part of a rotation only when food safety and animal care are planned. Removing the birds does not remove fresh manure or its risks.

Ask a qualified local adviser about the manure-management and harvest interval required for the next crop. Keep animals out of areas where manure could contact edible produce.

---

**Slide 7 — Manage Ducks Around Growing Areas**

Ducks eat slugs and snails without the heavy scratching typical of chickens.

They can still trample plants, foul water and leave manure. An established understorey is not automatically safe for ducks.

Plan a suitable managed area with clean drinking water, shelter and adequate feed. Keep them away from produce and water used for food handling.

---

**Slide 8 — Rotate the Tractor Across the Plot**

Move a chicken tractor according to bird welfare, ground condition, weather and the farm’s manure plan. Inspect it daily.

Do not keep birds in mud, overcrowding, heat or exhausted ground while waiting for a calendar date. Keep feed, water, shade and predator protection available.

A fixed number of hens on a fixed plot cannot guarantee all its fertiliser needs. Use soil and crop evidence to judge fertility.

---

**Slide 9 — Watch: Bees Moving Between Hive and Crops**

Watch the bees leave the hive and move among flowering crops.

Their movement carries pollen between flowers across the site.

---

**Slide 10 — Bees Make Food Forests More Productive**

Bees and other flower visitors help pollinate many food crops. Dependence on insect visits differs between crops and cultivars.

In avocado, flowers have female and male phases. Suitable flowering overlap and pollen movement can support fruit set, but weather and tree condition matter too.

Observe flowers and visitors before assuming a hive alone will solve poor fruit set.

---

**Slide 11 — South Africa’s Native Honeybees**

South Africa has Cape and African honeybee subspecies. Their natural ranges and movement restrictions matter when sourcing colonies.

Get help from an experienced local beekeeper before keeping or moving bees. Do not move colonies between regions on the strength of this lesson.

Both bee health and safe management need attention; a native colony is not automatically free of pests or disease.

---

**Slide 12 — Place the Hive With Care**

Assess a possible hive site with an experienced local beekeeper before bringing bees onto the land.

Consider people, neighbours, livestock, paths, the flight path, water, forage, shelter and access for safe management. Morning sun may help, but one compass direction does not make a site safe.

Plan training and protective equipment as well as the hive position.

---

**Slide 13 — Strong Colonies Need Space and Flowers**

Plan suitable flowering resources through the year and observe colony health with a trained beekeeper. A busy colony does not prove that the site is chemically clean.

Beekeepers must register with South Africa’s Department of Agriculture. Check its current registration and colony-movement requirements before starting, even with one hive.

Repeated swarming needs an experienced inspection. Space is one possible factor, not a diagnosis; do not attempt a split from this lesson alone.

**Slide 14 — Watch: Nutrients Moving in a Closed Livestock Loop**

Watch nutrients move from plants to animals, then through manure and compost back to the growing bed.

---

**Slide 15 — Close the Nutrient Loop**

Use suitable farm-grown feed as part of a balanced diet, with clean water and daily care. Scraps and insects alone may not meet the animals’ needs.

Collect and manage manure so it does not contaminate food or water. Properly managed composting comes before using this material in the food-growing loop.

Record purchased feed, work and health costs as well as useful outputs. Integration can reduce waste; it does not automatically remove outside inputs.

---

**Slide 16 — Observe Livestock and Plan Parasite Care**

Guinea fowl forage for insects and may eat ticks, but their presence is not a dependable tick-control programme.

Protect livestock through regular observation and a veterinary parasite-management plan suited to the animals and site. Do not stop prescribed care because birds are present.

Also plan the flock’s feed, water, shelter, welfare and effects on neighbours.

---

**Slide 17 — Ask Three Questions for Every Animal**

For each animal, ask what it eats that you already have.

Ask what it produces that helps another part of the system.

Then ask what it needs that you can supply from within the system.

These questions show whether the animal closes a loop or creates another bought input.

---

**Slide 18 — Manage Parasites With Evidence**

Do not rely on chickens following goats to prevent worms or replace treatment.

Work with a veterinarian or trained livestock adviser on grazing, monitoring, testing where appropriate and targeted treatment. Rotation needs to fit the parasite, season and grazing conditions.

Watch animal condition and keep records. An animal becoming weak or unwell needs prompt qualified attention.

---

**Slide 19 — Field Assignment: Draw Your Farm Loop**

Walk your smallholding and choose an area where livestock could support another part of the system.

Record what food, scraps, pests, surplus produce, manure, or flowering resources are already present.

Then note what each animal could produce, and what it would need from the farm.

Identify one loop that could work with the resources you already have.

---

**Slide 20 — Field Action: Put One Link to Work**

Choose one planning action before adding or moving animals.

Review an empty-bed rotation with a safe manure and harvest plan, assess a possible hive site with an experienced beekeeper, or record livestock condition for a veterinary discussion.

Write down feed, water, shelter, daily care, useful outputs and any costs. A useful farm link must also meet the animal’s needs and protect food safety.
