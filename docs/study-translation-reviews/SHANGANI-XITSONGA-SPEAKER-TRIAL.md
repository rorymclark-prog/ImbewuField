# Shangani-speaking ACT group: written-language trial

**Status:** field-trial packet, 25 September 2026. This does not approve a
translation or restart Xitsonga publication. Ask an ACT educator who speaks the
group's variety to check the trial wording before showing it to participants.

## Why test the text

Research describes a close relationship between South African Tsonga and
Shangani varieties, while documenting differences in vocabulary and writing
conventions. That supports trying a standard written Xitsonga draft with this
specific group. It does **not** establish a comprehension percentage for ACT
participants or show that the current machine draft is safe to teach from.

Sources: [South African Department of Basic Education, *2023 Xitsonga Reading
Benchmark Study*](https://www.education.gov.za/Portals/0/Documents/Reports/ReadingBenchmarks22/Xitsonga%20Tshivenda%20Reports/Xitsonga%20EGR%20Benchmark%20Learning%20Brief%20%28English%29%20final.pdf)
and [Mabaso, *Lexicographical Practice and Lexicological Research: The Case of
Shangani in Zimbabwe*](https://lexikos.journals.ac.za/pub/article/download/1175/691).
The second source concerns Zimbabwean Shangani; the ACT group's language
preferences must be established locally.

## Candidate stimulus for a short trial

Use the first two sentences of the existing **machine-draft**, source-paired
`reading-landscape-l1` body in `lib/course-translation-drafts-ts.ts`. Do not
present the text as a reviewed lesson. A local fluent educator should first
confirm that the excerpt is a fair example of the written variety being tested.

> **English source:** Before you harvest water, learn where it already goes.
> Watch from a safe place during heavy rain.
>
> **Draft Xitsonga:** Loko u nga se hlengeleta mati, tiva laha ma tshamaka ma ya
> kona. Hlalela u ri endhawini leyi hlayisekeke loko ku na mpfula ya matimba.

The on-disk wording is authoritative if this packet and the draft ever diverge.
Pair the excerpt with the existing lesson image if it helps participants point
to water flow. Do not use the image to supply answers that the text fails to
communicate.

## Five-minute interview, one person at a time

1. **0:00–0:45 — Set context and ask permission.** Explain that the wording is
   being tested, not the person. Participation is optional. Ask what they call
   their home language or variety, whether they read standard Xitsonga, and
   whether they would rather read or listen. Do not infer language from place or
   ethnicity. Do not record a name or audio without separate consent.
2. **0:45–2:00 — Present the excerpt.** Show it silently first. Ask: “In your
   own words, what is this asking someone to do?” If reading is a barrier, a
   fluent educator may read the **same** text aloud; record that this was a
   listening trial. Do not explain the meaning yet.
3. **2:00–3:30 — Check the action and safety condition.** Ask: “Where would you
   watch during heavy rain?” and “What should you learn before planning to
   harvest water?” Invite the person to point to the scene. Record their words,
   including any unsafe interpretation, without leading them to the answer.
4. **3:30–4:30 — Find unfamiliar language.** Ask which words or lines were
   unclear, how they would say them locally, and whether a paired English term,
   a few changed words, or a different full wording would help. A fluent note
   taker should capture the exact proposed local phrase.
5. **4:30–5:00 — Ask preference and close.** Ask whether they would prefer this
   written form, a locally reviewed adaptation, or both together. Thank them.
   **After** recording the response, restate the English safety instruction:
   observe heavy rain only from a safe place.

## Record and decide

For each willing participant, record the excerpt version, date, read/listened
mode, self-described language variety, relevant reading exposure, their own
paraphrase, any safety misunderstanding, unfamiliar terms and preferred form.
Use **understood / partly understood / unclear** for each key instruction and
retain the evidence behind that judgement. Do not calculate a percentage from
a small convenience trial or claim it represents all Shangani speakers.

Include people with different reading experience where possible. Repeated
misunderstanding of the safe-place condition means **hold the draft** and ask
a local fluent educator to revise it before another trial. Repeated systematic
wording changes may justify a distinct locally reviewed Shangani adaptation;
isolated unfamiliar terms may be addressed with a small paired glossary. A
positive first trial supports further checking, not general approval.
