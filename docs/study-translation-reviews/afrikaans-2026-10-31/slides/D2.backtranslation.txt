0 | Soil health and compost
1 | Soil is more than just the earth under your feet.
2 | It is a living place that feeds crops, holds water and carries your harvest.
3 | In this module you learn to read soil, make safe compost, protect bare soil, and use cover crops and worm farms.
4 | Why it matters
5 | The condition of the soil affects roots, water and the harvest.
6 | Leaving the soil bare repeatedly, compaction and loss of organic matter can damage the soil. Colour alone does not tell the whole story.
7 | Observe your soil, and then choose ways to protect and improve it.
8 | Learning outcomes
9 | Look for several clues about the soil's condition.
10 | Use a jar to explore soil texture, and acknowledge its limitations.
11 | Tend a compost heap, protect bare soil, and know the difference between worm castings and liquid that drains out.
12 | Soil is alive
13 | Soil contains many kinds of living organisms. Bacteria and fungi help break down organic matter and recycle nutrients.
14 | Some fungi help roots take up nutrients. Worm channels can help water and air enter the soil.
15 | Look at roots, soil structure and water movement, as well as visible soil life.
16 | Watch: read your soil
17 | Look carefully at these two soil examples.
18 | Look first at the soil structure. Feel in the field how a moist piece of soil breaks apart.
19 | Look at the roots and the spaces around them.
20 | Look at the covering on the surface. Loose mulch covers one example.
21 | Use several clues together. Colour alone cannot tell you how healthy the soil is. Check moisture, plant growth and how water enters the soil.
22 | Explore soil texture with a jar
23 | Put soil and water in a transparent jar, with a little suitable dispersing detergent. Close it and shake it, and then let it stand undisturbed.
24 | Sand settles first. Silt settles after that, while clay can stay suspended in the water much longer.
25 | This is a rough learning exercise. Clods and clay that have not yet settled can mislead you; use a soil laboratory when accurate texture is needed.
26 | Read the jar carefully
27 | A thick sand layer under cloudy water does not yet tell you the final proportions. Some fine particles may still be suspended in the water.
28 | Compare the layers that have settled, and feel the soil in the field.
29 | Record what you see and what remains uncertain. Do not water or prescribe soil treatments on the basis of only one jar.
30 | Investigate before you treat
31 | Compaction, poor drainage and loss of organic matter can limit roots and soil life.
32 | Pale colour or few worms does not prove that chemicals have killed the soil. Worm activity also changes with moisture and season.
33 | Look for patterns across the whole field. Check the management history, drainage and plant growth before you choose a solution.
34 | Compost feeds the soil
35 | Compost is organic material that has been broken down under controlled conditions.
36 | Finished compost can improve soil structure and contribute nutrients.
37 | The time before compost is ready varies with materials, moisture, air and temperature. A province name or a fixed number of weeks is not a test of whether it is ready.
38 | Watch: build the compost heap
39 | Build the heap with dry brown material and fresh green material.
40 | Keep the layers moist, not wet, so that air and decomposers can do their work.
41 | Balance brown and green material
42 | Mix dry brown material with fresh green material. Avoid thick, wet layers that keep air out.
43 | If the heap becomes slimy or smells strongly of ammonia, add dry brown material and turn it.
44 | Check moisture and air as the heap changes; one recipe does not fit every mix of materials.
45 | Heat alone is not a safety test
46 | A hot middle does not prove that every part of a heap has been treated. Time, temperature and management all matter.
47 | Keep meat, dairy, diseased plants, pet manure and contaminated materials out of this simple household system.
48 | Do not assume that home composting destroys every weed seed or disease organism. Use a recognised process where sanitation is required.
49 | Keep seed pods and contaminated material out
50 | Keep wattle seed pods out of the compost heap. An ordinary heap may not ensure that every seed loses its germination ability.
51 | Use only clean, untreated materials. Bark breaks down slowly; its name alone is not proof that it is free of contamination.
52 | Check the heap and turn it when it needs more air or mixing. Keep it moist rather than soaked.
53 | Watch: bare soil and mulch
54 | Compare bare soil with soil under mulch.
55 | When rain falls on mulch instead of on bare soil, much of the raindrops' energy is lost. This diagram shows only that idea of impact on the surface; it does not predict how water will move on a particular site.
56 | Mulch protects the soil
57 | Cover bare soil with suitable clean mulch, such as straw, dry grass or wood chips.
58 | Mulch can reduce evaporation, soften the impact of rain and suppress weeds.
59 | Keep it away from trunks and stems. Check the moisture underneath and adjust the layer; more mulch is not always better.
60 | Cover crops between seasons
61 | Cover crops can protect soil between main crops. Choose them according to the local weather, available water and the next planting.
62 | The course's examples include oats, lupins, sunn hemp and cowpea. Check whether they are locally suitable before you sow.
63 | Legumes need suitable bacteria and growing conditions to fix nitrogen. Nutrients in their plant residues become available as the material decomposes.
64 | Worm castings and liquid that drains out
65 | Worm farms can turn suitable food waste and bedding material into worm castings. Check the bin rather than expecting a fixed harvest date.
66 | Liquid that drains naturally from a worm bin is called leachate. It is not the same as prepared worm-casting tea.
67 | Leachate can contain harmful organisms or substances. Do not use it on edible plants, and do not assume that dilution makes it safe.
68 | Protect the soil all year
69 | A field on the Highveld that is left bare after the maize harvest faces two main risks.
70 | Winter wind can blow away dry topsoil.
71 | The first heavy spring storm can strike bare soil and damage its surface and structure. If water runs over the field, it can wash away loosened soil.
72 | Cover crops, mulch and organic matter can help keep soil in place and keep it alive.
73 | Field assignment
74 | Investigate the soil in a work area. Record colour, structure, roots, moisture and any worm channels.
75 | Compare it with another place and record the recent weather and management.
76 | Try the jar exercise. Write down what it indicates, what is uncertain, and whether a laboratory test or a local adviser can help.
77 | Field action
78 | Start with one action from this module that builds soil.
79 | Cover bare soil with mulch, start a balanced compost heap, sow a suitable cover crop, or prepare a worm farm.
80 | If your compost smells of ammonia and feels slimy, add dry carbon material such as straw and turn it.

82 | Vegetables and staple crops
83 | Welcome. This is Vegetables and staple crops. It is one of the ten modules in the ImbewuField training.
84 | A productive garden is not the one that looks fullest on one day. It is the one that keeps bringing food to the household week after week.
85 | In the next few minutes we discuss four things. How to build a bed. How to keep the harvests coming. Which staple crops to grow. And what to do when pests show up.
86 | Why it matters
87 | Let us start with the problem.
88 | One big sowing gives you a surplus. Everything is ready at the same time. You eat what you can, you give a portion away, and the rest is wasted.
89 | Then comes the gap. Nothing is ready. The garden is full of plants, but there is no food in it.
90 | Small sowings rather create a rhythm. Planting, tending and harvesting overlap. There is always something getting ready.
91 | That rhythm is what feeds a household. Not the size of the garden.
92 | Learning outcomes
93 | By the end of this module you will be able to make four decisions on your own land.
94 | One. Build the right bed — match the width and the kind of bed to your soil and your rainfall.
95 | Two. Keep the harvests going with succession planting and intercropping.
96 | Three. Choose staple crops that protect you when one crop fails.
97 | Four. Read a pest problem before you treat it.
98 | These four belong together. A well-shaped bed still fails if everything is put in on one day. A diverse planting still struggles if you treat every yellow leaf as an insect problem.
99 | Roots need loose soil, paths need your feet
100 | Compacted soil loses its air spaces. Roots grow more slowly. Water soaks in differently. The bed becomes harder to work every season.
101 | The protection is simple. Permanent paths, and a bed narrow enough to reach into from both sides.
102 | One metre to one comma two metres wide. That's the working number. At that width you can reach the middle from either of the paths, and your feet never touch the growing area.
103 | Now think of your own beds. Can you reach the middle without stepping inside? Go try it before you plant anything else.
104 | Choose the bed for the soil and rainfall
105 | There is not one bed shape that is right everywhere.
106 | Start with the least disturbance that solves your problem.
107 | The no-dig method suits most garden soils. Leave the structure alone and build fertility on top.
108 | Do not dig wet clay. If compaction or poor drainage is serious, identify the cause with local advice before you choose deeper cultivation.
109 | Raised beds suit wet soil, where water must be able to drain somewhere.
110 | Sunken beds suit dry soil, where you want to catch and hold the rain you do get.
111 | Look after heavy rain. Where does water stay standing or does it run off? Combine that observation with soil and drainage advice before you choose the bed.
112 | Seed or seedling?
113 | Some crops tolerate it badly if their roots are disturbed. They do better if they are sown directly where they will grow. Beans, carrots and maize belong in that group.
114 | Others do better with a protected start in a nursery, and then transplanting. Tomatoes and brassicas (cabbage crops) belong there.
115 | Use spacing guidelines for the crop, variety and local conditions. Check the packet and local nurseries' advice. Watch whether plants stand too densely as they develop.
116 | Mark out the working shape
117 | Before you plant, mark out the bed.
118 | One comma two metres wide. Three metres long. One practice bed.
119 | Use pegs and string. Mark off the rectangle, and mark off both access paths.
120 | Then prepare for your own soil — no-dig first, and dig deeper only if your soil really needs it.
121 | A string line turns an idea into a decision. Once the paths exist, keep them. Once the growing area exists, protect it.
122 | That bed becomes easier to improve every season, because you have stopped walking on it.
123 | A sowing rhythm keeps food coming
124 | Succession planting is a calendar habit, not a special crop.
125 | Choose something that your household really eats regularly. Then sow a small amount of it, time after time.
126 | Plant a short row every two to three weeks.
127 | Less waste when there is a surplus. Fresh food for longer. And the labour spreads over the season instead of it all falling on you at once.
128 | Separate sowings can reduce the risk that you lose everything at once. They do not guarantee a harvest if difficult conditions continue.
129 | Which quick crop can you sow in small amounts? Decide on one, and start it this week.
130 | Sow little and often
131 | This is what it looks like in practice.
132 | Sow the first. Two to three weeks later, sow the second. Then sow the third. Then sow the fourth.
133 | With suitable timing for the crop, harvests can start to overlap. The first group will not always be ready by the fourth sowing.
134 | Two to three weeks is a starting rhythm, not a law. A leaf crop for the cool season can last longer. Heat can speed things up, or cause a failure.
135 | Watch what your own garden does, and adjust the intervals. That observation is the skill.
136 | Every crop earns its place
137 | Intercropping is not just pressing different plants together. Every plant needs a task, and enough space to do it.
138 | The Three Sisters is an example from indigenous farming traditions in the Americas.
139 | Maize gives height and structure.
140 | Beans climb up the maize, and are stored as protein.
141 | Pumpkin spreads over the ground, gives shade on the ground and holds moisture.
142 | Timing matters. Establish the maize first, so that it is strong enough to carry the beans when they start climbing.
143 | The plants can still compete with each other. Give them suitable space, water and light. Beans fix nitrogen with root bacteria, but do not assume they feed the maize immediately; nutrients in plant residues are released during decomposition.
144 | Plan back from your hunger gap
145 | A household can have a hunger gap: weeks when stored food runs low before the next harvest is ready.
146 | Yours may come after the stored maize is finished. It may come before the winter leafy vegetables are ready. It may come in a dry period when water limits the garden.
147 | Do not copy someone else's calendar. First name your own months.
148 | Write them down. Then choose the crop and the sowing date that put food into that gap.
149 | This is planning backwards, and it is the difference between a garden that looks productive and a household that eats.
150 | Staple crops are food insurance
151 | A staple crop earns its place because it feeds the household even after the day of the harvest.
152 | It carries energy or protein. It can be stored, or it stays in the ground until you need it. And often it also carries cultural memory.
153 | One staple crop leaves you vulnerable. Two or more give you choices when weather or pests strike.
154 | Grow at least two. Not one.
155 | Which staple crop does your household lean on hardest now? That is the one whose failure would hurt most — so that is the one that needs a companion.
156 | Different staple crops protect against different risks
157 | Each staple crop protects you against something different.
158 | Maize gives calories, and can be stored dry. Open-pollinated maize also lets you save your own seed, if you manage isolation and selection.
159 | Beans and cowpeas give a protein harvest that can be stored.
160 | Sweet potatoes develop some drought tolerance after their storage roots have formed. They need water in the first weeks and while the roots are forming; water stress at that time can reduce the harvest. Their young leaves are also edible.
161 | Amadumbe handles wetter soil, where other staple crops struggle.
162 | Note that they fail under different conditions. That is the whole point.
163 | Diversity keeps food coming
164 | Resilience does not mean that nothing fails.
165 | It means that one failure does not end your household's food plan.
166 | One crop is one point of failure.
167 | Two or more staple crops give you more ways to keep eating.
168 | Different crops use water, soil and seasons differently. That difference is the protection.
169 | Pests are messengers before they are enemies
170 | Pest pressure usually rises for a reason.
171 | Plants under stress. One crop dominating the ground. Or broad use of chemicals that has already removed the predators that helped you.
172 | So look at the whole system before you treat anything.
173 | Is the plant short of water? Is the soil compacted, or hungry? Are predators already working on the problem for you?
174 | A yellow leaf is not automatically an insect. It can be water, nutrition or root damage. Find out which one before you act.
175 | Treat the cause before the insect
176 | Work through four steps, in order.
177 | One. Observe. Look at the damage pattern, the underside of the leaf, the stem and the plants nearby.
178 | Two. Check for stress. Soil moisture, roots, spacing, nutrition, drainage.
179 | Three. Protect what helps you. Useful insects do work you would otherwise do yourself.
180 | Four. Only then act — and start with the lightest thing that works. Physical removal, barriers or changes in crop care can help. Check that the action fits the problem and monitor the result.
181 | If a treatment is needed, use a product that is registered for that crop and pest, and follow its label. This includes neem products. Check the instructions about protection and the waiting period before harvest. Do not improvise mixtures or use stronger doses.
182 | Be honest with yourself about which step you usually skip.
183 | Field assignment
184 | Now the lesson ends in the soil.
185 | Build one bed that can keep feeding you. One comma two metres by three metres.
186 | Reach the middle from both sides. Keep every foot on the paths. Space your plants for your own climate. Cover the bed with mulch.
187 | Check the bed regularly from when you plant. Use the photo after ten days as a checkpoint for the assignment, not as a reason to postpone care.
188 | Take a photo when it is planted. Then come back after ten days with what you observed.
189 | The goal is not a perfect photo. The goal is a bed whose shape, spacing and rhythm you chose deliberately.
190 | Field action
191 | Put one bed into production this week.
192 | One. Mark out the bed and the paths.
193 | Two. Plant, with your spacing and your sowing rhythm.
194 | Three. Come back after ten days, with a photo.
195 | Then observe. Adjust. And write it down.
196 | Record the sowing date. The rain. What germinated. Pest pressure. What you harvested.
197 | Season after season your garden becomes less dependent on guesswork — and more on what you actually saw happen on your own soil.
198 | Use your record together with reliable local advice when you make the next decision.
199 | Seeds and seed sovereignty
200 | Seed is where the next season begins.
201 | When your household can save, store and share good seed, it carries more than a crop forward. It also carries the freedom to choose.
202 | In this lesson you follow seed from the parent plant to the packet, the germination test and the hand of a neighbour.
203 | Think of the crops in your area. Of which crops do you already save seed? Which seed do you buy? Which seed do you swap?
204 | Try to name at least one crop whose seed is already saved in your area.
205 | Why seed saving matters
206 | Saving seed does not mean that you reject all bought seed. It means that you build up practical choices.
207 | Every crop you learn to save well removes one repeated purchase. It also starts a local record of what performs well in your soil and your season.
208 | Think of the seed you buy time after time. Which repeated seed purchase costs your household the most each year?
209 | Choose one crop from your planting list. Make it your practical starting point for seed saving. Do not try to save everything at the same time.
210 | Learning outcomes
211 | This lesson ends with a seed store that works, not with a list of facts.
212 | You will make four decisions.
213 | What will you save?
214 | How will you process it?
215 | How will you protect it?
216 | And how will you know that it is still alive before you rely on it?
217 | Which of these four decisions do you know least?
218 | Say them again in your own words: choose, process, protect and verify.
219 | Open-pollinated seed and F1 seed
220 | Open-pollinated seed is not magic.
221 | A stable variety can produce similar generations when it is properly isolated or pollinates itself.
222 | F1 seed usually stays alive. But the next generation splits into different combinations of the parents' traits.
223 | The problem is unpredictability, not that it does not germinate.
224 | Think of one small bed on your own piece of land. Why can unpredictable plants be a risk when space is limited?
225 | If you have seed packets, look whether they are marked as open-pollinated or F1. Which one would you choose to save reliable seed?
226 | Remember: the next F1 generation varies. It does not automatically die.
227 | Watch: open-pollinated seed and F1 seed
228 | Look at both sides.
229 | Both kinds of seed germinate.
230 | Open-pollinated seed produces similar plants when the variety is stable and pollination is properly managed.
231 | Seed saved from an F1 plant also germinates. But the next generation varies. Some plants may be tall. Others may be short. Their vigour and yield can differ.
232 | Ask yourself what this means when you choose seed to save.
233 | If you understand the difference, you can explain it in one sentence: both germinate, but the next F1 generation is unpredictable.
234 | Seed sovereignty
235 | Seed sovereignty does not live in a slogan.
236 | It lives in a dry packet, a written record and knowledge of how the seed behaved.
237 | Your household starts it. Neighbours extend it. The seasons make the local seed collection more useful.
238 | Which seed does your household already save best?
239 | Draw a small map of the houses near you. Write next to each house one crop that that household can bring to a seed swap.
240 | Sovereignty needs three things: usable seed, written information and exchange.
241 | Watch: seed network of households
242 | Well-saved seed becomes more powerful when it is known and shared.
243 | Watch how one household starts by saving seed of one crop well. Dry seed goes into clean packets. Some packets go to houses in the neighbourhood. The neighbours in return share other seed.
244 | By the end every household has more varieties than it had before.
245 | Think about which seed you can share and which seed you may receive from a neighbour. Seed moves. Knowledge and trust must move along with it.
246 | Choose several parent plants
247 | The biggest fruit is not always the best seed parent.
248 | Look at the whole plant.
249 | Was it healthy?
250 | Did it ripen at the right time?
251 | Was it true to type?
252 | Save seed from several good plants, especially with crops that cross-pollinate. This prevents the next generation from coming from only one parent.
253 | What would make you reject a seed plant, even if its fruits were big?
254 | Go to your own piece of land. Point out the plants you would choose and the plants you would reject. Choose several healthy, true-to-type plants. Reject diseased plants and those that are not true to type.
255 | Control pollination
256 | Some crops mostly pollinate themselves. A small garden can therefore keep the variety similar.
257 | Crops that are pollinated by wind or insects can cross over long distances.
258 | The distance that is needed depends on the crop, the place and the purpose of the seed. Do not promise variety purity before you have checked the crop.
259 | How can you separate two varieties without using distance?
260 | You can plant them at different times so that they do not flower at the same time. You can also control pollination by hand or with coverings.
261 | Remember the three tools: distance, flowering time or controlled pollination.
262 | Watch: self-pollination and crossing
263 | Follow the path of the pollen.
264 | With self-pollination pollen moves within one flower from the part that makes it to the part that receives it. This often helps the variety stay similar.
265 | In a maize field the wind carries pollen from the tassels to the silks. If two varieties are close to each other and flower at the same time, they can cross.
266 | Think about how distance or different flowering times can prevent this on your own piece of land.
267 | Dry and wet processing
268 | There are two broad ways to process seed.
269 | Dry seed ripens in a pod, seed head or maize cob. It is collected when it is fully ripe.
270 | Seed inside a fleshy fruit must first be separated from the fruit flesh.
271 | Short fermentation is especially useful for tomato seed. It is not a rule for every crop with wet seed.
272 | Think of the crops in your area. Which suit the dry method? Which suit the wet method?
273 | If you have seed or fruits nearby, sort the examples into these two kinds. Explain the difference by describing how the seed looks when it is harvested.
274 | Process dry seed
275 | Leave crops with dry seed on the plant until the seed is fully ripe.
276 | Collect it before weather, birds or pods that burst open take away the harvest.
277 | Remove chaff and damaged seed.
278 | Then spread the clean seed out in a single layer, in the shade where the air moves.
279 | What tells you that dry seed is ripe enough to collect?
280 | If you have a dry pod or seed head, handle it. Listen to the seed. Compare green, flexible plant material with brown, brittle material.
281 | Wait until it is ripe. Use shade and airflow for the final drying.
282 | Watch: dry processing
283 | Let the seed ripen on the plant before you collect it.
284 | Watch how it is collected once it is ripe. Chaff and debris are removed. Damaged seed is taken out.
285 | The clean seed is then spread out in a single layer, in the shade where the air moves.
286 | Follow the steps in order: let ripen, collect, clean, dry.
287 | Think about where you can do each part of this process.
288 | Wet processing for tomato seed
289 | Scoop tomato seed and flesh into a clean jar with a little water.
290 | Leave it open or loosely covered. Keep it out of direct sun.
291 | After two to three days a light film and a sour smell may appear.
292 | Rinse it off immediately. Remove debris. Then spread the clean seed thinly to dry.
293 | Do not seal a jar that is actively fermenting.
294 | Think of the signs that tell you the process has started. Think also of signs that would make you throw away the batch.
295 | Get a jar, a sieve and a drying board. When you are ready, start a small batch. Remember the order: scoop, ferment briefly, rinse and dry fully.
296 | Watch: wet processing for tomato seed
297 | Look at the whole process in order.
298 | A ripe tomato is opened. The seed and gel are scooped into a clean jar. Only a little water is added.
299 | The jar is left open or loosely covered for two to three days.
300 | When a light film and a sour smell appear, the contents are poured through a sieve. The seed is rinsed until it is clean.
301 | Finally the seed is spread out in a single layer in the shade until it is fully dry.
302 | Repeat the steps for yourself: scoop, ferment briefly, rinse, dry fully.

311 | Drying is not a decorative last step.

317 | Repeat this to test your understanding: dry first, seal later.
318 | Protect seed from heat, light and moisture
319 | Paper packets can breathe and keep seed organised. But they are not enough in a humid climate.
320 | Once the seed is fully dry, put the labelled packets in a sealed, moisture-proof container. Add a food-safe desiccant if you have one.
321 | Keep the container as cool and dark as possible in your house.
322 | Which room or cupboard in your house stays the coolest and driest?
323 | Compare a paper envelope on its own with an envelope inside a sealed container.
324 | Safe storage has two layers: a labelled paper packet inside a dry, sealed container.
325 | Label every packet
326 | Storage protects the seed physically. Written information protects its meaning.
327 | The packet must tell the next person which crop it is.
328 | It must give the variety or local name.
329 | It must say when the seed was saved and where it comes from.
330 | Which local names can get lost if you write only the crop's name?
331 | Take an empty packet. Practise writing the four pieces of information: crop, variety or local name, date and origin.
332 | Check again that all four are there.
333 | Germination test with ten seeds
334 | A seed packet can look perfect even when the seed is losing its vitality.
335 | Ten seeds make the calculation easy.
336 | Keep them warm and moist. Do not submerge them under water.
337 | Then count the normal seedlings.
338 | Six out of ten is about sixty percent. It tells you to sow more densely, use the seed only as a trial, or replace it.
339 | If eight out of ten seeds germinate, what percentage is that?
340 | The answer is eighty percent.
341 | Start a test with ten seeds from an old packet. Write down the date and the day on which you will count.
342 | Watch: germination test with ten seeds
343 | Count exactly ten seeds.
344 | Put them in two rows of five.
345 | Keep them on moist paper or cloth. Do not submerge them under water.
346 | Count again after a while.
347 | Here six germinated. Four did not. The total is still ten.
348 | The germination rate is therefore sixty percent.
349 | Test yourself. Did you count ten at the start, and at the end six seeds that germinated and four seeds that did not germinate?
350 | Share seed together with its information
351 | A seed swap is not a table full of unknown packets.
352 | A good swap lets information move together with the seed.
353 | It tells you what the seed is. Where it comes from. When it was saved. And whether it was tested.
354 | This honesty strengthens the whole network.
355 | What information will help you trust seed from another household?
356 | Take one of your packets. Practise describing it before you share it. Give the seed and its basic history together.
