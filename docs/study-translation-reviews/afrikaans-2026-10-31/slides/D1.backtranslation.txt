6 | A good design saves work even before you pick up a spade.

8 | Permaculture is a way to decide where things go, so that the land works with you instead of against you.

16 | Earth Care
17 | The first ethical principle is Earth Care.
18 | It means that you treat soil, water, plants and animals as living systems that you must protect, not as resources to use up.
19 | A system that you use up stops paying you. A system that you protect keeps paying you.
20 | Look at one patch of bare ground on your property. Is anything protecting it at the moment?
21 | People Care
22 | The second ethical principle is People Care.
23 | Your family's needs come first, then those of your community.
24 | A farmer who sells every egg and every vegetable, and keeps nothing back for the family's table, skips People Care.
25 | The farm is meant to feed the household that runs it.
26 | What comes off your land that your own family never eats?
27 | Fair Share
28 | The third ethical principle is Fair Share.
29 | Take only what you need, and give the surplus back to the system — seed, food, water, knowledge.
30 | A farmer who sells all his surplus maize and keeps nothing back for compost or for seed breaks this one. Nothing goes back.
31 | A community that fences off a shared spring breaks it too.
32 | What does your land get back from you each season?
33 | Look: One decision, three ethical principles
34 | A borehole supplies your household. Neighbours also ask for water.
35 | First find out whether sharing is permitted. Check whether the borehole can serve all the users without extracting too much water. If sharing is permitted and there is enough water, agree on how to share fairly and keep monitoring the water level. People Care and Fair Share guide the agreement. Earth Care means you protect the source. Only monitoring the water level does not make extra use safe or permitted.
36 | Who can help you check the rules and the water supply?
37 | When there is no rulebook
38 | This is what the ethical principles are actually for.
39 | A neighbour asks after a drought to let cattle graze. A flood damages your swales. Nobody has written down the answer.
40 | The ethical principles help you weigh the choices and explain your decision. Check the rules and ask permission where it is needed.
41 | Build these three into the way you think, before you build anything on the land.
42 | Twelve principles
43 | David Holmgren set out twelve design principles in Essence of Permaculture. Bill Mollison and David Holmgren together developed the permaculture concept.
44 | You don't need all twelve to start. Three useful starting points for this lesson are: observe and interact, catch and store energy, and use edges and value the marginal areas.
45 | We will look at these three one by one.
46 | Observe and interact
47 | Watch your land for a full season before you commit yourself to big earthworks.
48 | One storm shows you one moment. A full wet season shows you the pattern — where water runs, where it forms puddles, where it never reaches.
49 | This helps you understand the site. It does not on its own show that a swale is suitable or safe to build. Before you dig, check the soil, slope, drainage and a safe overflow route that will not cause erosion or send harmful water to neighbours. Ask a trained local adviser to assess the site.
50 | Where does water leave your land? Do you really know, or are you guessing?
51 | Catch and store energy
52 | Catch rain, sun and biomass before it leaves your property.
53 | Each of them arrives free and leaves free again. Water runs off, leaves blow away, sun falls on bare ground.
54 | Compare the cost and work with the benefit on your own farm.
55 | Name one thing that arrives free on your land and leaves again without being used.
56 | Use edges and value the marginal areas
57 | The edge is where two things meet — a hedge line or the strip along a path.
58 | These can be useful places to observe. Look at what already grows well along yours. That is the land telling you something.
59 | Which edge on your piece of land is doing nothing at the moment?
60 | Use and value diversity
61 | Use and value diversity. Hail damage to maize depends on the storm and the crop's growth stage. This principle does not promise that a crop will survive every event.
62 | Two others worth knowing: produce no waste, so that food scraps become compost and compost becomes soil. And use small and slow solutions — a bucket can irrigate a bed without any electricity at all.
63 | What would one bad day cost you now?
64 | Integrate rather than segregate
65 | Put each element where it works for its neighbours.
66 | A garden, fruit trees and a chicken run laid out so that the chickens rotate through the beds after the harvest is integration. Keep chickens away from crops harvested for food. Fresh manure can carry germs. Ask an agricultural extension officer how to manage the bed safely before edible crops return. The chickens can clean up pests and add fertility, instead of sitting idle in a fixed coop.
67 | The same three things, fenced off separately, only do their own job.
68 | Choose two or three principles that address your biggest problem and apply them thoroughly. The rest becomes clear by itself as you go on.
69 | Zones: Organise by how often you go there
70 | Zones run from 0 to 5, and they are about your feet, not about your fences.
71 | Zone 0 is the house. In this example Zone 1 is near the house and contains herbs and salad greens that are picked regularly.
72 | Zone 2 is the main garden and the chicken run, visited once or twice a day. Zone 3 is the main field, visited weekly.
73 | Zone 4 is semi-wild — fruit trees and fodder that need attention now and then. Zone 5 is left wild.
74 | Keep daily crops close
75 | Herbs you pick regularly are easier to tend near the house.
76 | Put them far away, and the long walk can mean you skip a visit. The herbs can be neglected.
77 | Think about the paths you walk every day.
78 | What do you visit regularly that could be closer?
79 | Zones plan your labour
80 | This is the point of the whole exercise.
81 | Zones help to organise space around your effort — the walking, the carrying and the checking that fill a real day.
82 | The more often something needs you, the closer it lives. This can add up over a season and save work.
83 | Stand at your kitchen door. What can you reach without thinking about it?
84 | Sectors: The energies that arrive from outside
85 | Zones come from inside your land. Sectors come from outside.
86 | Sun, wind, rain, flood and fire all come across your boundary, whether you plan for it or not.
87 | Look where strong wind on your farm comes from. Records from a nearby weather station can help you check the wind direction. Look where rainwater enters your land and flows over it.
88 | From which direction does the weather come that does you damage?
89 | Look: Shelter between wind and crops
90 | Look at this example. The wind comes from the northwest.
91 | The trees and shrubs stand between that wind and the crops.
92 | Some air goes through the windbreak. Other air moves over it or around its ends.
93 | The shelter can reduce the wind speed behind it. The design and care of the windbreak matter.
94 | On your own site, observe the harmful winds before you choose where to plant. This picture is not a planting plan.

110 | Reading the landscape
111 | A permaculture course for South African small farmers.
112 | Read the land before you change it. Find out where water moves, where sunlight falls, where wind passes through and where cold air settles.
113 | These observations guide practical choices for crops, trees, buildings, roads/paths and water. They are the beginning of good design.
114 | Why it matters
115 | Every farm has patterns that work before we add anything.
116 | Rain already follows lines over the land. The sun already warms some places more than others. Wind already finds gaps and ridges. Cold air already settles in hollows.
117 | Once you read these patterns, you place water, crops, trees and buildings where they can work with the land.

123 | Lesson 1: Where rain goes
124 | Before you catch water, learn where it already goes.
125 | Watch from a safe place during heavy rain. When it is safe afterwards, walk over your land. Look for rills (wash channels), places where water fans out, puddles and where water leaves your property.
126 | Some excess water needs a safe route away so that it doesn't cause damage.
127 | Look: Water slows, sinks in and runs away
128 | The picture shows rain running down the slope. Follow where it speeds up, spreads, sinks in, collects and leaves the land.
129 | Follow contours with an A-frame
130 | Build an A-frame level from three poles and a string with a weight on it.
131 | Walk with it over the land to find points at the same height. Connect these points to follow a contour line.
132 | An A-frame can help you mark points at the same height and follow a contour line. Its marks are an observation, not a design or approval for earthworks. Have the site assessed before you dig. Soil, slope, drainage, storm flow and a safe overflow route are all important. Ask a trained local adviser.
133 | Observe water before you build
134 | Water gains speed and erosive power as it runs down the slope.
135 | There is not one placement rule for every slope. Observe where water moves and collects.
136 | Poorly laid-out contours can worsen erosion, and soil that absorbs water slowly can hold too much water. Choose any water works according to the site and plan a safe route for excess water.
137 | Lesson 2: Read sun and shade
138 | In large parts of South Africa, especially in winter, the sun is in the north. Its path changes with the season and your location.
139 | North slopes often get more sun and can be warmer and drier. South slopes are often cooler and moister. Frost can collect in low hollows where cold air settles.
140 | Watch your own site before you choose where to place tender crops, trees or buildings.
141 | Look: Follow the sun across the site
142 | Follow the sun, the building, the tree and their shadows across the slope. Compare summer's high sun with winter's lower sun.
143 | Check winter shadows before you build
144 | The winter sun is lower and further north than the summer sun.
145 | A wall or shade net can shade a bed longer in winter than in summer. Check the real shadows before you build or permanently fix a shade structure.
146 | Stand at the spot on a winter's day at 8 a.m., noon and 4 p.m. Look where the shadow falls.
147 | Protect frost-sensitive plants
148 | Papaya and young citrus are sensitive to frost.
149 | Keep tender plants out of known low frost patches. Observe local frost before you plant.
150 | Lesson 3: Read wind, frost and slope
151 | Wind can damage crops on a smallholding.
152 | The direction and strength of harmful wind change with the region, the season and your site's ridges and gaps. Walk over the land on windy days. Record where harmful wind comes from and what it hits. Check local weather records before you place a windbreak.
153 | Wind, cold air and slope all matter when you choose places for crops and shelter.
154 | Look: See wind and cold air on the map
155 | A place that is sheltered from wind can still let cold air collect. Check wind shelter and frost risk separately.
156 | Cold air flows down the slope
157 | On a clear, windless night cold air can flow down the slope and collect in low places. These places can be colder than nearby slopes.
158 | Frost is ice that forms on a cold surface. Mist alone does not show that ice has formed, and frost damage can happen without visible ice.
159 | Look for ice and plant damage, compare low ground with slopes, and check minimum temperatures where you can. Mark places where cold or damage lasts longest. Keep sensitive plants away from the cold patches you observe.
160 | Compare possible places for a nursery through the local frost season. Check local records of minimum temperatures or ask a local agricultural adviser before you choose a permanent place.
161 | Choose airflow and warmth
162 | Put a nursery for frost-sensitive seedlings outside the cold patches you have observed. Compare possible places through the local frost season. Check local records of minimum temperatures or ask a local agricultural adviser before you choose a permanent place. Also check sun and harmful wind. No place on a hillside guarantees that it will stay free of frost.
163 | For tomatoes troubled by late blight, good airflow and morning sun can help leaves dry. Prolonged cool, damp weather can still favour the disease. Only moving a bed does not control late blight. Also get local advice on crop health.
164 | Lesson 4: Start your site map
165 | For a site map you need paper, a measuring tape, a compass and time to walk over your land.
166 | Walk along the boundary and make a first sketch. Mark it 'not to scale' until you have checked the distances. Mark north. Add the house, trees, water, roads/paths and fences.
167 | Then draw the patterns you have observed. Your map becomes the design skeleton for the whole smallholding.
168 | Look: Draw the land you already have
169 | Use the picture as a guide: boundary, buildings, paths, water, slopes and direction arrows. Draw what already exists before you plan changes.
170 | Let plants help you read land
171 | Look for places where frost lies longest and where the soil smells damp in dry months.
172 | Note existing vegetation. Mark where khakibos or blackjack grows densely.
173 | These plants can grow in disturbed places, but their presence alone does not show whether the soil is compacted. Check the soil before you decide what the patch means for your design.
174 | Add seasons, zones and sectors
175 | Mark summer and winter wind separately. They can come from different directions, so a windbreak or crop place that works in one season can be wrong in the other.
176 | Lay your zones and sectors over the same base map.
177 | Update the sketch season by season. A pencil map that you actually use is worth more than a perfect map drawn only once.

186 | Water harvesting
187 | Swales, banks/berms, dams, rainwater tanks and greywater — slow, spread and let every drop sink in.
188 | Learning outcomes
189 | Explain how a level swale can slow and spread runoff on a suitable site so that it can infiltrate.
190 | Explain why dams need a designed spillway and a site assessment.
191 | Explain what a first-flush diverter does and why tank water still needs a safety check.
192 | Keep greywater away from people, food and drinking-water pipes.
193 | Two designs for swales
194 | A level swale is one design to slow runoff. The swale follows a level contour so that water can spread and sink into suitable soil.
195 | Some swales are deliberately given a slight fall, with a designed safe outlet. Soil, slope, drainage and storm flow determine which approach is suitable. Ask a trained local adviser to assess the site before you dig.
196 | A swale can let water sink in
197 | This picture shows a level swale as a concept. Water can spread along the swale and sink in where the soil allows it.
198 | It does not show how deep moisture sinks into your soil. Check the soil, slope and overflow route before you build.
199 | The berm on the lower side
200 | The excavated soil forms a berm on the lower side. That is where trees can be planted when the site design is suitable.
201 | Trees can use moisture stored in nearby soil after rain; the result differs from site to site.
202 | Storms need a safe overflow
203 | Heavy rain can fill a swale faster than water sinks into the soil. Plan a safe overflow before you dig.
204 | The route must not let the slope erode or send harmful water to a neighbour. A swale or dam lower down must be able to receive it safely.
205 | Ask a trained local adviser to assess the soil, slope and storm flow. A picture is not a construction design.
206 | Assess the overflow before you dig
207 | A swale needs a planned overflow route for heavy rain. Check it before you dig.
208 | Water may only go to another swale or a dam when a site assessment shows that the route, outlet and receiver can handle the flow without damage.
209 | Check the site before you dig
210 | Slope alone does not tell you whether a swale is suitable. Soil, drainage, unstable ground and the water arriving from above are all important.
211 | Keep good ground cover. Get a local assessment before you dig on steep, wet or unstable ground. Grass barriers/strips and terraces also need a design that fits the site.
212 | Look: Roots help to hold soil
213 | Roots help to hold soil along a planted contour line.
214 | Choose erosion control with local advice; steep land needs a site assessment.
215 | Store rain for the dry season
216 | A dam or pond can store runoff, but how much water is available depends on local rain, the catchment area, losses and how much water you use.
217 | Rainy seasons differ across South Africa. Use local records and plan for dry periods; a full dam is not guaranteed.
218 | Before you change a watercourse or build storage works, check with the water authority what authorisation is needed.
219 | Design the spillway before the wall
220 | A dam needs a site investigation and a design by a suitably qualified person. Runoff from the catchment, soil, foundations, downstream risk and a safe spillway are all important.
221 | Do not assume that annual rainfall tells you how big a flood will be or how much water you will be able to store.
222 | Uncontrolled overflow can erode the wall and make it breach. Plan a safe route for excess water before construction.
223 | Dam and spillway: A concept
224 | Design the spillway before the wall — a wall that water runs over can fail catastrophically.
225 | Runoff from the catchment is one input. A suitably qualified person must assess the site, the dam and the spillway design before construction.
226 | Care for the dam and its walls
227 | Water can be lost through evaporation and seepage. Check the water level and look for leaks or erosion.
228 | Keep the spillway open and maintain the wall cover specified in the design. Do not plant trees on an earth dam wall.
229 | Animals can damage walls and add manure to the water. Their presence does not make the water clean or safe.
230 | Your roof is a catchment surface
231 | Your roof can catch rainwater. The amount depends on roof area, rainfall and losses.
232 | Check whether the roof material is suitable for catching rainwater before you connect a tank.
233 | Use the roof area as seen from above and local rainfall records. Then allow for water that misses the gutter, that is diverted or that overflows from a full tank.
234 | An annual total does not tell you how much water will be available during a dry period. Compare the supply with the uses you plan.
235 | Divert the dirty first flush
236 | Runoff from the roof can carry dust, droppings and other pollution. A first-flush diverter keeps part of the first runoff out of the tank.
237 | How much water must be diverted depends on the roof and the system. Use the supplier's instructions for size and maintenance; there is not one volume for every roof.
238 | A diverter does not make the remaining water safe to drink.
239 | First flush to tank: A concept
240 | A first-flush diverter keeps early runoff from the roof out of the tank.
241 | Choose the size for your roof and maintain it; later runoff still needs a safety check.
242 | Match tank size to water demand
243 | Tank size depends on water demand, rain, roof area and how long dry periods last.
244 | List the planned uses and estimate their water demand from your own records. Compare it with the supply through the seasons.
245 | Plan what you will do when the stored water runs low. A province name alone cannot tell you what tank size you need.
246 | Keep stored water protected
247 | Keep the tank closed, put mesh over openings against insects, and maintain the roof, gutters and diverter. Keep rainwater separate from drinking-water pipes.
248 | Water that looks clear can still contain germs or chemicals. Ask the local health authority about testing and treatment that suits the planned use.
249 | A basic filter alone is not a guarantee for drinking water. Water used on food crops also needs a safety assessment.
250 | Used water differs by source
251 | Used household water can contain germs, salts, cleaning agents and other substances. Guidelines do not define every source the same way.
252 | South African guidelines differ about kitchen water and laundry water. Do not include toilet water, water from nappies, or water used to wash a sick person or animals, in a reuse plan. Do not reuse water that contains harmful chemicals.
253 | Get local advice before any reuse
254 | Before any reuse, ask the municipality and a qualified local sanitation adviser to check the exact source, the household's water and sanitation services, the planned use and the site.
255 | If this advice is not available or not clear, do not reuse the water. Soil and mulch do not disinfect wastewater.
256 | A picture is not a farm design
257 | This general picture is not a tested or approved farm design. It does not prove that the source, route or destination is safe.
258 | Check with qualified local advisers before any reuse. Soil and mulch do not disinfect wastewater.
259 | Prevent contact and pollution
260 | Prevent contact with people or animals. Do not connect wash water to drinking-water plumbing, and do not spray it, let it form puddles or let it run off the property into a street, drain or watercourse.
261 | If a reuse system is already working and the water smells bad, forms puddles or damages plants, stop using it and get qualified local advice.
