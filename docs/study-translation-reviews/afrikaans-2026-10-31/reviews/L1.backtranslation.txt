2 | The three ethical principles: Earth Care, People Care, Fair Share
3 | Permaculture rests on three ethical principles. Earth Care means that you treat soil, water, plants and animals as living systems that you must protect, not as resources to use up. People Care means that your family's needs come first, then those of your community. Fair Share means that you take only what you need and give the surplus — seed, food, water, knowledge — back to the system.
4 | These are not abstract ideas. A farmer who sells every egg and every vegetable, but keeps back nothing for the family's table, skips People Care. A community that fences off a shared spring breaks Fair Share.
5 | Ethics matter because they help you decide when there is no rulebook — a neighbour who asks after a drought to let cattle graze, a flood that damages your swales. Build these three into the way you think, before you build anything on the land.

23 | Twelve principles: Design with nature
24 | David Holmgren set out twelve design principles in Essence of Permaculture. Bill Mollison and David Holmgren together developed the permaculture concept. Three useful starting points for this lesson are: observe and interact — watch your land for a full season before major earthworks; catch and store energy — notice rain, sun and biomass before it leaves your property; and use edges and value the marginal areas — a hedge line or a strip along a path can be a useful place to observe.
25 | Others worth knowing: produce no waste (food scraps become compost, compost becomes soil), use small and slow solutions (a bucket can water a bed without electricity), and use and value diversity. Hail damage to maize depends on the storm and the crop's growth stage.

44 | Zones and sectors: Organise your farm by energy
45 | Zones and sectors help you to reduce wasted labour. Zones run from 0 to 5, according to how often you go there. Zone 0 is the house. In this example Zone 1 is near the house and contains what you pick often — herbs, salad greens. Zone 2 is the main garden and chicken run, which is visited once or twice a day. Zone 3 is the main field, which is visited weekly. Zone 4 is semi-wild — fruit trees and fodder that need attention now and then. Zone 5 is left wild.
46 | Sectors are the energies that arrive from outside — sun, wind, rain, flood, fire. Look where strong wind comes from on your farm. Records from a nearby weather station can help you check the wind direction. Look where rainwater enters your land and flows over it. Draw arrows for what you observe.

67 | Understand water flow: Where rain goes on your land
68 | Before you catch water, learn where it already goes. Watch from a safe place during heavy rain. When it is safe afterwards, walk over your land. Look for erosion rills/runnels, places where water fans out, where it forms puddles and where it leaves your property. Some excess water needs a safe route away so that it does not cause damage.
69 | An A-frame level can help you mark points at the same height and follow a contour line. Its marks are an observation, not a design or approval for earthworks. Before you dig a swale, dam or other structure, have the site assessed. Soil, slope, drainage, storm flow and a safe overflow route are all important. Ask a trained local adviser.
70 | There is not one placement rule for every slope. Observe where water moves and collects. Poorly laid-out contours can worsen erosion, and soil that takes up water slowly can hold too much water. Choose any waterworks according to the site and plan a safe route for excess water.

88 | Sun angles, shade and aspect: Get the most out of sunlight
89 | In large parts of South Africa, especially in winter, the sun is in the north. Its path changes with the season and your location. North slopes often get more sun and can be warmer and drier. South slopes are often cooler and damper. Frost can collect in low hollows where cold air settles. Watch your own site before you choose where to plant tender crops or place buildings.
90 | The winter sun is lower and further north than the summer sun. A wall or shade net can shade a bed for longer in winter than in summer. Before you place anything permanent, stand on the spot on a winter day at 8 a.m., noon and 4 p.m. and look where the shadow falls.

109 | Wind, frost and topography: Read the invisible forces
110 | Wind can damage crops on a smallholding. The direction and strength of damaging wind change with the region, the season and your site's ridges and gaps. Walk over the land on windy days. Note where the wind comes from and what it affects. Check local weather records before you decide where shelter is needed.
111 | On a clear, windless night cold air can flow down the slope and collect in low places. These places can be colder than nearby slopes. Frost patterns also depend on the site. Compare possible places through the local frost season. Check local records of minimum temperatures where they are available. If records are not available, keep observing over cold nights and ask a local agricultural adviser before you choose a permanent place for tender seedlings.
112 | Frost is ice that forms on a cold surface. Mist/fog alone does not show that ice has formed, and frost damage can happen without visible ice. Look for ice and plant damage, compare low ground with slopes, and check minimum temperatures where you can. Mark places where cold or damage lasts longest. Keep sensitive plants away from the cold spots that you observe.
113 | For tomatoes troubled by late blight, airflow and morning sun can help leaves dry. Late blight can still spread during prolonged cool, damp weather. Just moving a bed will not control it; also get local advice on crop health.

131 | Make a simple site map: Your design begins on paper

154 | Swales and berms: Slow water down the slope
155 | One kind of swale is a level ditch on the contour. It slows and spreads runoff so that part of the water can sink into suitable soil. Other swales are designed with a slight, controlled fall to lead excess water slowly to a safe outlet. Which approach fits your land depends on the soil, slope, drainage and storm flow. Have a trained local adviser check the line, overflow and receiving point before you dig.
