## 1. Do not use a stream bank as a general productive-edge example

### Current learner locations

- `lib/course-modules.ts:103`, L2 body: “the fence line or stream bank is often your most productive spot.”
- `lib/course-transcripts.ts:485-488`, slide 12: “a fence line, a stream bank, the strip beside a path”, followed by “Edges are often the most productive ground you have”.
- `docs/narration/intro-permaculture.en.md:159-169`, English slide 12 narration, contains the same stream-bank example.
- There is no L2 quiz question or rationale about stream banks.

### What the sources support

South Africa’s National Water Act defines a watercourse to include its bed and banks and defines riparian habitat as land adjacent to a watercourse affected by stream processes. The Department of Water and Sanitation (DWS) lists altering the bed, banks, course or characteristics of a watercourse as a section 21(i) water use that requires the appropriate authorisation route. These sources support treating a real stream bank as a watercourse setting that needs local checks before an activity is recommended. They do **not** say that merely observing a bank needs a licence, and they do not establish that every stream bank is productive ground.

Sources: [National Water Act 36 of 1998, DWS PDF](https://www.dws.gov.za/IO/Docs/nwa.pdf); [DWS e-WULAAS, water-use authorisation process](https://www.dws.gov.za/ewulaas/WUA.aspx).

### Narrow English correction for review

- Body: replace only “the fence line or stream bank is often your most productive spot” with “a fence line or strip beside a path can be a useful place to observe.”
- Slide 12 narration: replace “a fence line, a stream bank, the strip beside a path” with “a fence line or the strip beside a path.” Keep the observation prompt; do not turn it into a recommendation to plant, clear, graze or take water at a stream bank.

**Still site-specific/practitioner-dependent:** whether a particular bank is a watercourse or riparian habitat, who may access it, and whether a proposed activity needs DWS or catchment-management authorisation. This wording gives no access or use permission.

## 2. Put a food-safety check between poultry and the next edible crop

### Current learner locations

- `lib/course-modules.ts:123-131`, L2 integration quiz: correct option says chickens “rotate through beds after harvest”; its rationale says they “clean up pests and add fertility.”
- `lib/course-transcripts.ts:496-500`, slide 14: chickens “rotate through the beds after harvest” and “clean up pests and add fertility.”
- `docs/narration/intro-permaculture.en.md:187-197`, English slide 14 narration, contains the same claim.
- The L2 body and key points contain no poultry-return claim.

### What the source supports

The South African Department of Agriculture hosts the Codex *Code of Hygienic Practice for Fresh Fruits and Vegetables*. It says pathogens may be present in manure and may persist for weeks or months where treatment is inadequate. It says fully composted animal waste should generally be used on production fields; untreated or partly treated material after plant emergence needs appropriate measures to reduce microbial contamination. It does not provide a single poultry-to-edible-crop interval for every farm.

Source: [Department of Agriculture-hosted Codex code, CXC 53-2003, section 3.2.1.2](https://www.nda.gov.za/images/Branches/AgricProducHealthFoodSafety/food-safety-and-quality-assurance/food-safety/audit-policy/cxc-codes-of-hygienic-practices-for-fresh-fruits-and-vegetables.pdf).

### Narrow English correction for review

- Slide 14 narration: after “after harvest”, add: “Keep chickens away from crops being harvested for food. Fresh manure can carry germs. Ask an extension adviser how to manage the bed safely before edible crops return.”
- Quiz correct option: change to “Garden, fruit trees and a chicken run arranged so chickens use an empty bed after harvest, then the farmer checks safe management before edible crops return.”
- Quiz rationale: retain the integration explanation, then add: “Fresh manure can carry germs, so check safe management before edible crops return.”

**Still site-specific/practitioner-dependent:** manure management, composting process, crop, soil, rainfall/runoff, harvest practice and the suitable interval. This proposal deliberately sets no number of days, weeks or months and does not treat post-harvest poultry access as a guarantee that the next crop is safe.

## 3. Remove the universal cost comparison

### Current learner locations

- `lib/course-transcripts.ts:478-482`, slide 11: “Catching them is almost always cheaper than buying the same thing later.”
- `docs/narration/intro-permaculture.en.md:145-155`, English slide 11 narration, contains the same sentence.
- The L2 body, key points and quiz contain no equivalent cost claim.

### What the source supports

FAO’s *Farm Business School Handbook* treats enterprise choices as depending on a farmer’s financial, labour and land resources and level of risk. It includes enterprise budgets, cash flow, risk planning and review. This supports asking a farmer to compare the local cost and work against the expected benefit. It does not support “almost always cheaper” for rainwater, sun or biomass capture across farms.

Source: [FAO, *Farm Business School Handbook*](https://www.fao.org/fileadmin/user_upload/nr/research-extension/FBSHandbook.pdf), especially its farm-management and enterprise-budget sections.

### Narrow English correction for review

- Slide 11 narration: replace “Catching them is almost always cheaper than buying the same thing later.” with: “Compare the cost and work with the benefit on your own farm.”

**Still site-specific/practitioner-dependent:** price, labour, materials, maintenance, yield or saved purchase, local weather and risk. The replacement makes no saving, payback or affordability promise.
