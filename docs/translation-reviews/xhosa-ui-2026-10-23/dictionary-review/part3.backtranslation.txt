128 | biomeWaterSavanna | Strong summer rain storms arrive quickly. Dig swales along the side of the slope (water-keeping swales) and small ponds before the rain starts in August (eyeThupha) or September (eyoMsintsi), so that water enters the soil instead of spilling away. Make tanks big: at least 50 litres per square metre of roof that pours water into them. Dig long and narrow dams — about three times longer than wide — on gentle slopes.
129 | biomeWaterGrassland | Summer brings short thunderstorms with heavy rain. On each slope, dig swales that go down slightly following the land (about a 1 m drop per every 1,000 m across) so that they spread the water instead of it rushing off. Winter is dry, so keeping water is important. As a rule: 1 square metre of roof collects about 1 litre of water for every 1 mm of rain.
130 | biomeWaterFynbos | Rain falls only in winter, so do digging work at the end of autumn (March (eyoKwindla) to May (Canzibe)), before the rain arrives. Small soil walls following contour lines and shallow (not deep) pits covered with mulch work well. Measure the size of your water tanks carefully — summer (December to February) is dry. Used household water (greywater) is fine for food-growing areas, but never use it on natural Fynbos — it has too many nutrients for it.
131 | biomeWaterSucculentKaroo | On slopes facing west, shade net can catch fog water. Build a small raised edge all around each plant to catch all the raindrops where they fall. Reusing household water (greywater) is suitable to do here. Every 100 mm of rain is important — even a small roof tank is valuable.
132 | biomeWaterNamaKaroo | Storms rarely occur and are unexpected, so catch water everywhere you can. Build small curved walls just above each plant. Catch water spilling on the farm paths and let it into shallow pits. Underground water tanks help you get through times of drought — try to keep every raindrop that falls.
133 | biomeWaterDesert | Shade net can catch fog water in the air. Design swales so that they safely catch floods that come suddenly. Keep water underground, where it stays cool and does not evaporate. Plant trees as wind barriers — they reduce water loss by evaporation and give shade before you plant crops.
134 | biomeWaterAlbanyThicket | Rain falls in winter and summer. Use shallow swales and soil walls that follow contour lines. A food forest based on Spekboom can resist drought and uses water wisely. Small tanks that can irrigate with gravity-driven drip work well. On clay soil, be careful that plants do not stand in water.
135 | biomeWaterIocb | Rain is heavy, so flood control is more important than collecting water. Use shallow swales to slow water on steep slopes. Raised beds stop plants from sitting in water. Swales following contour lines also help stop the soil collapsing on clay soil. Make pipes so that water from the roof goes into underground tanks.
136 | biomeWaterForest | The forest makes its own water cycle with fog and moisture released by trees when they breathe. Keeping the upper leaves of the trees undisturbed keeps the soil wet. Swales at the edge of the forest catch any water that spills. Springs inside the forest give reliable water all year.

140 | biomeSoilSavanna | The soil contains a lot of iron but few of the nutrients that plants need most (phosphorus and nitrogen). Cover the soil with mopane leaves, but only a thin layer — too much can stop other plants growing nearby. Plant acacia trees nearby; their roots add nitrogen to the soil. Moringa trees also draw nutrients from deep soil. Add compost and biochar (charcoal mixed with soil) until the soil has carbon of about 2% at least.
141 | biomeSoilGrassland | Grazing animals often press this soil until it becomes hard. Loosen it below the surface before digging swales or ponds. Plant oats or vetch as a winter cover crop to protect and feed the soil. The soil is usually slightly acidic (pH 5.5–6.5) — add agricultural lime if it is more acidic than that. Add rotting matter to the soil with compost and biochar.
142 | biomeSoilFynbos | Do not add compost, lime or phosphorus on natural Fynbos areas — it kills the plants, which are used to poor soil. Grow food separately, in soil brought in from somewhere else. Cover the food beds with Fynbos plant material. Cape reeds help hold the swale walls in a natural way.
143 | biomeSoilSucculentKaroo | Cover the soil with stones rather than using rotting mulch — it is cheap and works well here. Add compost only in the food-growing area. Plants that keep water in their leaves (succulents) need soil that drains water well, not soil that holds water. In food beds, add biochar and gypsum. Worm farms are a good way to make strong liquid fertiliser.
144 | biomeSoilNamaKaroo | A hard cement-like layer (calcrete) stays beneath the soil surface and may need to be broken with a special plough (chisel plough) so that roots grow downward. Add gypsum rather than lime — the soil is already alkaline. Biochar in the soil helps it hold water. Bitter Aloe grows well here and can be sold.
145 | biomeSoilDesert | Anything that rots here is valuable — use goat or camel dung. Plant shade trees first, before any food crops. In low places, plant only types that can resist salt. Raised sand beds with a wick (a strip of material that absorbs water and draws it upward) work well for vegetables.
146 | biomeSoilAlbanyThicket | Spekboom is the most important plant here: you can eat it, it stores a lot of carbon, and it can resist drought. To restore damaged soil, plant cut pieces of Spekboom directly — there is no need to first grow them in a nursery. Clay soil needs air that moves well, so use raised beds for vegetables.
147 | biomeSoilIocb | The soil is acidic and nutrients wash away quickly. Add lime to raise the pH to about 6.5. Cover the soil with mulch and plant cover crops to stop nutrients being washed away. A mix of biochar and compost (terra preta) greatly improves the amount of food you can grow. Coffee, avocado, banana and sugarcane all grow well here.
148 | biomeSoilForest | Forest soil is rich — disturb it as little as you can. Use small deep planting holes (keyhole beds) for vegetables rather than clearing large areas. Cover the soil with fallen leaves as mulch. Working with the structure of the forest itself (the food forest way) gives the best results. Leave most of the forest undisturbed.
