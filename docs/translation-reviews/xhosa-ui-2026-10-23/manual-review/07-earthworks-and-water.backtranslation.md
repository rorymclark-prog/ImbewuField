L1 Works of the land and water.
L3 Rain is a free harvest falling on every farm. On most of our land it runs straight downhill taking the topsoil (dark living soil) with it. One storm that fills the road with floods leaves the field dry after a week.
L5 Earthworks = changing the land: digging, moving and levelling it. By levelling land with pick/spade/machine we slow water, spread it and let it enter the ground. Earthworks are the bones of the farm; gardens, trees and animals depend on them.
L9-13 You will be able to: measure roof to see how much rain you can collect and how big a tank; build A-frame, mark contour with pegs, dig a swale at a suitable distance from one another; choose soil preparation that disturbs soil little; plan beds and choose irrigation that suits your water; use bath and shower water on trees safely.
L17 Water that runs off bare ground carries soil too. Water that stays in soil enters and feeds roots for weeks. Earthworks decide which of the two happens.
L19-26 Good earthworks: let rain enter instead of running downhill; refill underground water stored deep in the ground and rocks; stop erosion; irrigate trees without carrying buckets; lead water to crops; make homes for frogs, birds, insects.
L28 Earthworks can also do great damage. Water collected in one place and released suddenly can dig a big gully in one storm. So we design thinking of the biggest storm. Each earthwork has a safe overflow route so excess water leaves slowly without breaking the earth wall.
L30-37 Most-used: swales (ditch dug level across slope, dug soil on the lower side as a bank); infiltration basins (shallow pits with level bottom filled by runoff, letting it enter); ponds and dams (open storage, filled by runoff or tank overflow); diversion ditches and mounds (small ditches/low mounds turning water away from the road to swale, basin or pond); net-and-pan (small ditches leading runoff to planting basins); hugelkultur (mounds over buried big logs/branches that soak water like a sponge as they rot).
L39 Rain and slope decide which suits your land.
L43 Water sent by pipes from far can stop at any time. Water you collected yourself is there when you need it. So simple rule: collect rain as close as possible to where it falls, and keep it.
L45 We keep it in drums, tanks, ponds, dams. But the biggest store is the soil itself. Soil rich in organic matter, covered by plants with mulch, absorbs and holds rain much better than bare hard soil. Permaculture calls this soil a living sponge.
L47-58 Eight principles (Brad Lancaster): 1 Watch for a long time - walk land when raining, see where water runs and stands; build on what already works. 2 Start at the top and work downhill - above the slope water is small and slow, easy to catch; from there it can go down to where you need it. 3 Start small and simple - many small works you can build and fix yourself better than one big. 4 Slow, spread, sink it - don't let water run off the ground. 5 Plan an overflow and use it - lead excess water where it helps more. 6 Keep the ground covered with plants and mulch so the sponge grows each year. 7 Make each work do many jobs - swale bank can be a dry path; trees watered by a basin can bring shade to the house. 8 Keep watching and adjusting - each rainy season teaches something, which returns you to the first principle.
L62 Hard smooth roof gives lots of water. Zinc, IBR sheets and tiles give much more than thatch.
L64-68 1 Fit gutters at lower roof edges, sloping slightly toward the tank so water doesn't stand in them. 2 Put a leaf filter over gutter or tank inlet. 3 Fit a first-flush diverter before the tank: a pipe or container that catches the first and dirtiest water of each rain with dust, bird droppings, leaves. Throw it away (empty it) after each rain. 4 Put an overflow near top of tank and lead it to another tank, pond, swale or tree basin, not to the wall. 5 Join two or more tanks with a pipe near the base so they fill and release water together.
L70 Leaves reaching the tank rot and bring germs, so keep gutters and filters clean. Keep the whole tank closed with a strong lid with mesh so children, animals and mosquitoes cannot get in. Roof water is not clean enough if it is drunk as it is: first boil it.
L74-78 1 mm rain on 1 m² gives 1 litre. Litres = area × rain × runoff coefficient. Runoff coefficient = fraction of rain that runs off, 0 to 1. 0.8 means 80% runs off; the rest wets the roof, spills over the gutter or goes to the first-flush diverter.
L80 Measure the roof from above, including overhanging edges. Rain falls straight down, so a very steep roof does not catch more rain than one that measures the same over the same floor.
L82-89 Example 750 mm rainfall: roof 12×10 from above = 120 m²; rain falling on it in average year 120×750 = 90 000 L; zinc roof with good gutters 0.8–0.9, plan with 0.8; collectable 72 000 L/yr. New roof with perfect gutters can reach 0.95 (85 500 L) but plan with 0.8. A dry year gives much less.
L91 Same formula works anywhere, only coefficient changes. Runoff is higher on steep slopes, clay soil and big storms.
Table: zinc 0.8–0.9; tile 0.6–0.9; thatch about 0.2; concrete/tar 0.7–0.95; gravel road 0.5–0.7; bare hard ground 0.3–0.6; grass/veld 0.05–0.35.
L103 Covered ground lets most rain enter, which is what we want in planting land. Hard areas are where we collect.
L107-115 You use water all year, so tank needn't hold whole year's rain, just carry you through dry months. Example: 5 people × 25 L/day for drinking, cooking, washing = 125 L/day. Per year 45 625 L. 120 m² roof gives ~72 000, so roof is big enough. In summer-rainfall interior the dry season runs about from [month 'ngoCanzibe'] to [month 'iyoMsintsi']: about 150 days. (In Western Cape the dry season is summer.) Water needed to get through = 125×150 = 18 750 L. So the household needs about 20 000 L storage, e.g. two 10 000 L tanks, full when the rain stops.
L117 For a more accurate answer write down 12 months. Each month calculate what the roof gives (area × that month's rain × coefficient) and what you use, and keep a running tally of what's left in tank. The deepest shortfall in the dry season shows the storage you need.
L119 Garden water is extra, and a dry-season vegetable garden uses much more than a household. So we store water in soil too.
L123 Infiltration basin = shallow pit with level bottom that catches runoff and lets it enter (infiltration = water entering ground). Basins suit gentle slopes. They catch water from large and small roads, overflow from tanks and greywater (used water from bath and shower).
L125-128 1 Dig shallow pit with level bottom below where water comes from. 2 Give each basin an overflow into the next so the chain passes water down the slope, finally to swale or a safe open place. 3 Plant the edges with fruit, nut or native trees. 4 Cover the basin well with mulch; mulch keeps soil open so water enters quickly.
L130 Keep basins no deeper than about 50 cm, make sure water enters ground within about 12 hours. Roots need air, and mosquitoes need several days of standing water to breed. If basin stays wet long, make it shallower, add mulch or divide water among several basins.
L134 A pond turns one storm into weeks of water. Waters animals and plants, slows storm water, can grow fish, and attracts frogs, birds and dragonflies that eat harmful insects.
L136-142 Think before you dig: Soil – water leaks out through sand; pond holds water only in soil with enough clay, or with compacted clay lining or pond liner. Slope – the steeper the land, the harder to design and build pond/dam; a dam is a job for an experienced designer. Spillway – every pond and dam needs a wide overflow route, protected with stones or thick grass, so water never goes over the wall. Evaporation – in much of SA open water surface loses over 1 400 mm/yr, in much of dry interior 2 000 mm or more; often more than rainfall. At 5 mm/day a 20 m² pond loses 100 L/day. A deep pond with small surface shaded by trees loses least. People – slope the sides as you dig so no one falls, keep all people far from working machine. Fence the finished pond with a gate a child cannot open, and make one side slope gently so a person or animal that falls in can climb out. Where bilharzia is present, don't wash, swim, or stand barefoot in the pond water.
L146-155 Volume = length × width × average depth. For bowl-shaped pond average depth about half the deepest. 1 m³ = 1 000 L. Example: 5 m long, 4 m wide, 1.2 m deep in middle, average 0.6; volume 12 m³ = 12 000 L. To fill with one 40 mm rain from hard surface (coeff ~1) need 12 000÷40 = 300 m² roof/pavement. From grazed veld (coeff ~0.2) each m² gives 8 L so need 1 500 m². New pond needs more than this because dry surrounding soil drinks first.
L159-171 Sand dams: in dry land rivers are sand most of the year; under it water usually still flows. Sand dam holds it there, away from sun. Strong wall of stone masonry with steel or concrete, usually 1–5 m high, built across a seasonal sandy river. 1 When river flows heavy sand settles behind wall, light silt washes downstream. 2 Within one to four rains sand fills the area behind the wall. 3 Water held in gaps between sand grains; sand can hold up to about 40% of volume as water. 4 Most flow still goes over the wall; Excellent Development, a charity that built many sand dams in Africa, says 97–99%. 5 People draw water from a well dug beside the dam or via pipe in wall to tap/pump. Charity also reports a large sand dam can hold up to 40 million L, enough for over 1 000 people all year. Under sand very little evaporates, sand filters and keeps out animals and mosquitoes. Groundwater level rises around dam, trees, crops and pasture grow better. Sand dams only work on suitable site. Wall must stand on solid bedrock and go in at least 1.5 m beyond the flood edge on each bank so floods can't cut around. River must carry mainly coarse sand, not mostly silt, and must keep flowing as before. Experienced designer's job. In Lesotho, Bethel Business and Community Development Centre built one as example.
L175-177 Swale (ditch that keeps rainwater) = ditch dug exactly along contour (imaginary line across slope joining points of same height). Because ditch is level water doesn't run along it. Spreads and enters ground; trees below grow deep roots toward this stored water. Two parts: ditch and berm (low earth wall), bank of dug-up soil placed on the downhill side.
L181-186 Swales + ponds: 1 runoff fills swale, spreads along it to a pond at one end. 2 When pond is full water backs up and fills the swale. 3 Excess water leaves over spillway: short level section of berm that is the lowest of all, protected with stones or thick grass. Keep berm crest at least a hand's width higher than spillway so water never pours over the berm. 4 Spillway feeds next swale down, and next, until water reaches a lower pond or leaves the land. Water from such a system is slow and clear, carries very little soil. A diversion mound lower down built level across the driveway will divert its runoff to the swale too.
L190 A small garden swale can be two spade-blades deep and one wide. A field swale can be much bigger. Each swale must hold runoff from the land above it in a big storm and roof formula tells you how much.
L194-198 Example: swale about 0.5 m wide holds water 0.3 m deep below spillway. Each metre holds 0.5×0.3 = 0.15 m³ = 150 L. Plan for a 50 mm big storm. Land above is grazed veld coefficient about 0.3: each m² gives 50×0.3 = 15 L. Each metre of swale can take runoff from 150÷15 = 10 m²: strip 1 m wide and 10 m long running up the slope. So swales spaced about 10 m apart, measured down the slope. In a bigger storm spillways carry the rest.
L200 Where rain lighter or ground well covered, swales may be further apart. On bare ground, steeper slopes or wetter places, closer.
L204-207 Where not to dig: Steep slopes – keep swales on slopes under about 15% (fall of 15 m in 100 m). Wet soil on steep place can slide. Use terraces and trees instead. Heavy clay or waterlogged soil – if water doesn't enter, swale becomes a ditch with standing rotting water. Use pond or diversion ditch. Near buildings – keep swales and basins at least 3 m from walls and foundations, and never put them above a pit toilet, septic tank or soak-away. Very wet places – need ditches leading water away.
L211-216 Digging: first mark contour with pegs using A-frame. Then: 1 dig ditch beside pegs, keep its bottom level. 2 put soil and stones on lower side and press the berm down in layers with feet. 3 protect ends and spillway with stones or strong grass so water can't cut around. 4 plant and mulch the berm on the same day, before rain washes it.
L220-227 Planting: water-loving plants in ditch, useful trees in berm and below it, drier-loving on top. Berm: pioneer trees and shrubs that grow fast, such as native legume trees like Vachellia, to hold the bank and build soil fertility. Mulch plants on berm: comfrey, lemongrass, yarrow, nettle, non-seeding vetiver, cut often for mulch and compost. Berm becomes a mulch bank. Soil builders: clover, lucerne, sunn hemp; let dandelion and plantain grow and cut them for mulch. In ditch: cowpea or lupin as cover, water-loving plants like amadumbe (taro); keep taro in swales and garden beds away from streams and wetlands where it may spread. Below swale: lemons and other citrus, bananas where no frost, tomatoes; consider the shade they will make. Under trees: shade-tolerant groundcover like sweet potato, NZ spinach.
L231 Every time we turn soil we break tunnels, fungi and clumps that took years to form. So choose method that disturbs soil little. As the farm grows making its own mulch and compost you will need to disturb it less and less.
L237-239 Start the same way always: 1 remove bad spreading weeds like kikuyu (roots run over ground) and couch, which digging would multiply. 2 cut plants as close to ground as you can. 3 irrigate well or wait for rain, let soil drain until moist but not wet.
L243-247 By hand (small/medium): pick, spade, garden fork. 1 remove what you cut and use for compost or mulch. 2 loosen topsoil with fork. 3 rake away all rubbish and level the surface.
L251-253 Tractor: Repeated ploughing is one of main causes of soil erosion and soil carbon loss worldwide. Heavy machines compact soil, and ploughing every year makes a hard layer under the blade called plough pan. Ploughing gives short burst of fertility as air enters, then fertility drops each time. So if we use a tractor at all, mostly to start the farm. Rippers and chisel ploughs open hard ground without turning it, good for repairing damaged ground. Choose tools that turn soil little, pass over with as little as possible.
L255 Two-wheel tractor = walk-behind machine driving rotavator, small plough or mower. Costs much less to buy and run than big tractor. Use rotavator sparingly; often using it also damages soil structure.
L259-265 Animal tractor (moving chicken or pig house) uses animals to clear and prepare soil (see Ch 5). Chickens, ducks, sheep, goats and cattle eat it down; pigs also dig. 1 cut big plants to ground level. If you want pigs to dig, water area first. 2 Put a movable fence around area. Solar-powered electric fence works well. 3 Give animals shelter inside if they need, like a mobile chicken house. 4 Follow cattle or pigs with chickens. They spread dung and eat fly maggots in it. 5 Loosen soil with fork or chisel plough without turning, then let it level.
L269-271 No-dig imitates forest soil: leaves fall, rot and feed soil, becomes light like sponge without any digging. Earthworms and fungi do work. Buried weed seed stays asleep in darkness, saving hours of weeding. Price: many organic materials needed. Sheet mulching (lasagne beds) builds no-dig layers on ground. It shuts out grass so it dies and feeds soil life from above, and is fastest way to start small garden on hard bare ground.
L273-279 1 cut grass and shrubs leave where fall; water well. 2 spread animal manure 6–10 cm thick. Fresh manure goes only here at the bottom to wake soil life. 3 cover with cardboard or newspaper overlapped so no light enters; wet it. 4 add 10–15 cm dry grass or dry mulch, then layer of compost. 5 another 10–15 cm seedless dry grass. 6 finish with compost or good topsoil. What you plant into must be well-made compost or well-rotted manure, never fresh manure. 7 Water, open small holes in upper layers, plant seedlings in compost, mulch around.
L283-287 Solarisation cooks weeds with sun. Water soil, cover tightly with clear plastic, bury edges, leave at least 4–6 weeks in hottest weather. Also kills other useful soil life, so use only where needed. Tarping uses black plastic: warms soil little and works by blocking light. Cover cut and watered area, hold sheet with stones or sandbags, leave 6–10 weeks. Then loosen topsoil by rocking the fork back and forth, without turning. Market gardeners use tarps for stale seedbed: prepare and water bed, cover, let weed seed sprout and die in darkness, remove tarp after few weeks and sow into clean bed.
L291-295 Bed = place where water, soil and your back meet. Do it well and soil improves each year. These are guides not rules. Raised beds suit medium–high rain. Very dry places: sunken beds lower than path to catch rain like basins. If slope steeper than about 3% (fall 3 m in 100 m) lay beds and paths along contour so heavy rain can't run down them. On flat ground free to make circles, spirals, mandalas.
L299-313 Double-reach bed: thin enough to reach middle from paths on both sides so you don't stand on it compacting. Beds 80–120 cm wide; commercial gardens often 75–90 cm, suits hand tools and two-wheel tractors. Paths 40–60 cm. Beds about 8 m long in small garden up to 30 m commercial. Raised beds lift roots out of waterlogged soil, make topsoil deep with soil from paths, and compost ends where plants grow. Soil never stepped on and not so much dug improves each year until garden fork goes in to the handle. Steps: 1 mark top contour with A-frame; if room put small swale above top bed. 2 measure other beds and paths down slope, mark edges with lime or soot. Put a garden hose along curved edges. 3 starting at top dig soil from path above each bed and throw it to bed below. Raise normal bed 10–15 cm. 4 level each bed with rake, neat edges. 5 add lime or gypsum if soil test shows needed, with about 3 cm compost. 6 open bed with fork without turning, cover with thick layer and water. 7 let bed rest few weeks before planting.
L315 Small gardens: border beds with stones, strong branches or pallet planks. Put cardboard over inner grass, fill with good topsoil, finish with compost. Works on concrete too.
L319-327 Trench bed = deep bed filled with organic things. Turns stony or clay soil into planting soil and holds water in dry places. Hard to dig but lasts years. 1 mark bed about 1 m wide, 2–5 m long, dig about 60 cm deep. 2 dark topsoil one side, light subsoil other. 3 sticks/coarse stuff at bottom. 4 fill in layers: ~20 cm organic (dry leaves, grass, kitchen scraps, manure), then ~10 cm subsoil; water each layer. 5 finish with topsoil, then mulch. Bed sinks as it rots; top up with compost each time.
L331 Terraces: on very steep land a terrace is a level step cut in slope. Its front bank works like a swale berm, holds water and soil. Build walls with stones, big logs or old tyres. Where wattle has invaded, cut woven stems make barriers that hold soil well; use only dry stems without seed pods so seed isn't spread.
L335-343 Pit bed = round hole about 50 cm deep (up to 1 m), 1–2 m wide filled with organic matter. Plants grow around edge fed by rot and water in middle. Banana circle is well-known kind. 1 choose where water arrives: below a tap, near where water seeps from ground, or where greywater or runoff flows. 2 lift grass pieces and soil with topsoil, set aside. 3 dig small ditch to lead water to middle, then dig the hole and fill with organic matter. 4 return soil as low ring around edge, plant and mulch well. 5 keep feeding middle with organic matter. Bananas, taro, sweet potato, ginger and papaya do well in frost-free places. You can put a perforated wooden floor over the hole and shower there.
L347 Keyhole and mandala beds suit flat ground. From one place you reach most of bed, so put plants you harvest often near the path. Put tall plants on the south side, because the sun is in the north.
L351 Every drop you carry or pump costs you something. Better irrigation puts water at roots only, not faster than soil can absorb. Water very early morning or in evening, mulch everything you water.
L355 By hand: hose or can throws water onto paths and waters unevenly. Instead make small basin or ditch beside each plant or row, fill, let soak in, continue. For seedlings use can with rose; water that sat in sun is better than cold borehole water.
L359-362 Bottle: cut base off old plastic bottle; small holes in lid; bury bottle neck-down beside plant, open mouth just above soil; fill. Water enters slowly to roots.
L366-372 Drip lets water drip slowly into soil at roots of each plant. Paths stay dry, fewer weeds, little water lost: about 90% reaches roots vs 75–85% for sprinklers. Drip tube 16 or 20 mm with built-in drippers 20–30 cm apart under mulch. Lines 30–50 cm apart; closer in sand where water spreads little sideways. 1 m bed needs 2 lines, 1.5 m bed 3. Always fit filter because dirt blocks drippers, and flush line ends periodically. Pressure from tank: each 10 m height between tank water and tap gives about 1 bar. Tank on 1 m stand gives only 0.1 bar, enough for gravity-fed low-pressure drip kit but not ordinary drippers or sprinklers. Read the pressure your kit needs on the package.
L376-378 Micro-sprinklers suit zones 1 and 2, beds close to house you visit daily. Each head on a stake, fed by thin tube from low-pressure pipe, covers circle a few metres across. Lose more water to wind than drip. Impact sprinklers (click as they turn) throw water over wide circle. Need more pressure, lose much water to wind and sun, wet leaves which spreads leaf diseases. Keep for pasture and big fields.
L382-384 Every bath and shower sends good water out through a pipe. On a dry farm this water can keep a line of fruit trees alive in dry season. Greywater = used water from bath, shower, hand basins and washing machine. Blackwater = from toilets. Blackwater and nappy water carry germs and must not go into garden. Kitchen sink water is dirtiest greywater, full of fat and food waste; use only after passing through a grease trap, only on trees and flowers.
Table: shower/bath/basin: yes, carefully. Washing machine: yes with gentle soap with little salt. Kitchen sink: only via grease trap, trees and flowers. Washing nappies or toilet: No.
L395-401 Rules: 1 In the soil under mulch. Lead greywater to a covered tree basin, swale or pit bed with pipe ending under mulch. Never spray it. 2 Never on food eaten raw. Keep away from leafy greens (morogo), lettuce and roots like carrots. Use on fruit trees, bananas, shrubs and flowers. 3 No puddles. It must enter soil quickly where people and animals won't touch it. 4 Use it the same day. Stored greywater goes bad and breeds germs and mosquitoes. 5 Away from drinking water. Never near or above a well, borehole or spring. 6 Gentle soaps. Salts, bleach, borax and heavy chemicals accumulate and damage soil and plants. Use gentle biodegradable soaps with little salt; liquid laundry soap usually has less salt than powder. 7 Move it. Rotate between basins and let rain flush the soil. Yellow leaf edges or hard white layer mean salt accumulating.
L405-410 Simple system: 1 find where drain pipe from bath, shower, basin leaves house, usually beside bathroom wall. 2 connect a PVC pipe to outlet and lead to garden, sloping slightly down all the way. 3 fit flexible hose at end so you can move outlet between covered basins. 4 keep outlet buried in mulch. When greywater has entered healthy covered soil, soil life and roots break down most of what it carries.
L414-423 Try this: A-frame, costs nothing apart from three sticks and one afternoon. 1 tie tops of two straight sticks ~2 m long until exactly equal, inverted V. 2 tie short stick between the legs, same height both sides, make an A. 3 hang stone on string from top so it hangs freely past middle stick. 4 stand frame on slope, mark where string crosses stick. Turn frame so legs swap places on same two spots, mark again. Level mark halfway between. 5 drive peg where you want line to start, stand one leg beside it. 6 move other leg raising or lowering up/down the slope until string hangs at level mark. Drive peg there. 7 keep that leg still, swing the first over to other side, find level again, drive peg. Continue across slope. 8 walk along line, smooth very twisty parts. Put stones or branches beside it and watch what the next rain does there.
L427-431 Key points: design for the biggest storm: start at top, give every earthwork a safe overflow. Rain collected = area × rain × coefficient; 120 m² zinc roof at 750 mm gives ~72 000 L/yr. Size the tank for the dry season not the whole year's rain. Swales follow contour exactly; space so each holds big storm. Disturb soil as little as possible, irrigate at roots, send greywater under mulch to trees.
L433 Walk your land in the next heavy rain and watch where the water goes. It already shows where the first swale should be.
