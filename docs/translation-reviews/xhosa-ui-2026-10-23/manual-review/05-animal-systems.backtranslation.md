# Animal systems
Animals close the circle. A chicken turns kitchen peels into eggs and manure. A pig turns fallen fruit into meat and a clean bed. A bee turns the flowers at the edge of your fence into honey and a heavy pumpkin harvest.
Kept badly, animals become work and worry. Kept well, they do much of the work without you. The difference is design: where the animal lives, what it eats, and which of the things it does naturally you give it as a job.
By the end you will be able to: learn an animal's needs, products and behaviours, and put it where it does useful work; build and move an animal tractor for chickens or pigs; feed, house and protect a small flock of chickens, and keep Newcastle disease out; keep pigs and ducks where they help the farm and cannot ruin it; site a beehive safely and make your land a good home for bees.

## Put animals where they work
A goat eats the leaves of trees and shrubs. A pig digs. A chicken scratches and eats every grub it finds. Left to do these alone, these ways do damage. Designed to work, they pay you back many days of work.
Well-placed animals give manure, dug-up soil, short grass, fewer insects, plus meat, eggs, milk, wool and honey. Oxen and donkeys pull the plough, and dogs, even geese, warn you when strangers arrive.
To place an animal in the right place, look at three things:
1. Needs: what it must have. Food, water, shelter, space, company.
2. Products: what it gives. Eggs, meat, manure, feathers, heat.
3. Behaviours: what it does naturally. Scratching, digging, grazing, hunting insects.
Then connect each of these with another part of the farm. A chicken needs food and likes to scratch. A bed after harvest needs cleaning of weeds and insects. Put the two together and both needs are met.
As the weather becomes less predictable, choose tough breeds that can withstand heat, cold and drought.

## Animal tractors
An animal tractor is a moving pen: the animals work one piece of land, then you move the pen to the next. Chickens, ducks, geese, pigs and goats all work this way. They deposit manure, scratch, graze and dig as they always do, but now it happens where you want: on a garden bed between crops, under fruit trees, in an exhausted pasture, on ground covered with grass and weeds, or at the bottom of a new dam, where pigs dig and compact it.
A closed animal tractor is a wooden or post frame covered with wire netting, with a roof on one side for shade and rain protection, and light enough for two people to drag. An open animal tractor is a circle of movable electric netting, run by a small solar energiser.
1. Place the animal tractor on the piece you want to work.
2. Leave the animals until the soil is scratched and manured, but not bare and hard from trampling. For a few chickens on a garden bed, that usually takes a few days.
3. Move the animal tractor to the next piece.
4. Plant the used piece, or let its grass return.
Follow pigs, goats or cattle with chickens: they spread manure, scratch it into the ground and eat fly larvae.

## Chickens
Chickens are usually a family's first animals. They are small, cheap and light on the land, and they give eggs, meat, feathers, insect control, cleaned ground and some of the richest manure on a farm. They should be in Zones 1 and 2, the places near the house that you visit every day.
### Feeding chickens
Chickens eat everything (omnivores): they eat plants and animals. A laying hen needs:
- Protein, from insects, earthworms, cooked beans and pigeon-pea seed
- Energy, from grains like maize, sorghum and wheat
- Green food, from grass, weeds and garden waste
- Calcium for hard shells, from crushed oyster shell, small limestone chips or dried crushed eggshells
- Grit, small hard stones or coarse sand that she keeps in her grinding stomach (gizzard) to grind food
- Vitamins, especially A from green leaves and yellow maize, and D from sun
- Clean water at all times
A laying hen eats about 110 to 140 g of food a day and drinks water about double the weight of the food, especially when hot.
Bought layer feed is balanced but costly. Most South African maize, with nearly all local soya, is grown from genetically modified (GM) seed, and bought feed usually contains both, so some permaculture farmers choose to grow their own feed. Home-grown feed must still be balanced: hens fed only grain lay few eggs. If laying drops, add protein or bought layer feed.
Cheap food is available all over the farm: kitchen leftovers and old bread; slugs, snails and grubs; leaves with aphids; [?"acokisiweyo"] leaves full of insects and seeds; outside the compost heap (rotted plant material that feeds the soil), where scraps rot; cattle dung full of larvae; and sunflower heads hung up ripe for birds to eat.
> Tip: Dry and crush eggshells before putting them back in the feed. Hens that see a whole shell may start eating their own eggs.
Give them a safe place of dry, soft soil, sand or wood ash for a dust bath. Rolling in it keeps their feathers clean.
### House, water and predators
Snakes, mongooses, genets, wild cats, dogs, rats and birds of prey all take chickens. In villages a good house is the most important part of protection.
1. Space. Allow 3 to 4 chickens per square metre of floor. Crowded chickens fight, get sick and lay less.
2. Perches. Chickens sleep high, as in a tree. Give each chicken 15 to 20 cm of perch, all at one level, otherwise they fight over the highest. Put perches above the nest boxes.
3. Nest boxes. About 30 cm wide, deep and high, one per four or five laying hens. Old fruit boxes work. Fill with dry grass or smooth wood pieces.
4. Air. Let air pass through the house, with no cold wind blowing on the chickens at night.
5. Mesh. Use strong wire mesh with small holes. Bury it, or bend it outward on the ground, so that nothing digging can get in underneath.
6. At night. Close the chickens in one by one [?"ngenye ngenye"] at night. Keep small chicks in a covered netted place.
7. Water. An upturned cold-drink bottle standing in a shallow dish makes a simple drinker. Keep it in shade and clean it often.
Chickens also scratch seedlings and mulch (dry grass and leaves that cover and protect the soil), so rotate them: a bed after harvest, then back to their yard until the next bed is ready.
### Which chickens?
Local breeds have been kept in southern Africa for generations. They are tough, find their own food and are good mothers (raise chicks well), but grow and lay less than commercial chickens. Well-known South African breeds are Potchefstroom Koekoek, Venda, Ovambo, Naked Neck and Boschveld, a new breed made in Limpopo from Venda, Matabele and Ovambo chickens.
In ARC trials with good food and house, these breeds began laying at about 24 to 26 weeks. Koekoek hens laid about 196 eggs a year, Venda about 154, Naked Neck about 139 and Ovambo about 126. A village hen getting all her own food lays very few eggs, often three batches of about 10 each a year, because she stops laying to sit on eggs and raise her chicks.
Commercial hybrids, layers for eggs and broilers for meat, are bred more but need good food and care, and are less hardy when roaming alone.
### Keeping chickens healthy
Newcastle disease is the biggest killer of village chickens in southern Africa. It can wipe out most of an unvaccinated flock in days.
1. Vaccinate. Newcastle vaccines for a small flock are sold at co-ops and farm stores. Eye drops protect best; vaccine in drinking water works too. Follow the instructions on the pack.
2. Repeat. Protection wears off, so village flocks are usually re-vaccinated about every three months.
Biosecurity (protection from disease) is daily habits that keep disease off your farm:
- Keep new birds separate from your flock for about a month, and watch them for sickness.
- Keep wild birds away from your chickens' food and water.
- Keep houses clean and dry, and change nest material often.
- Wash hands after handling birds or manure.
- Bury or burn dead birds, where dogs cannot dig them out.
When new birds arrive, wait a day or two of fighting while the pecking order (arrangement of top and lower birds) settles. Add them at night, after the flock has gone to sleep, two or more together so none is attacked alone, and there should be room for an attacked bird to run away.

## Pigs
Pigs are the best cleaners. Put them on hard ground grown over with grass and weeds and within a week they will have worked it better than a pick or fork. They suit Zones 2, 3 and 4, and work well in an orchard and a food forest, a garden planted in layers like a natural forest.
### Feeding pigs
Pigs eat grass, creepers, fallen fruit, nuts and any roots they can dig. Give them clean water and shade at all times. Feed them vegetables and garden waste from your own farm, plus grains or bought feed. Never give them meat scraps or kitchen waste from other homes, restaurants or hotels. Such food may carry African swine fever, which kills pigs and has no treatment or vaccine.
### Pig tractors
1. Fence a plot with a movable electric fence: one or two wires at pig-nose height.
2. Let the pigs first eat most of the above-ground plants. They will not dig while there is lots of food on top.
3. Make sure the soil is moist. Pigs dig soft soil, not hard dry soil.
4. After one week the plot has been dug. Move the fence.
5. Follow the pigs with a chicken tractor (a moving chicken house) so it spreads their manure.
In an orchard, pigs eat fallen fruit and the fruit-fly larvae in it, which helps break the fruit-fly cycle.
> Tip: Teach young piglets the electric fence while small, in a strong pen where the electric wire runs inside. A pig that has never felt the wire will push right past.
### Keeping pigs in
A free pig can ruin young trees and a whole vegetable garden in one night. Fence them well, and choose carefully where they are allowed to go.
Pigs like company, so keep at least two. If you do not want to breed, keep only females or castrated males. Give them a dry shelter without cold wind in winter and shade in summer.

## Ducks
"You don't have a snail problem, you have a duck shortage." This saying is commonly attributed to Bill Mollison.
Ducks suit Zone 2. They do not scratch like chickens, so they are kind to mulch. Let them roam the orchard, and the vegetable garden after harvest, but keep them away from small seedlings, which they trample or eat.
Ducks eat slugs, snails and insects. Muscovies eat grass and keep it short. Duck eggs are bigger than most chicken eggs, and ducks lay very early in the morning: keep them in the house until mid-morning, and you will find the eggs in the house, not hidden in the garden. Muscovy meat has not much fat and is dark.
What ducks need:
1. A safe house at night, the same as chickens.
2. Water deep enough for the whole head to go in. Ducks wash food from their eyes and nostrils. A deep basin or bucket is enough; they do not need a pond.
3. Fresh water often. Ducks foul their water quickly. Change it daily and pour the old water on fruit trees and on plants you cook. It carries manure, so do not pour it on morogo or anything eaten raw.
4. Fencing. Most farm ducks, such as Pekin, runner and Khaki Campbell, come from the wild mallard. Escaped mallards breed with native wild ducks like the yellow-billed duck and drive them away. Keep your ducks fenced in, away from rivers, dams and wet places. The Muscovy is a different species.
Most ducks are noisy. Muscovies are the quiet ones: mostly the males hiss.

## Bees
Bees are pollinators (insects that carry pollen): they carry pollen from one flower to another so that fruit and seed form. Worldwide, about three quarters of the main food crops benefit from animal pollination. Maize, wheat and sorghum are wind-pollinated, but most of our fruit and vegetables need pollinators.
### Feed the bees
Try to have something flowering in every season.
- Plant a flowering hedge of indigenous trees, growing naturally here, like karee and umnga (sweet thorn).
- Plant aloes. They flower in winter, when little else does.
- Grow lavender, rosemary, borage, rocket, fruit trees and berry bushes.
- Leave a patch of wild grass and weeds to flower.
- Put out a shallow dish of water with stones, so bees drink without drowning.
- Stop using poison to spray insects. If you must spray, use the mildest, very late in the evening when bees are not flying, and never spray open flowers.
Worldwide, honeybees are dying from pests, diseases, hunger and insect poison. The South African story is more hopeful. The varroa mite, a creature living on another, arrived here in 1997, and our honeybees were able to resist it within a few years, so beekeepers here do not stay with the month [?"abahlali beyinyanga" — unclear]. The biggest threat is American foulbrood, a disease caused by a bacterium that kills young bees. It was first confirmed in the Western Cape in 2009, and its spores live for decades in honey, wax and old hive equipment.
### South African honeybees
We have two honeybees: the African or savanna bee (Apis mellifera scutellata) across most of the country, and the Cape honeybee (Apis mellifera capensis) in the Western Cape and nearby places. Both defend themselves strongly. If you disturb a hive, they sting in large numbers, and can follow a person or animal for hundreds of metres. Learn at a course and from an experienced local beekeeper before opening your first hive.
### Siting hives
Natural beekeepers open the hive a few times a year, so it can stand at the edges of Zone 2, or in Zone 3 or 4.
1. Away from people and animals. Keep hives far from houses, paths, roads, main roads, schools, children, and tied or penned animals. A hedge or wire in front of hives makes bees fly up and over people's heads.
2. Level and raised. Put each hive level on a firm standing place, so it doesn't lean and the combs stay unbroken. Lift it off the ground against rats and damp. A pile of old tyres works.
3. Ants. Stand the hive-stand legs in tins of water or oil, or smear them with sticky grease.
4. Sun and wind. Protect the hive from strong wind and heavy afternoon sun, so bees use little energy cooling and warming it. Face the entrance east, to the morning sun.
5. Hive security. Honey attracts thieves and animals. Fence a group of hives, or chain and lock one hive.
### Natural beekeeping
Natural beekeeping disturbs the colony as little as possible. Treat the colony as one living body. Open it rarely, so that the nest keeps its scent and warmth. Let the bees build their own comb, and multiply by going off in their swarms. Keep local bees, and only as many hives as local flowers can feed. Put nothing in the hive that does not come from the bees, take only the honey the bees can give up, and let what they do guide you.
Leave them enough honey and pollen in winter and in dry times, instead of giving them sugar water to drink. Never feed bees honey, and never use hives, frames or wax that has been used before unless you know where it comes from: both can carry foulbrood spores.
### Bee stings
Being stung by many bees at once, or once by a person with an allergy, can kill.
1. Wear a veil, a bee suit or clothes with long sleeves and long trousers, and gloves.
2. Work calmly, using a smoker (device that sprays smoke). Do not open hives on a cold, wet or windy day, or at night.
3. If someone is stung, walk away from the hive. Remove the sting by scraping sideways with a fingernail or knife; do not squeeze it. Wash the place and cool it.
4. If the face, lips, tongue or throat swell, if it is hard to breathe or swallow, or if the person gets dizzy, faints or is covered in spreading skin patches, call an ambulance at once: 10177, or 112 from a cellphone. Do the same for anyone stung many times.
Anyone who has ever had a bad reaction to a bee sting must not work with bees.

## Fodder plants
Fodder is plant food grown or gathered for animals. The best fodder plants do more than one job. Many are legumes (bean family) and nitrogen fixers, which add nitrogen from the air to the soil. An annual lives one season; a perennial lives many years.
Table (Plant | What kind | Use):
- Lucerne | perennial legume | rich hay, silage and cut-and-carry feed for animals
- Cowpea | annual legume | leaves and vines fed fresh, or as hay or silage
- Sweet lupin | annual legume | grain and grazing; good to rotate with grains
- White clover | low perennial legume | grazing, fixes nitrogen, feeds bees
- Rye | winter grain | winter grazing and cover crop
- Turnip | root crop | winter food for sheep and cattle; grazing for pigs
- Fodder radish | annual root crop | sown in autumn; its thick root loosens the soil
- Pigeon pea | short-lived tree, damaged by frost | leaves and pods rich in protein; seed for chickens
- Vetiver grass, sterile varieties only | clump-forming grass | holds soil, gives mulch; young leaves as extra feed
- Umnga (sweet thorn) | indigenous tree, 7 to 12 m | fixes nitrogen; pods and leaves are food for browsing animals
- Puzzle bush | indigenous tree, 5 to 12 m | food for browsing livestock; attracts birds
- Karee | indigenous tree, about 7 m | hedge; feeds bees; holds soil; resists frost

## Try this
Give one animal a job this week.
1. Choose an animal you keep, or are thinking of keeping.
2. On paper, draw three columns: Needs, Products, Behaviours. Fill in each.
3. Walk your land with the list. For each product, find the place that wants it: manure on a bed, scratching on a weedy piece.
4. For each need, find something already on the farm that provides it: leftovers, weeds, shade, water.
5. Try the best combination for a few days. For chickens, put a few on a harvested bed.
6. Write down what you see: weeds gone, soil scratched, eggs laid, any damage.

## Key points
- Look at each animal's needs, products and behaviours, and connect them to other parts of the farm.
- Animal tractors move animals over the ground so that their manure, scratching and digging do useful work.
- Chickens need balanced food, grit, calcium, clean water and a house safe from predators, plus Newcastle vaccine regularly every few months.
- Pigs clean the ground, ducks eat snails, but both must be fenced well.
- Put hives away from people, feed bees with flowers all year, and never feed them honey.
Start with one animal and one job. The second will show itself, then the third.
