# Vegetables and main staple crops
A family eats every day, so the garden must feed it every day. Some foods we pick by the handful at a time: leafy greens [imifino], parsley, a bowl of beans. Others we harvest once a season and store: dry maize, beans, sweet potatoes.
Each kind of food wants its own garden, in its own place. Put each where it belongs and the whole farm will work with little effort.
By the end of this chapter you will be able to: place your gardens by your climate and keep crops out of the reach of frost; match the size of your garden to the time and money you really have; plan a kitchen garden, vegetable garden, market garden and staple-crop garden; plant maize, sorghum, dry beans, sweet potato and cassava at the right time and spacing.

## Growing food in southern African climates
There is no single planting calendar here. Know your climate:
- Summer-rainfall interior (Highveld, Free State, most of Eastern Cape, KZN Midlands, Lesotho): rain falls from about October to March; winter is dry and cold, often with frost.
- Winter-rainfall Western Cape: rain falls from about May to September. Summer crops need irrigation.
- Lowveld and frost-free coast (KZN coast, Limpopo and Mpumalanga lowveld): if you have water you can grow for most of the year.
In cold areas frost is the main danger. Cold air is heavy. On still, clear nights it moves downhill like water and collects in low places called frost pockets. Work with it:
1. Leave a low gap in walls and living fences on the uphill side [?"ngaseluphezulu ethambekeni" - toward the upper side of the slope], so cold air keeps moving past and goes far.
2. Plant frost-tender plants high on the slope, never at the bottom of the valley.
3. Use north-facing slopes for the earliest and the latest crops. They get most sun in winter.
4. Extend the season with a plastic tunnel, less costly than glass. If built beside the north wall of the house it also helps warm the house. Some farmers keep a few chickens in winter in a wire-fenced corner of it for warmth and manure; give lots of clean air, since manure gives off ammonia.

## Plan with the life you have
A big garden you cannot look after quickly becomes a weedy, lost garden. Before you dig, be honest about time, money and interest.
1. Do a simple lifestyle audit. Write how many hours a week you can give the garden, what you want from it (food, income or both) and what you can spend.
2. If time is short, keep it simple. Plant many perennials, plants that stay many years, like fruit trees, herbs and asparagus. Save work with drip irrigation, pipes that drip a little water at each plant, and a timer fitted to the tap.
3. If you want income and have time and energy, make a bigger, more intensive garden.
The four gardens in this section follow the permaculture zones: parts of the farm you visit most are closest to the house.

## 1. Kitchen garden (zone 1)
The kitchen garden is in zone 1, a few steps from the kitchen door. You pass it many times a day, so pick from it many times a day.
Grow what you use daily:
- greens and salad leaves you can cut that regrow (cut and come again: pick a few leaves and let the plant sprout again): spinach, Swiss chard, lettuce
- beans and peas
- cherry tomatoes
- cooking herbs: parsley, coriander, spring onions, thyme
Most are annuals, plants that last one season, with a few perennial herbs.
Plant close. Use every bit of ground, and grow upward too, with beans climbing on wire. Per square metre this is the most productive garden, so it needs the most care: sowing, weeding, mulching (covering the soil with dry plant material), feeding the soil and picking off damaging insects.
Make it beautiful. It is small and beside the house, so take time to decorate the bed edges with blocks of wood, old bricks or stones, and cover the paths with small stones or wood chips.

## 2. Vegetable garden (zone 2)
The vegetable garden is bigger. It holds plants that need more room or grow for a long time: cabbage, beetroot, leeks, onions, peppers, tomatoes and okra. You visit it once a day or after a few days, so it is a bit further away, in zone 2. Home tunnels and shade-net houses also belong here.
1. Lay out clear beds with paths between them. On a slope, lay out beds and paths following the contour (a line across the slope at the same level), so that water goes into the place of rolling [?"indawo yokuqukuqela" - likely wrong].
2. Plant many plants together. Add a small frog pond and flowers that feed helpful insects (chapter 10).
3. Stop making edges. [?] Re-loosen / smooth the beds by hand a few times a year.
The work is always the same: checking the drip lines, mulching, adding compost (rotted plant and animal waste), weeding and sowing.

## 3. Growing vegetables to sell in small quantity (zone 3)
A vegetable garden grown mainly to sell is a market garden. From 0.4 hectares (one acre) upward it is in zone 3, the main growing area.
1. Plant one crop per bed. Sowing, weeding and harvesting go fast. Change the crop in each bed every season (chapter 10).
2. Plant close in deep beds with lots of compost. Close planting gives plenty of food per square metre and prevents weeds by shade.
3. Use time-saving tools: a push seeder and a broadfork (a wide fork with two handles that loosens soil without turning it). A bigger farm can add a two-wheeled tractor walked behind by a person.
Market gardens work because buyers want fresh food grown near. Sell directly to buyers, at the farm gate, at the market, to schools, local shops (spaza shops) or restaurants, and you will get the retail price, not the low wholesale price paid by the middleman. Start-up costs are low, and a range of products (vegetables, fruit, eggs, honey, jams) keeps money coming in.
> Tip: Talk to your buyers before planting. Find out what they want, how much, how often and at what price. Grow the market you know, not the one you expect.

## 4. Staple-crop garden (zone 3)
Staple crops are the food that fills the family plate most days. Grains, potatoes and other root crops give energy. Beans and other legumes (plants of the bean family) give protein.
Staples need a lot of room to give a middling harvest, take months to ripen and arrive all together, so they go in zone 3. Their garden needs little work and grows mostly by rain, so choose crops that suit your climate and plant them to catch the rain.
Table: Grains: maize (mielies), sorghum, pearl millet; wheat, oats and barley (mostly winter crops). Beans and other legumes: sugar beans and other dry beans, cowpeas, jugo beans, groundnuts, soya, pigeon peas, mung beans, chickpeas. Root crops: potatoes, sweet potatoes, amadumbe, cassava (only in frost-free areas). Space-hungry vegetables: pumpkins, butternut, watermelon, melons and gourds.
### Maize
1. In summer-rainfall areas plant after good rain has wet the soil and the frost is over, usually from October to December.
2. Plant in rows about 90 cm apart. In dry areas leave about 50 cm between plants in the row, so each has enough water. With good rain, irrigation or rich soil, plant them about 30 cm apart.
3. Choose open-pollinated (freely pollinated) seed if you want to save your own. Hybrid seed gives a good yield at first, but seed saved from a hybrid crop gives about one-third less yield, roughly.
### Sorghum
Sorghum tolerates heat and drought better than maize. Where water is scarce, plant it in wide rows about 0.9 m to 2.3 m apart depending on rain and soil, so each plant has more soil to draw water from.
### Dry beans, cowpeas and jugo beans
1. Plant dry beans after the first good rain, when the soil is warmer than about 15 °C: from mid-October to December in most summer-rainfall areas, and up to mid-January where there is frost [?].
2. Grow them beside or between maize. They feed the soil with nitrogen.
3. In hot, dry areas grow cowpeas and jugo beans, which tolerate drought well.
### Sweet potato
1. Cut vine cuttings 20 to 30 cm long from healthy plants.
2. Plant them on raised ridges about 1 m apart, 30 cm apart on the ridge.
3. Where there is frost, plant from mid-November to early December. In frost-free areas plant from August to March.
Grow an orange-fleshed type. It is rich in vitamin A and good for children.
### Cassava
Cassava grows only in warm, frost-free places, like the KZN coast and lowveld, and tolerates drought well once established. Its roots release cyanide, a poison, so we don't eat uncooked cassava: peel the roots and cook them well, and soak the bitter types in water for many days first, and throw the water away.
### Rules of the staple garden
1. Mix crops. Grow maize with beans and pumpkins, not a single crop alone.
2. Disturb the soil as little as possible.
3. Leave stalks and leaves on the ground as mulch. It protects the soil and feeds it as it rots.
4. Plant cover crops between the staple crops.
5. Plant along the contour so rain soaks in and the soil stays in place.
6. Rotate crops so no crop returns to the same place year after year.
7. Rest the soil by planting cover crops or pasture after a few seasons.

## Try this
Draw your four gardens this week.
1. Draw your house and the land around it on paper.
2. Stand at the kitchen door and walk 10 steps. Mark the best kitchen-garden place within that distance.
3. Mark the vegetable-garden place, close enough to walk to easily and close to water.
4. Mark the staple-crop place, and notice where the land slopes.
5. On a still, cold evening, feel [vakalisa] where the cold air settles. Mark that frost pocket.
6. Beside each garden, write the hours a week you can give it.

## Key points
- Know your climate, and let cold air leave the tender plants.
- Match your gardens to your real time, money and interest.
- Kitchen garden is near and planted densely; vegetable garden follows the contour; market garden has single-crop beds and sells directly.
- Plant staples to catch the rain, and never eat cassava uncooked.
- Mix crops, disturb the soil little, mulch, rotate and rest the soil.
Start with the garden at the kitchen door. When it feeds you every day, you will know when it is time to go to the next one.
