# 12 glossary (blind back-translation of the isiXhosa)
Intro: Some permaculture words have no short everyday word in our home languages. In all languages of this book, these words are kept in English, so that farmers, agricultural advisers, trainers and sellers all use the same word. Each chapter explains the word when it first appears. This list explains them all in one place.
Animal tractor: Animals such as chickens kept in a moving pen on a garden bed. They scratch, remove weeds, eat insects that damage plants and add manure, then the pen is moved to the next bed. Also called chicken tractor.
Annual: A plant that grows, flowers, makes seed and dies in one year or one season, like maize, beans or lettuce.
Appropriate technology: Simple, cheap tools and methods that local people can build, use and repair themselves, like rocket stove or solar dryer.
Berm: A low wall of soil, usually built on the lower side of a swale, helping to hold water on the slope.
Biodiversity: The variety of living things in a place: plants, animals, insects, birds, soil life and fungus. Great variety makes the farm strong.
Biogas digester: A closed tank where animal manure and food waste rot without air, releasing biogas (mostly methane) for cooking, and a liquid fertiliser called slurry.
Biomass: All plant and animal matter that grows on your land: leaves, stems, cut grass, cut branches, manure. Can be used as mulch, compost, fodder or firewood.
Biosecurity: Steps you take to stop diseases and pests entering your farm or spreading among animals, like keeping new animals separate for some time.
Blackwater: Water from toilets and nappies. Contains germs and never use it in a food garden.
Canopy: The top layer of the food forest: the tallest trees, whose leaves make a roof over the low plants.
Carbon to nitrogen ratio: The balance between "dry" materials (carbon, like dry leaves and dry grass) and "green" materials (nitrogen, like fresh grass and manure) in a compost heap. 25 to 30 parts dry to 1 part green work well.
Catchment: An area of land or roof where rainwater runs to one place, like a tank, pond or river.
Chop and drop: Cutting plants and letting what you cut fall on the soil as mulch, where it rots and feeds the soil.
Climate change: Long-term change in weather, caused mainly by human activities. In SA it leads to hotter days, unreliable rain, long droughts and stronger storms.
Companion planting: Planting together plants that help each other, e.g. by attracting useful insects, confusing harmful insects or giving shade.
Compost: Plant and animal matter rotted into dark, soft, sweet-smelling material that feeds the soil.
Contour: A line that goes across a slope but stays at the same level from start to end. Swales, beds and ploughing should follow the contour.
Coppicing: Cutting a tree close to the ground so new shoots sprout, which can be cut again later to get poles, firewood or fodder.
Crop rotation: Moving each plant family to a different bed each season or each year, so pests and diseases do not build up, and the soil stays balanced.
Double-reach bed: A raised garden bed narrow enough (about 1 to 1.2 m wide) to reach the middle from both sides, never stepping on the soil.
Drip irrigation: Irrigating with pipes with tiny holes or drippers that release water slowly at the foot of each plant. Saves a lot of water.
Ecosystem: A group of plants, animals, soil life and fungus living together with soil, water and surrounding air.
Edge: Where two different areas meet, like the edge of a pond or forest. Edges are often places very rich in life and growth.
Erosion: Soil being carried off or blown away by water or wind, especially on bare or heavily grazed soil.
Firebreak: A strip of cleared or burnt ground so a wildfire cannot cross it. SA law requires firebreaks on most farm boundaries.
First-flush diverter: A device in the gutter or pipe that throws away the first very dirty rain from the roof, before the clean water goes to the tank.
Fodder: Crops grown or collected to feed animals, like grass, lucerne, tree beans and tree leaves.
Food forest: A garden planted like a natural forest, in layers of trees, shrubs, herbs, ground-covering plants and climbers, giving food with little work once established.
Frost pocket: A low place where cold air stays on windless, clear nights, so frost is heavier there than on the upper slope.
Grafting: Joining a piece of one plant (like a good fruit variety) onto the roots of another plant, so they grow into one tree.
Green manure: A plant, usually NOT of the bean family (legume), planted to be cut or dug into the soil to feed it, not to be harvested.
Greywater: Used water from washing, baths and basins (not from toilets). With careful use, it can irrigate trees and non-food plants.
Groundcover: Low plants that spread over the ground to protect it, like pumpkins, sweet potato or clover.
Guild: A group of plants (sometimes with animals) placed around the main plant, usually a fruit tree, so they help each other.
Habitat: A plant's or animal's natural living place, with the food, water and shelter they need.
Humus: The dark, stable part of organic matter in the soil, left after plant and animal matter has fully rotted. Holds water and nutrients.
Indigenous: A native plant or animal that grows naturally in a place and was not brought by people.
Infiltration: Water soaked down into the soil instead of running off the surface.
Invasive species: A plant or animal from elsewhere that spreads uncontrolled and damages natural areas, water availability or farming. In SA, listed invasive non-local plants and animals are controlled by law (NEMBA).
Keyhole bed: A round garden bed with a path cut into it like a keyhole, so you can reach the whole bed without stepping on it. Often built with a compost basket in the middle.
Legume: A plant of the bean and pea family. Most legumes can take nitrogen from the air and add it to the soil.
Microclimate: The weather of a small area around a certain place, like a warm north-facing wall or a cool shady low place. Can differ a lot from the weather of the whole area.
Mulch: A cover of dry grass, leaves or other material spread on the soil to keep it moist, cool and protected, and stop weeds.
Nitrogen fixer: A plant, usually a legume, with roots that work with bacteria to take nitrogen from the air and add it to the soil.
Organic matter: Anything in the soil that was once alive: roots, leaves, manure, dead insects and soil life.
Perennial: A plant that lives more than two years, like fruit trees, rhubarb, herbs and many kinds of grass.
Permaculture: A way of designing farms, gardens and communities so they work like a natural system and keep producing for generations. The word joins permanent and agriculture.
pH: A measure of how much sourness ("bawa") or sweetness the soil has, on a scale from 0 to 14. Most plants grow well between about 6 and 7.
Pollinator: An animal, like a bee, butterfly, beetle or bird, that carries pollen from one flower to another so plants make fruit and seed.
Predator: An animal that eats other animals. In the garden, useful predators like ladybirds, birds and frogs eat harmful insects.
Rocket stove: A simple stove that keeps heat well, burns small pieces of wood very hot and clean, using very little wood and making little smoke.
Rootstock: The root part of a grafted tree, chosen for its strength or its suitability to soil or climate.
Runoff: Rainwater running across the surface instead of soaking in.
Sector: Forces entering your land from outside, like sun, wind, fire, water or noise. Sector planning shows on the map where they come from.
Slurry: The liquid left in a biogas digester. Can be used as fertiliser, but not on crops eaten raw.
Solar dryer: A box or cabinet that uses the sun's heat, the food being shaded from direct sun, so fruit, vegetables and grains are dried safely.
Succession: Natural change, step by step, in a place's plants and animals over time, e.g. from bare ground to grass to shrubs.
Swale: A level ditch dug following the contour of a slope, to catch rainwater and let it soak into the soil.
Thermal mass: Heavy materials like stone, brick or water that absorb heat in the day and release it slowly at night.
Topsoil: The top, very dark layer of soil, where most roots, soil life and organic matter are found.
Trap crop: A plant planted to attract insects away from your main crop, so they can be removed.
Understorey: The layer of small trees and shrubs growing under the canopy in a food forest.
Windbreak: A row or belt of trees, shrubs or tall grass that slows the wind and protects crops, animals and homes.
Wonder bag: A heat-keeping cooking device: an insulated bag into which you put a pot of boiling food so it continues to cook with its own heat without adding firewood or coals.
Worm farm: A box or container where special compost worms (red wigglers) turn kitchen waste into rich worm manure and worm tea.
Worm tea: Liquid that comes out of the worm farm. Mixed with water, it is a gentle liquid fertiliser.
Zone: An area of the farm arranged by how often you visit it. Zone 0 is the house, Zone 1 is the home garden near the kitchen, up to Zone 5, left as nature.
Key points: Words in this list are kept in English in all languages of this book. Each chapter explains a word when it first appears; come back here any time you forget one.
