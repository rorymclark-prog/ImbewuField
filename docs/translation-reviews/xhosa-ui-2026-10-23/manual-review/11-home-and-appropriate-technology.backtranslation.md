# Phase A blind back-translation (from isiXhosa only) - ch 11 home and appropriate technology

Title: A sustainable home: Zone 0 and appropriate technology.
At the centre of every farm is the kitchen. That is where fuel, water and a large part of the money go. A family may grow plenty of food yet lack energy: buys paraffin, carries firewood for hours, sits in darkness when electricity goes off. The home is where permaculture pays back quickest. Every litre of rain caught, every piece of wood saved and every hour of free sun used is money that stays in the home.
Outcomes: explain what Zone 0 and appropriate technology mean; plan how to make a house warm in winter and cool in summer using the sun; choose a suitable solar device for lighting, hot water, cooking and drying food; cook with little fuel using a wonder bag and rocket stove; explain how a biogas digester turns manure into cooking gas.
Home is Zone 0: in permaculture we plan the land in zones according to how often we visit each part. The house is Zone 0, the central point of the design. Think of a home that is built of local materials such as clay and nearby wood, replaceable when old; stays warm in winter and cool in summer with little heating or cooling; makes its own electricity with the sun so lights stay on when the grid goes out; cooks with gas made from animal manure; collects rainwater in tanks and ponds, enough to carry through dry season if used carefully.
Tools that make this possible are called appropriate technology (simple suitable technology): simple, cheap tools and methods that local people can build, use and repair themselves. They work with local skills and materials and meet basic needs for energy, water, food and waste. Some you can make this weekend; others need small investment. All pay for themselves over time. You don't have to do everything at once. Each step saves money and makes the family safer.

Design the house with the sun: in Southern Africa the sun is in the north. It climbs high in summer, low in winter. A house that works with this warms and cools itself free. 1 Put main living rooms and most windows to the North so winter sun enters. 2 Build an eave or pergola over north windows. It blocks high summer sun and lets low winter sun in. 3 Keep west windows small. Summer afternoon sun makes house very hot. 4 Use heavy materials, like stone, brick, clay or concrete floor, where winter sun falls. This thermal mass absorbs heat by day and returns it at night. 5 Put in ceiling with insulation (thing that stops heat getting in or out). Keeps heat in in winter and out in summer. 6 Plant trees or climbers that lose leaves in winter on north and west. Give shade in summer and let sun in in winter. If building, finish these before the first brick is laid. In an existing house start with ceiling.

Solar power: Renewable energy renews itself: sun, wind, flowing water and biomass (plant and animal stuff like wood and manure). Coal does not: when burned it's finished. Coal still produced about 83% of SA's electricity in 2024, and burning it pollutes air and causes climate change. The sun is renewable energy we all share. In one hour, the sunlight reaching Earth carries almost the same energy as everything the whole world uses in a year. SA gets some of the strongest sunshine anywhere, and solar panels now cost less than 1% of what they cost in the 1970s.
Solar panels (PV) turn sunlight into electricity. Batteries store electricity for night. 1 Place where nothing shades between 9 am and 3 pm. 2 Face north. 3 Tilt at angle near your latitude: about 26 degrees in Johannesburg, 34 in Cape Town. 4 Wipe off dust and bird droppings. Small set runs LED lights, charges phones, plays radio. Big system runs fridge, TV or computer. Needs inverter, which turns the direct current (DC) of panels into alternating current (AC) used by most appliances. Keep solar electricity for lights, phones, fridges and TVs. Geysers, kettles, heaters and stoves eat a lot of power and running them on panels needs a big, expensive system. Heat water and cook with the devices below.
Solar geyser: In many SA homes the geyser uses the most electricity: Eskom research put it at 39% of a typical household's use. A solar geyser heats water in collector (panel that collects heat from the sun) of flat panels or glass tubes on roof then stores it in an insulated tank. In the simple type hot water rises to tank by itself, so tank must be above collector. - Face the collector north and tilt as a solar panel. - Where frost happens, which is in many places on the inland plateau, use an indirect geyser. Heats antifreeze liquid inside collector instead of water. A direct geyser can freeze and burst. - Put the electric back-up element on a timer so it works only when the sun hasn't finished the job. Tip: A drum or water bottle painted black and put in sun in morning gives warm washing water in the afternoon, free.
Solar cooker: cooks with sun only and costs nothing to run. Box cooker: insulated box, black inside, with clear glass or plastic lid. Holds heat like a closed car in the sun and reaches about 100 to 150 degrees C, enough to cook and bake, slowly. Panel cooker: shiny panels reflect sunlight onto a black pot inside a clear bag. Cheap and easy to make. Parabolic cooker: curved dish of mirrors focuses sunlight on pot. Reaches over 250 C and cooks fast, but must be turned often to follow sun. 1 Cook between 10 am and 3 pm. 2 Use thin dark pots with tight lids. 3 Turn cooker to face the sun about every hour. 4 Add time beyond what you'd need on ordinary stove. If sun goes before meat or beans fully cooked, continue cooking on stove.

Drying food with sun: Summer gives more than family can eat; winter gives little. Drying carries the surplus. Works because mould and germs [iintsholongwane] that spoil food can't grow without water. Sun drying outdoors is cheapest but slow: food may go mouldy before dry, and flies, dust and dew reach it. Do it well: 1 Use fresh, clean produce. Wash it and wash your hands. 2 Cut into thin, equal pieces. 3 Dry on trays or raised racks, never put on ground. 4 Cover with clean net so flies don't reach. 5 Bring in at night, or cover so it doesn't get dew. 6 Turn pieces once or twice a day.
Solar dryer: box that dries food with the sun's heat; faster and cleaner. Can be simple as wooden box with clear plastic lid and trays of mesh or shade cloth, or strong as metal frame with glass. Dryers that let hot air move through trays work quickly because moving air removes moisture. Food is dry enough when: fruit is like leather and bends without wet spots when pressed; vegetables brittle, break or rattle when shaken; maize, beans and nuts hard and rattle, dried to below about 13% moisture, within two or three days after harvest. Dry grain and nuts fast, throw away any mouldy, discoloured or shrivelled. Mould on wet grain makes aflatoxin, poison to the liver from mould, which cooking does not destroy. Store dried food in clean, tightly closed containers so air does not enter, in cool dark place, throw away anything smelling of mould. Dipping fruit pieces in lemon juice and water mix keeps their colour. Quickly dunking vegetables in boiling water (blanching) helps them last longer.

Wonder bag: Pot of samp and beans may simmer hours and every hour burns wood, gas or paraffin. A heat-retaining cooker lets the pot finish cooking with its own heat. Wonder bag is an insulated bag that keeps boiling pot hot for hours. Wonderbag developed in SA in 2008; home-made hay box, filled with dry grass or old blankets, works same way. Burning less fuel means less money spent and less smoke in the house. 1 Boil food until it boils hard, then let it simmer a few minutes. Samp, beans and tough meat need to simmer longer. Soak dry beans in water then boil hard for at least 10 minutes before they go in bag. 2 With pot still covered, put straight in bag and close. 3 Use a pot that sits well and is full. A full pot keeps heat longer. 4 Do not open the bag to look. Each look lets heat out. 5 Eat food while still steaming. If it is only lukewarm, boil again from start. Never leave food in the bag overnight.

Rocket stove: Wood is heavy to carry and slow to regrow. And smoke kills. WHO estimates about 2.1 billion people cook on open fires or simple stoves burning wood, dung, crop residue, charcoal or paraffin. Smoke in their homes caused an estimated 2.9 million deaths in 2021. Women and small children, who spend lots of time beside fire, suffer most. Rocket stove burns small sticks in short, insulated, L-shaped chimney. Fire burns hot and clean, heat rises straight to pot. Field tests found rocket stoves use an estimated 30 to 50% less wood than an open three-stone fire, with much less smoke and less carbon monoxide. Some portable; some built in, with chimney. 1 Burn dry wood. Wet wood gives smoke and less heat. 2 Put in a few small sticks at a time and push in as they burn. 3 Keep the air space under sticks clean. 4 Set pot firmly, leave small gap so hot gases go round pot. 5 Cook outside or in a kitchen with a chimney. Safety: rocket stove makes carbon monoxide, a gas "which you [did show / unclear: ongayibonisiyo]" or smell. Never bring a burning stove, brazier or charcoal fire into a closed room to warm up.

Biogas digester: Manure is fuel and fertiliser at once, if used well. Biogas is fuel gas made when bacteria break down manure, food waste or toilet waste in a sealed tank without air. The tank is the digester. Biogas is 50 to 70% methane, the burning part. Most of the rest is carbon dioxide, with a little hydrogen sulphide, which smells of rotten eggs. At home the best job is cooking. The liquid that comes out, slurry, is good fertiliser. You can buy a digester or build from tanks, pipes and fittings bought at hardware shop. It must be fully sealed so air does not enter. 1 Feed with fresh cow, pig or chicken manure mixed with same amount of water, plus chopped vegetable scraps. 2 Don't put things like wood, dry grass, bones, plastic, soap, bleach and other chemicals. Chemicals kill bacteria. 3 Feed little every day rather than a lot at once. A family usually needs the fresh manure of many [ezininzi] cows or pigs per day. 4 Keep warm. Bacteria work best at about 30 to 38 C and make very little gas below about 15 C. On Highveld and in Lesotho, bury it in ground, cover with insulation or put small plastic tunnel over it. 5 Test joints for leaks with soapy water; bubbles show leak. Keep flames away from digester and pipes, cook in well-ventilated kitchen, close gas tap after cooking. 6 Cover inlet and outlet openings. Never enter digester or the pit, even if empty: the gases inside can kill. 7 Let slurry stand or turn into compost for a few weeks. Then put on fruit trees and plants you cook, never on leafy vegetables (morogo) you eat raw.

Try: make a hay box and cook in it this week. 1 Find box, crate, basket or old big bucket large enough to hold your pot plus hand-width space all round. 2 Pack bottom firmly with dry grass, old blankets, cushions or foam pieces. 3 Put pot in and fill sides firmly with same stuff. Lift pot out, leaving nest. 4 Make firm pillow of the same stuff for top. 5 Cook something that needs a long simmer, like samp and beans. Boil it, let simmer a few minutes, put lidded pot in nest and cover with pillow. 6 Leave for a few hours without opening. Write the time it took and the fuel used compared with usual way.
Key points: Home is Zone 0. Appropriate technology = simple tools you can build and repair yourself. Orient house and solar panels north, use thermal mass and eaves to catch winter sun and block summer sun. Keep solar electricity for lights, phones, fridges. Heat water and cook with solar geyser, solar cooker, wonder bag, rocket stove or biogas. Dry food quickly, hygienically and not on the ground. Start with one device this month. When the woodpile lasts longer, you'll know which to build next.
