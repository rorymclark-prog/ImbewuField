# 08-soil — blind back-translation (Phase A, written from the isiXhosa only; line numbers = xh file)

L1 Soil: feed the soil, and it will feed you.
L3 Soil is the beginning of everything. It is not dirt. It is a living ecosystem, and all the food we eat began in it.
L5 Soil formed slowly. Sun, rain, frost and roots break rock into small pieces. Plants, animals, fungi and countless tiny creatures (micro-organisms, creatures so small they cannot be seen by the eyes) turn dead leaves, roots and manure into organic matter, meaning anything in the soil that was alive before. It can take up to 1,000 years to build 1 cm of soil. One storm can wash that centimetre away.
L7 So we turn the usual question around. We do not ask what the plant needs. We ask what the soil needs. Feed the soil, not the plant.
L9-15 By the end you can: read your soil's layers and tell sand, silt, clay and loam apart by hand and with a glass jar; read a pH result and know whether your soil needs lime; plant green manure and use animal manure without making anyone ill; cover a bed with mulch, and turn grass into a new bed without digging; build a hot compost heap and a working worm farm.
L17 Soil layers
L19 A hole in the ground is like a history book. Dig it, or look at a road cutting or gully, and you will see layers: the soil profile.
Table: Fallen leaves: dead leaves on top, starting to rot. Topsoil: very dark layer, full of roots, soil life and organic matter. Usually only 10 to 30 cm deep. Subsoil: lighter, with little organic matter. Clay, iron and minerals washed down from above collect here. Rotten rock: cracked bedrock mixed with a little soil. Bedrock: solid rock.
L29 Topsoil is where fertility lives. Deep dark topsoil holds water well, because its organic matter and crumbly structure leave small spaces of air and water. Trees and plants with a taproot (one big root going straight down) reach into the subsoil for water and minerals, and when their leaves fall and rot, some of the minerals return to the top.
L31 Topsoil is the first thing lost through erosion, the washing or blowing away of soil by water and wind. Keep it covered and fed, and the dark layer will deepen.
L33 Soil types: sand, silt, clay and loam
L35 Soil type decides how fast water drains in your soil and how much plant food it can hold. Soil is made of mineral particles of three sizes: gritty sand is the biggest, powder-like silt is in the middle, and sticky clay is the smallest. The mix of the three is called soil texture.
Table (Soil | In your hand | When there is water): Sandy soil: gritty and loose. Crumbles. Water drains fast and it dries out. Nutrients wash out. Clay soil: sticks when wet, hard when dry. Rolls into a long thin sausage. Holds water and nutrients but water drains slowly. It can fill with water, then crack and form a hard crust. Loam: dark, soft and crumbly. Water drains well but it holds enough water and food.
L43 Clay can be red, brown, yellow, grey or black. Grey or blue-grey patches often mean the soil stays wet a long time.
L45 Loam is the best soil for most vegetables. You cannot change your soil type, but you can change the structure of your soil (how the particles stick together into small crumbs) and how much organic matter it holds. Organic matter is the answer for both problem types: it helps sand hold water like a sponge, and opens clay so air and water move. Fed this way year after year, both sandy and clay soil will move closer to loam.
L47 Testing your soil
L49 Most small farmers have an idea about their soil. This idea is often wrong.
Touch test (ribbon test): 1 Take a small handful of soil from about 10 cm down. Remove stones and roots. 2 Add water little by little and knead until the soil is like dough and stops sticking to your hand. 3 Feel it. Gritty means sand. Smooth and light/floury means silt. Sticky means clay. 4 Press the ball between thumb and first finger to make a flat ribbon, and let it hang until it breaks. 5 Measure the ribbon.
Table: no ribbon, crumbles: sand or loamy sand. Under 2.5 cm: sandy loam or loam. 2.5 to 5 cm: clay loam. Over 5 cm: clay.
Jar test: 1 Take soil from many places in the bed, from the top 15 cm. Mix it and remove stones, roots and leaves. 2 Fill a clear jar with straight sides one-third with soil, almost to the mouth with water, and add one teaspoon of dishwashing liquid to help the particles separate. 3 Shake hard for 3 to 5 minutes and put the jar where nobody will move it. 4 After 1 minute, mark the top of the bottom layer: this is sand. 5 After 2 hours, mark the top of the next layer: silt. 6 After 1 day to 2 days, when the water is mostly clear, mark the top layer: clay. Heavy clay can take a week or more. Anything floating is organic matter. 7 Compare each layer to the whole depth of soil in the jar.
L76 Loam has about 40% sand, 40% silt and 20% clay. If sand is more than half, the soil is sandy; a thick layer of clay and water that stays cloudy for many days means the soil is clay.
L78 Read your soil
L80 While you dig, write down what you see. Dark soil usually holds a lot of organic matter. Sedge and reeds show wet soil. Rocks like sandstone, shale or basalt (dark volcanic rock of Lesotho and the Drakensberg) tell you what the soil is made of. Healthy soil has a pleasant smell of earth; a sour smell can mean water has collected. Count the worms in one spade of soil. See whether rain goes in or runs downhill. And ask what the soil was used for before: old kraals, fields and rubbish places all change soil.
L82 Soil pH: acid or sweet?
L84 pH measures how strongly acid or sweet (alkaline) the soil is, on a scale from 0 to 14. pH 7 takes neither side (neutral), below 7 shows acid soil, above 7 shows sweet soil.
 - Most vegetables grow well at a pH between about 6 and 7, when measured in water.
 - In very acid soil, phosphorus is locked up and aluminium poisons the roots.
 - Most soils in the wetter east of South Africa are acid. Soils in the drier west and in low places may be sweet or salty.
L90 A cheap pH kit gives an inaccurate measure; a soil lab gives an accurate measure and the lime you need. South African labs usually report pH (KCl), which reads lower by about 0.5 to 1 unit than pH in water. pH (KCl) below about 4.5 is very acid for many plants. Spread agricultural lime or dolomitic lime at the rate on the lab sheet. Organic matter also helps stop pH changing quickly.
L92 Looking after your soil
L94 Nature does not leave soil bare. The soil of a forest or healthy natural grassland is always covered, always fed, and never dug. We copy that.
 - Keep it covered with mulch (a blanket of dry grass, straw or leaves) or living plants. Bare soil bakes, forms a hard crust and is blown by the wind.
 - Feed it. Plant residue, compost (rotted plant and animal waste) and manure go back to the soil, never to be burned.
 - Plant many different plants. Different roots reach different depths and feed different soil life.
 - Dig little. Each digging or ploughing breaks the soil structure and burns organic matter. Instead, loosen it with a fork.
 - Do not step on the bed, especially when wet. Walk on the paths.
 - Let it rest. Give a tired bed a season covered with green manure or mulch.
L103 Covered, undisturbed soil gets deeper and more fertile each year. There are five main ways to feed it: green manure, animal manure, mulch, compost and worms.
L105 Method 1: Green manure
L107 Plants can make fertility right where you stand, from sun, wind and rain. Green manure (a plant planted to enrich the soil) is a plant you plant to feed the soil, not to harvest.
L109 Green manure covers bare soil, kills weeds by covering them, feeds bees when it flowers, and adds organic matter when it rots. Many green manures make deep roots that break hard soil. If it is a legume (plant of the bean and pea family), it adds nitrogen as well: bacteria in small lumps on its roots, called nodules (root lumps), take nitrogen from the air. That makes the legume a nitrogen fixer.
How to use green manure: 1 Sow it thickly in a rested bed, or between crops. 2 Let it grow until it starts to flower, when it has the most leaves and nitrogen. 3 Cut it before it has seed, and chop it into short pieces with a spade or slasher. 4 Leave it on top as mulch (chop and drop), or dig it in lightly into the top 10 to 15 cm. 5 Wait 3 to 4 weeks before planting in the bed, a little longer in cold weather.
L119 Tip: The first time you plant a legume in a field, buy the matching rhizobium inoculant (powder of helpful root bacteria) from the seed seller and mix it with the seed. The plants will fix more nitrogen.
Table (Season | Plant | Why plant it): Summer sunn hemp: legume. Grows fast and tall, with deep roots. Summer cowpea: legume. Tolerates heat and drought, and you can eat its leaves and beans. Summer lablab: legume. A climbing plant that tolerates drought and suits livestock feed. Summer buckwheat: flowers in about 6 weeks. Bees like it very much. Winter oats and vetch: oats gives bulk; vetch, a legume, adds nitrogen. Winter fodder radish: has a long taproot that breaks hard soil.
L132 In summer-rain areas, sow the summer ones when the rain has started and the soil is already warm. In the winter-rain Western Cape, or anywhere you can irrigate, sow the winter ones in autumn. Mexican sunflower, praised in many permaculture books, is a non-native invasive plant in South Africa; instead plant sunn hemp or lablab to get bulk.
L134 Method 2: Animal manure
L136 Every animal on the farm is a small fertiliser factory. Manure adds organic matter and nutrients, especially nitrogen, and feeds soil life. Manure of cattle, sheep, goats, horses, rabbits and chickens all works; chicken manure is very strong, and burns roots when it is fresh. An animal tractor, a mobile chicken house standing over a resting bed, spreads manure straight onto the soil (see the chapter on animal systems).
How to use manure: 1 Make compost with manure before it comes near plants you eat raw. Compost kills most germs and weed seed, stops the manure burning roots, and turns it into humus: the dark, stable part of organic matter that holds water and nutrients. 2 If you must use manure that has not rotted, work it into the soil at least 4 months (120 days) before you harvest crops that touch the soil or are eaten raw, such as lettuce, spinach and carrots. For crops that grow above the ground, such as maize and fruit trees, leave at least 3 months (90 days). Never put fresh manure beside vegetables already growing. 3 Never use human manure, or dog or cat manure, in a food garden. They carry worms and other parasites that infect people, especially children. 4 Use pig manure only after fully hot composting and long storage, and never on crops eaten raw. Its roundworm eggs survive for years. 5 Wash your hands after handling manure, and wash vegetables before you eat them.
L146 Know where your manure comes from. Do not use manure of sick animals, or manure of animals given medicine against worms in the past few weeks (with ivermectin, for example), because that harms dung beetles and worms. Some long-lasting weedkillers sprayed on pasture pass through animals and survive composting, and very small amounts damage tomatoes, beans and potatoes, so ask whether the animals grazed on sprayed pasture.
L148 Method 3: Mulch
L150 Go into healthy natural grassland and push the grass aside. The soil underneath is dark, moist and cool. Mulch does the same job in the garden: a blanket of dry grass, straw, leaves, wood chips or crop residue spread over the soil. Of all the ways of caring for soil, start here.
L152 Mulch protects the soil from sun, wind and heavy rain, helps rain soak in, greatly reduces loss of water by evaporation so you water less, keeps soil cool in summer and warm in winter, and slows weeds. As it rots it feeds soil life and becomes humus.
How to mulch: 1 Water the bed well first, or mulch soon after good rain. 2 Spread mulch 5 to 10 cm deep. Use a thin layer of fine stuff like cut grass, which mats into a thick wet blanket. 3 Keep mulch about 5 cm from vegetable stems and 10 cm from tree stems, otherwise the stems stay wet and rot. 4 Top it up again as it rots.
L161 Woody mulch such as wood chips or sawdust takes nitrogen from the soil surface as it rots. Put it on paths and around trees, or put a thin layer of manure or compost under it.
L163 Tip: Slugs and snails love the dark wet place under mulch and eat seedlings at night. Pick them off with a torch in the evening, keep mulch a hand's width away from small seedlings, and let ducks or chickens hunt in the bed when the plants are big enough that they can no longer be scratched out.
Sheet mulching: turns grass or weeds into a new garden bed without digging. 1 Cut the grass or weeds and leave them lying. Water well, and spread a thin layer of manure or compost over the cut grass. 2 Cover with flat cardboard or thick newspaper, overlapping by 15 cm so light cannot get in. Remove tape and staples, and do not put in glossy or colour-printed paper. 3 Wet the cardboard, and cover it with 10 cm or more of straw, leaves or dry grass. 4 To plant, push a spade into the mulch and cut a hole in the cardboard. Fill it with a few handfuls of compost, plant a seedling, small shrub or tuber, and water well.
L174 Cardboard kills grass by covering it, and rots slowly. Worms go in underneath and open the soil for you.
Mulch bank: Where mulch is scarce, grow it. A mulch bank is a place of fast-growing plants that you cut often to get biomass, lots of plant material for mulch and compost. It also breaks the wind and holds soil on a slope. Choose indigenous plants (born here) or ones known not to spread: thatch grass, red grass and weeping love grass; seedless vetiver on contour lines; pigeon pea; and shrubs that regrow after cutting, like Cape honeysuckle and sand olive.
L182 Method 4: Compost
L184 Forest soil makes compost slowly, all the time. A good heap does it much faster. Compost is dark, crumbly and smells sweet. It improves sandy and clay soils, holds water, feeds plants slowly, and turns waste into fertility instead of smoke.
L188 Compost needs browns, greens, air and water. Then it needs time.
L190 Browns are dry and rich in carbon. Greens are fresh and rich in nitrogen. The balance between them is called the carbon-to-nitrogen ratio. In a hot heap, aim for 25 to 30 parts carbon to 1 part nitrogen, which in practice means about 2 to 3 buckets of browns to each bucket of greens.
Table browns: straw and dry grass; dry leaves; chopped maize stalks; cardboard and newspaper torn into small pieces. Greens: fresh cut grass; green weeds without seed; vegetable and fruit peels; animal manure.
L199 If it is slimy and smells of ammonia: add browns. If it stays cold: add greens or manure. If it smells rotten: it is too wet, so turn it and add browns.
Air. Compost life needs oxygen. Turning lets in air, and a base of sticks does so too. Water. Keep the heap wet like a squeezed sponge. Squeeze a handful: only a drop or two should come out.
L205 Do not put in: dog, cat and human manure; meat, fish, bones and milk, which attract rats; diseased plants and weeds with seed, unless the heap gets hot properly; couch-grass roots (put them in a bucket of water for a few weeks first, so that they die); and wood treated with preservatives, coal ash and plastic. A little ordinary wood ash is acceptable.
L207 How to build a hot compost heap
L209 A hot compost heap reaches 55 to 65 °C, hot enough to kill most germs and weed seed. To get this hot it must have a width, length and height of not less than 1 m, and not be taller than 1.5 m so that you can turn it.
 1 Choose a shaded, flat place near the garden and water. Build directly on the ground so worms can come in. 2 Put about 20 cm of sticks, small branches or maize stalks, so air gets in and water drains. 3 Add 10 to 15 cm of browns. 4 Add 5 to 10 cm of greens. Bury kitchen waste in the middle, away from the edges, to keep out flies and rats. 5 Add a thin layer of manure, about 5 cm. 6 Sprinkle a handful of garden soil or old compost to bring in micro-organisms. 7 Water each layer until it is wet but not soaked. 8 Repeat steps 3 to 7 until the heap is 1 to 1.5 m high. 9 Cover with dry grass and then old sacks or a tarpaulin to keep out heavy rain, leaving the sides slightly open so air gets in.
L221 Looking after the heap
 1 In 2 to 4 days the heap should get hot. Push an iron rod into the middle for 5 minutes; when you pull it out it is too hot to hold comfortably, the heap is working. 2 After 5 to 7 days it starts to cool. Turn it now. Take the whole heap apart with a fork and put it into a new heap beside it, the outside going into the middle and the middle going outside, adding water if it is dry. 3 It heats again. Turn it each time it cools, about once a week in the first month. Four or five hot turns kill most germs and weed seed. 4 If it goes above about 70 °C, turn it or add water. Too much heat kills the helpful micro-organisms. 5 Stop adding new material. Start a second heap of fresh waste.
L229 A well-turned hot heap is ready in a few months in warm weather; a heap that is never turned can take 6 months to a year or more. It shrinks by a third to a half. Finished compost is dark, crumbly and smells like forest soil. If it is still hot when you turn it, wait: half-rotted compost takes nitrogen from the soil, and your plants will turn yellow. When the heap has cooled, red wigglers (see Method 5) will finish it. Keep finished compost covered and use it within a few months.
L231 Method 5: Worms and the worm farm
L233 Charles Darwin spent years watching worms. In 1881 he wrote: "It may be doubted whether there are many other animals which have played so important a part in the history of the world, as have these lowly organised creatures."
L237 Worms in your soil. Worms are the garden's silent workers. They pull dead leaves into the soil, their tunnels let in air and water, and worm castings bind soil into small crumbs that show healthy soil. To attract more, mulch, add compost, dig little and use no poison.
L239 Composting worms. A worm farm is a container where special composting worms turn kitchen waste into rich worm castings. These worms live in rotting leaves and manure, not deep in the soil, and breed fast in a box full of them. The red wiggler is the most common in South Africa and tolerates cold and heat best. The African nightcrawler and Indian blue worm work well in warm areas but do not work well in cold winters.
L243 Buy them from a worm farm or nursery. Garden worms will not survive in a worm farm.
L245 A worm farm gives you two things: worm castings: smooth dark compost with nutrients that plants get more easily than from the waste or soil they come from. Use as top-dressing, in seedling mix, or a handful in each planting hole. Worm tea: liquid that comes out of the farm. Mix with about 10 parts of water, until it is like weak tea, and pour it onto the soil around plants. If it smells bad, the farm is too wet; give that liquid to a compost heap or trees, not to vegetables.
L250 Building and running a worm farm
L252 A worm farm is just a shaded, moist, dark, well-draining home, safe from chickens, birds and ants. An old bath standing on bricks works, with a drainage hole at the low end above a bucket, stones and shade net at the bottom, and a hessian sack and roofing sheet on top. The easiest to harvest is a stack of three crates or buckets:
 1 Get three equal dark containers, about 20 to 30 cm deep. 2 Leave the base of the bottom container solid: it collects liquid. Fit a small tap to it, or stand it over a basin. 3 Drill many holes of about 5 mm in the bases of the top two containers, and a few air holes near the top on their sides. 4 Stand the bottom container on bricks in shade, with one working container on top. 5 Fill the working container two-thirds with worm bedding: torn cardboard, newspaper, dry leaves and straw, soaked and squeezed, with well-rotted manure and a handful of soil. 6 Add about 500 g of worms, about a thousand. One handful takes several months to begin working well. 7 Cover with a wet sack and a lid. Start feeding after a few days. 8 When the working container is full of dark worm castings, usually after 2 to 4 months, put the third container on top with new bedding and food, touching the castings below. 9 In a few weeks the worms climb up. Take out the bottom container, return any worms, and use the castings. The empty container becomes the next top one. 10 Drain the liquid from the bottom container every few days so that it never fills the worms [sic: "so it never fills with/up the worms"].
L265 Red wigglers do well between about 15 and 25 °C, and above about 30 °C they start to die. Keep the farm in deep shade with a wet sack on top in summer, protected in cold winter, and the bedding wet like a squeezed sponge.
L267 When the farm is full of worms and small lemon-shaped cocoons (worm eggs), take out a few handfuls to start another farm or to add to a cool compost heap.
Feeding your worms: 1 Start small. Add more only when most of the last food is gone. 2 Chop or mash the food so it rots quickly. 3 Bury it under the bedding, in a different corner each time, to keep fruit flies away. 4 Add a handful of dry bedding at each feeding.
L276 A strong farm eats up to half its weight in food a day: 1 kg of worms, up to 500 g of waste.
Table (Worms like | Worms don't like): vegetable and fruit peels | onion, garlic, chilli and a lot of citrus; tea leaves, coffee grounds and crushed eggshells | meat, fish, bones and milk; cardboard and soaked paper | oily, salty or cooked food; well-rotted cow, horse or rabbit manure | dog and cat manure.
L285 Some farmers make compost tea, passing air through worm castings in water with a little molasses using bubbles. Research on whether it helps is mixed, and molasses can feed germs such as E. coli and Salmonella. Clear worm tea is simpler. Whichever one, pour it onto the soil and never pour it over vegetables eaten raw.
L287 Try this
Study your soil this week. 1 Choose three places: your best bed, your worst bed, and a patch of undisturbed natural grassland. 2 At each, dig one deep hole of one spade. Measure the dark topsoil and count the worms in the whole spade of soil. 3 Do the ribbon test with a handful of soil from 10 cm down. 4 Pour a bucket of water beside each hole. Does it go in or run off? 5 Start the jar test with soil from your main bed, and read it in the next two days. 6 Write everything down with the date. Mulch your worst bed, and test it again at the same time next year.
L298 Key points
 - Soil is alive and forms very slowly. Keep it covered, feed it, dig little.
 - Organic matter improves sand and clay. Most vegetables like a pH of about 6 to 7.
 - Cut green manures before they have seed. Make compost with manure before it comes near plants eaten raw.
 - Mulch to a depth of 5 to 10 cm, away from stems.
 - A hot heap needs browns, greens, air and water. Worms need shade, wet bedding and a little food.
L306 Dig one spade of soil in the same place each year. The soil keeps its record, cm by cm, about whether your care works.
