# Phase A blind back-translation (written from the isiXhosa only) - ch 10 natural pest control

Intro: A caterpillar on the cabbage is not the enemy; it is food. Birds, frogs, lizards, spiders, ladybirds and small wasps all live on creatures we call pests. If we kill all the pests we also finish off the helpers' food, and the garden does not become more stable, it stabilises less. So when pests suddenly burst out in numbers we take it as a message: something is out of balance - exhausted soil, plants parched by drought, or too much of one crop in one place. A quick natural fix can save this season's harvest. The lasting work is treating the cause. This is not doing nothing. "Obtain a yield" is one of our principles and the family must eat. We protect always-at-risk crops like cabbages and seedlings. But we don't expect every plant to survive. A small portion returns to the system, and the system does most of the pest control for us.
You will be able to: recognise main garden pests of South Africa and handle each without poison; attract predators with flowers, water, shelter; use trap crops and good neighbours and know which companion-planting claims are proven; make garlic/onion/chilli spray and use it without harming bees; plan crop rotation so no family returns to same bed for 3 to 4 years.

Why not chemical poison: costs money every season and harms people and animals. Natural control costs little once set up and gets easier each year. Poison also traps the farmer in a cycle: 1 spray kills pests and also predators (animals and insects that hunt and eat pests). 2 Pests breed much faster than their predators. 3 Pests return first and now nothing eats them. 4 Farmer sprays again. Problem grows and many pests resist the poison. Before chemical farming there was no word "organic" because all farming was natural. Old ways still work, and the garden "that is young" [eselula] needs them a lot.

Step by step: most pest control happens before the pest arrives. Strong plants in rich soil (Ch 8) resist attack. Biodiversity gives every pest an enemy (Ch 9). A big block of one crop does the opposite. When pests arrive do in order: 1 Watch - walk the garden twice a week at least, look under leaves; pests are easy to control when found early. 2 Attract predators - food, water, shelter. 3 Pick by hand - remove caterpillars, snails, beetles; squash aphid clusters. 4 Barriers - net, shade net, collars, fences. 5 "Bring in birds": between plants, chickens or ducks in a moving house, an animal tractor (Ch 5), clean up slugs, snails and soil caterpillars. Keep them away from small seedlings because they trample "it". 6 Spray only as last resort, and only the attacked plants. Balance can take a few seasons. If poison is little and diversity much it arrives quickly.

Common pests: Know your enemy. Most are insects and feed two ways. Sap-suckers pierce the plant and drink its juice: aphids, scale insects, mealybugs, whiteflies, thrips, red spider mites (small relatives of spiders), root-knot nematodes (tiny worms invisible to eyes that make lumps on roots). In numbers they weaken the plant body, and many carry viruses from plant to plant. Plant-eaters chew leaves, stems, fruit or roots: caterpillars, beetles, slugs, snails. They are big so you can often pick them by hand. Moles, porcupines and some birds are also pests.

Aphids: small, soft, pear-shaped, green/black/grey, dense on new shoots and under leaves. Cabbage, beans, peas hurt most. Stunt growth, curl leaves, carry viruses. In warm climate females bear live young without meeting a male so a colony can explode in few weeks. When crowded winged aphids fly to new plants. 1 Squash small colonies with fingers or wash off with strong water jet. 2 Protect ladybirds, hoverflies, lacewings, parasitic wasps; they eat aphids or lay eggs inside. 3 Control ants that protect them. 4 Plant nasturtium at bed edge as trap crop, a plant the insect likes more than your food. 5 Don't add fresh manure or too much nitrogen: soft fast juicy growth attracts aphids. Tip: An aphid colony turned brown and soft like paper has been "xelwe" [?infected] by parasitic wasps. Leave it; the next wasps come out of it.

Cutworm: fat grey-brown caterpillars curl into a C when disturbed. Hide in soil by day, come out at night and cut seedlings at soil level: tomatoes, cabbages, maize, beans. 1 Clear weeds from bed a few weeks before planting so young caterpillars have nothing to eat. 2 When you find a cut seedling dig gently around; cutworm stays within a few cm. Remove it. 3 Put a collar round each seedling: cardboard tube or plastic bottle cut top and bottom, pushed into ground 3 to 5 cm. 4 Turn the soil before planting and let birds and chickens search in it.

African bollworm: striped caterpillar, green, brown or red, soft(?), that bores into tomatoes, maize cobs, beans, peas, peppers and grapes. A small round hole in a tomato is the usual sign. 1 Check plants often while flowering and fruiting. Remove eggs and caterpillars. 2 Remove and destroy damaged fruit so caterpillars inside can't complete their life cycle. 3 Protect parasitic wasps, hunting insects, birds. 4 If you must spray use Bt, a natural bacterium that kills only caterpillars. Works on small caterpillars before they enter fruit.

Diamond-back moth: worst pest of cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower, rape and relatives. Caterpillar about 1 cm, light green, pointed both ends. When touched it jumps backward and falls on silk thread. Young ones leave visible "windows" in the leaf; big ones eat holes right through. This moth has beaten many chemical sprays so natural control matters most here. 1 Look under leaves, remove by hand. 2 Protect small wasps that lay inside caterpillars; poison kills them first. 3 Cover young plants with fine net or shade net. 4 Leave the bed until it is not planted with cabbage family, to break the moth's life cycle. 5 "Drenching by spraying from above knocks off many small caterpillars." If necessary spray Bt on small caterpillars.

Fall armyworm: came from the Americas. First confirmed in South Africa, Limpopo, in "uMdumba" 2017, spread fast. Attacks mostly maize, also sorghum and other grasses. Look for an upside-down pale Y on head and four dark square-arranged spots on second-last body segment. Big caterpillars eat irregular holes and fill the funnel (whorl) of young maize with sawdust-like droppings. 1 From the day maize emerges, check twice a week; look inside funnel for fresh droppings. 2 Squash egg masses (hairy clusters on leaves) and small caterpillars. 3 Plant at once, same time as neighbours, so your maize is not young when armyworm numbers are high. 4 Plant maize mixed with beans, cowpeas or pumpkins; mixed fields have more ants, earwigs, hunters, parasitic wasps. 5 If you must spray put Bt in the funnel while caterpillars are small.

Fruit flies: lay eggs under skin of ripening fruit; larvae eat inside, fruit rots and falls. SA has Medfly, Natal fruit fly and Oriental fruit fly (first found in Limpopo 2010, now settled in many northern provinces). Pumpkin fly does same to pumpkins, melons, cucumbers. 1 Every few days gather all fallen and damaged fruit. Seal in plastic bag and leave in hot sun until larvae die, or bury at least 50 cm deep. 2 Harvest fruit at once when ripe. 3 Tie paper or cloth bags over young guavas, mangoes, peaches. 4 Hang traps with attractant in trees. 5 Work with neighbours; flies move garden to garden and one neglected fruit garden causes all others to be filled again.

Slugs and snails: live in wet places, eat leaves, do real damage when many or reach seedlings. Mulch, a blanket of dry plant material spread on soil, keeps THEM moist and safe, but coarse, dry, scratchy mulch suits them less than wet soft stuff. 1 Collect with torch at night, esp after rain. 2 Put board or old sack on wet ground; in morning collect snails hiding under. 3 Let ducks/chickens clean the beds between plants. 4 Encourage frogs, toads, birds, ground beetles. 5 Pull mulch back from small seedlings until strong. Safety: snails and slugs can carry rat lungworm, a parasite found in SA rats that can make people very sick. Wear gloves or wash hands after picking them, and wash vegetables (morogo) well before eating. We don't use slug pellets: they poison dogs and cats as well as snails.

Birds: most are friends. Red-billed quelea is the exception: flies in big flocks, can strip sorghum, millet and wheat, esp when wild grass seed is scarce. 1 Cover small grain areas with net. 2 Scare birds early morning and afternoon while feeding with noise, flags or shiny tape. 3 Harvest at once when grain ripe. 4 Don't put poison. Eagles and owls that eat dead birds also die.

Ants: ants don't eat plants much. Fed by honeydew, sweet liquid produced by aphids, scale and mealybugs, and so protect these sap-suckers from ladybirds and wasps. Honeydew also causes black mould on leaves that blocks light. In some places ants hunt termites and caterpillars, so we act only where they farm sap-suckers. 1 On fruit trees tie a band of grease or sticky stuff around the trunk, on a piece of cloth or tape rather than on the tree's bark. 2 Cut branches that touch ground or wall; ants use as bridges. 3 Control aphids and scale, ants lose interest.

Moles and porcupines: "moles" that eat bulbs, potatoes, sweet potato in SA are usually mole-rats: mouse-like animals pushing up heaps of soil from deep tunnels. Golden moles are a different animal; dig just below surface and eat insects and worms, not plants. Some species rare so leave alone. 1 Cover underside of raised beds and planting holes of valuable plants with strong wire mesh. 2 Plant bulbs and small trees in wire baskets. 3 Welcome mole snakes and owls. Don't use poison: a poisoned mole-rat kills the owl, snake, dog or cat that eats it. 4 Porcupines dig roots and gnaw bark. Strong fence with mesh buried deep stops digging under.

Encourage predators: give hunters habitat: food, water, shelter: stones and logs for lizards, small pond for frogs, mulch for ground beetles, flowers for wasps and hoverflies. Chameleons [unwabu] and lizards eat insects and are safe among warm stones. Frogs and toads eat slugs and insects. Ladybirds and larvae eat many aphids. Hoverflies look like small bees; larvae eat aphids. Lacewings soft green insects; larvae eat aphids, mites and small caterpillars. Parasitic wasps are small and cannot "xela" people [sting?]; lay eggs inside caterpillars and aphids. Ground beetles, praying mantises, spiders hunt almost any insect smaller than themselves. Insect-eating birds eat caterpillars, moths, termites, scale insects and even snails. Tip: Not all ladybirds are friends. Shiny ones eat aphids. Dull orange ones with many black spots and slightly hairy back, with their yellow spiny larvae, eat leaves of pumpkins, potatoes and relatives. Learn to tell apart.
Flowers: adult wasps, hoverflies, lacewings live on nectar and pollen; small open flowers like carrot family best. Plant a mix that flowers all year: flowering herbs dill, coriander, parsley, caraway, a few carrots left to flower; yarrow, calendula, chamomile, zinnia, sunflower, borage, lemon verbena; French and African marigolds and cosmos (near natural grassland, cut heads before seed falls, as cosmos spreads into natural grass); indigenous flowers: gazania, African daisies, pincushion, everlastings. Leave herbs and a few vegetables to flower in every bed. Helpers stay where there is food.

Companion planting: planting crops, herbs and flowers together so they help each other. Very old. Chinese farmers planted a small floating fern with rice for thousands of years because it adds nitrogen to the water. In the Americas maize, beans and squash grew together as "three sisters": maize supports beans, beans add nitrogen, big squash leaves shade soil and suppress weeds. Trap crops, predator flowers and mixed planting are well tested. Many other claims are only farmers' reports. Try, watch carefully, keep what works on your land.
Mixed planting: mixture feeds more soil life and more predators. Many insects find their food plant by shape, colour and smell; a bed with many shapes and colours confuses, so some leave.
Trap crops: plant near the food plant to draw insect away. Nasturtium near crop often draws aphids, esp black ones, away. 1 plant nasturtium at edge not middle 2 check twice a week 3 when leaves full of aphids, cut them and give to chickens. A trap crop you never check becomes a breeding place.
Scent confusers: strong smell can hide plant hunted by insect. Gardeners plant onions around carrots against carrot fly. Research from England found it works only when onions far outnumber carrots and only while onions are young with leaves, before they form bulbs.
Strengthening plants: basil with tomatoes reduced thrips and whiteflies a little in some trials. Claim that it improves their taste not yet proven.
Repellent: French and African marigolds reduced whitefly on greenhouse tomatoes in trials. They suppress root-knot nematodes only when planted dense as cover crop 2 to 4 months before vegetables. A few scattered plants do little. Mint, rosemary, sage, lavender, wild wormwood (traditionally used to keep insects away) are said by farmers to reduce aphids and moths on cabbage. Worth trying. Plant mint in a tin because it spreads fast.

Neighbours: well-proven rule is simple: keep plants of one family apart. Potatoes, tomatoes, brinjals, peppers all suffer same blights, wilts, nematodes so row of one beside another passes problem. The rest of table comes from gardeners' experience. Start with it, then over time trust your own notes.
Table (good neighbours / keep apart): Beans: maize, potatoes, carrots, cabbage, cucumbers / onions, garlic, leeks. Cabbage family: onions, celery, beetroot, potatoes, lettuce, herbs / tomatoes, strawberries. Carrots: onions, leeks, chives, peas, lettuce / -. Chilli and pepper: carrots, onions, basil, parsley / potatoes, cabbage family. Maize: pumpkins, beans, peas, cucumbers / -. Onion family: carrots, lettuce, beetroot, cabbage family, tomatoes / peas, beans. Peas: carrots, radish, turnips, maize, cucumbers / onion family. Potatoes: beans, maize, cabbage / tomatoes, brinjals, peppers, pumpkins, cucumbers. Pumpkin, squash, butternut: maize, beans, radish / potatoes. Tomatoes: basil, onions, chives, carrots, parsley, marigolds / potatoes, brinjals.
Tip: farmers find sunflowers slow the growth of crops near them. Plant at garden edges where they feed bees.

Homemade sprays: A spray is last resort. Even a "natural" spray kills ladybirds, hoverflies and bees you worked hard to attract. Garlic, onion and chilli repel rather than kill and smell fades, so spray must be repeated.
Garlic/onion/chilli spray: repels soft insects like aphids and small caterpillars. Soap helps it stick. Need: 1 medium onion; 4 cloves garlic; 2 cups fresh mint or 20 drops peppermint oil; 2 tbsp chilli powder or cayenne, or handful fresh hot chillies; 2 tbsp (30 ml) mild liquid soap, or bar soap dissolved in water, without bleach, degreaser or strong scent; 3 litres water.
Method: 1 Wear rubber gloves and keep hands from eyes. Chilli and garlic burn. 2 Chop; crush in mortar and pestle or blend with about 1 litre water. 3 Cover and leave soaking a few hours. 4 Strain through clean cloth. 5 Add remaining 2 litres water and gently mix in soap. 6 Pour into spray bottle and label it. Never keep spray in a cooldrink or milk bottle. 7 Test on a few leaves, wait 1-2 days. If leaves show scorched or yellow spots, add more water. 8 Spray top and underside of attacked plants only, late afternoon or evening when bees stopped flying. Don't spray open flowers or plants wilted in midday heat.
Use once or twice a week and after heavy rain. Make a fresh batch each time and wash sprayed vegetables before eating.
Nicotine poisons people and animals as well as insects, so we don't make tobacco or snuff spray. It enters through skin of the person spraying and a child who drinks it may die. We also don't make sprays from syringa fruit or thorn apple (Datura), because they too are poisonous.

Crop rotation: moving each plant family to a different bed each season so pests and diseases don't gather. Same crop same place every year: soil loses same nutrients each time while soil pests wait for their food to return. Rotation breaks their life cycle and lets soil recover.
By family: same family needs same food and suffers same pests. Wait at least three years, better four, before family returns to bed. Simple four-bed rotation: Y1 cabbage family; Y2 carrot family or root crops; Y3 bean family; Y4 onion family. "This suits farmers who plant one crop in each bed."
Family table: cabbage (cabbage, cauliflower, broccoli, kale, rape, mustard greens, turnip, radish); tomato (tomatoes, potatoes, peppers, chilli, brinjals); carrot (carrots, celery, parsley, coriander, dill); beet (chard, beetroot, spinach, imbuya); pumpkin (cucumbers, pumpkins, butternut, squash, melons, "amakhabe"); bean/legume (peas, beans, cowpeas, groundnuts, jugo beans, soya); onion (onions, leeks, garlic, spring onions, chives); grass (maize, sweetcorn, sorghum, millet).
Potatoes are tomato family. Don't follow tomatoes, peppers or brinjals with potatoes or vice versa. Where bacterial wilt or nematodes a problem leave longer gap.
By hunger: heavy feeders need rich soil: cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower, maize, tomatoes, potatoes, pumpkins, butternut. Light and medium feeders need less: lettuce, chard, carrots, beetroot, onions, garlic, radish. Soil builders are legumes: beans, peas, cowpeas, groundnuts, broad beans. Bacteria in small lumps on roots take nitrogen from air and make available to plants. Rotate in three steps: 1 grow heavy feeders after legumes or after plenty of compost (rotted plant and animal waste) or manure. 2 follow with light feeders. 3 then legumes to rebuild soil, and start again. Suits mixed beds: move group of companions through steps together. Most nitrogen fixed by legume goes into its seed and leaves with harvest. Leave roots in soil and return stems and leaves to bed or compost heap.

Try this: survey one bed this week, meet creatures you share with. 1 choose one vegetable bed, take notebook. 2 walk slowly beside it early morning or evening; turn over ten leaves of each plant. 3 write down everything living you see - harmful or helpful. Draw those you can't name. 4 note damage: holes, windows, curled leaves, cut seedlings, droppings in maize funnel. 5 For each harmful insect pick first step in this chapter and do it today. 6 Sow short row of coriander, dill or marigolds at end of bed. 7 Check bed one week later and compare notes.
Key points: outbreak = sign of imbalance, treat cause. Poison kills predators with pests and often makes problem worse. Check twice a week, find pests early, remove by hand, barriers or trap crop. Feed helpers with flowers, water, mulch, stones, and keep same family apart as you rotate. Spray only as last resort, in the evening and away from flowers. We don't make tobacco spray. Season by season the list of helpers in your notebook grows. Watch it grow and let the sprayer gather dust.
