What is permaculture?
Most of us learned to farm by fighting: fighting weeds, pests and weather. We plough, spray, buy fertiliser, and each year the land gives us a little less.
Permaculture starts somewhere else. It asks what the land is already doing and how we can work with it. It is a way of thinking, based on three ethics and twelve design principles, which you can take to any piece of land, from a back garden in a township to a mountain field.
By the end: explain in simple words what permaculture is and where the name comes from; name the three ethics and use them to test a decision on your farm; name Holmgren's twelve design principles and give a farm example for each; start observing your land as a designer does.
What the word means: permaculture joins two English words: permanent (what stays) and agriculture. It means farming that can feed a family for generations. It also means culture, a way of life that lasts a long time.
Two Australians coined the term in the middle of the 1970s: Bill Mollison (1928-2016) and David Holmgren, who was his student at the time in Tasmania. Their first book Permaculture One came out in 1978. Since then permaculture has spread worldwide including SA.
Mollison defined it as "the design and deliberate maintenance of food-producing ecosystems with the diversity, stability and resilience of natural ecosystems."
In simple words: we plan and run the farm so it works like nature. An ecosystem is a group of plants, animals and soil life living together with the soil, water and surrounding air. A healthy natural veld has many kinds of life (diversity). It continues year after year (stability). It rises again after drought, fire or damage (resilience). A permaculture farm aims at these three things, and feeds people too.
Why it matters in SA: Families who live from the land feel the weather first. There are two growing pressures.
- Climate change = long-term change in weather, caused mainly by people. Farmers already see hotter days, rain that comes late or all together, long droughts and strong storms. Most small farmers farm rain-fed, without irrigation, so they carry the risk heavily.
- Erosion = soil carried off by water or blown by wind. Bare soil, heavily grazed by livestock or ploughed on a very steep slope, loses its topsoil (fertile top soil) with each storm. Soil erosion has affected more than 70% of South Africa's land. Lesotho, where the guide began, also has severe erosion and deep gullies.
South Africa is also a dry country. It gets about 450 mm of rain a year on average, nearly half the world average of 860 mm. Every drop counts.
Land that loses its soil and water stops feeding its people, and they leave. We cannot farm the old way and expect a different result.
Permaculture gives us a map and tools: protect the soil, catch the rain, plant many crops, work with nature. It brings [it] back to daily life.
- Food all year. Vegetables, fruit, grains and legumes (bean and pea family), grown without poison. A plate coming from many [sources] is healthier.
- Money saved and money earned. Few bought things, many products, so that one crop that fails does not bring down the home. The surplus can be sold, dried or put in bottles.
Three ethics: All decisions on a permaculture farm are weighed by three ethics, values that guide us. Mollison and Holmgren placed them at the heart of permaculture.
1 Earth care: Care for all living things (plants, animals, soil life) and the soil, water and air that support life. We depend on all.
2 People care: Meet people's needs for food, water, shelter, health, learning and relationships without damaging the Earth. Build self-reliance in your family and community.
3 Fair share: The Earth has limits. Fair share, often called "fair share", means taking what we need and returning the surplus (food, seed, time, skills and money) to caring for the Earth and people.
Use these ethics as a test. Before you plough a slope, buy a spraying poison or block a spring, ask three questions. Is it good for the land? Is it good for people? Is it fair? If any answer says no, look for another way.
Design principles: The ethics tell us what matters. The design principles are thinking tools for designing: to plan a farm, to see how its parts fit together and to solve problems permanently. Teachers use slightly different lists. These twelve come from David Holmgren's book (2002). Holmgren linked each principle with an old proverb, and some of them are shown here.
1 Observe and interact. You cannot design land you do not know. Where does water go? Where does frost sit? What grows well without help? Books and teachers help, but nothing replaces your time on your land. Steps: 1 Walk your land and the surrounding veld in all seasons. 2 Sit quietly watching plants, birds, insects, animals. 3 Keep a notebook of rain, first and last frost, and when things are [in flower?] ("ziyintyatyambo" - check). Many farmers also note moon phases. 4 Walk during and after heavy rain. See where water goes and where it gathers. After a year of watching, you will know your land better than any visitor. Your design will go along with the land instead of fighting it.
2 Catch and store energy. "Make hay while the sun shines." Nature goes in circles: plenty, then shortage. In much of the interior of SA, the warm wet summer is the growing time and winter is cold, dry, slow. In the Western Cape rain falls in winter and summer is dry. We catch the plenty and keep it for the time of shortage. - Catch summer sun as crops: vegetables, fruit, nuts, livestock feed, firewood and meat. - Catch rain in tanks, ponds and in the soil itself, with mulch (dry grass or leaves covering the soil) and swales (ditches laid level, running sideways across the slope to soak up rain). - Dry, bottle and store summer's surplus for winter. A farm that stores well is not shaken when the rain stops.
3 Obtain a yield. "You cannot work on an empty stomach." A yield is anything useful that the land gives you: food, livestock feed, firewood, shade, medicine, seed or income. A fruit tree may need many seasons before it bears, so plant quick and slow yields together, so that there is always something to show for this season's work. We design for surplus: enough for family, land, animals, neighbours and market. - Sprouts of mung beans or lentils are ready to eat in 2 to 5 days. Use clean water, rinse them twice a day, and throw away any that smell bad or stay slimy. - Fruit trees are slow to start, but give a yield for many years once they begin to bear. - Plant flowers for bees. Many indigenous aloes flower in winter, when few things are flowering. Bees pollinate your crops and can give you honey.
4 Apply self-regulation and accept feedback. Natural systems keep themselves balanced. In a healthy veld no single plant or animal rules for long. People are different: we always take more than we need, and over-use causes climate change and loss of wild plants and animals. So we set limits for ourselves: live simply and take what we need, so that our children get living land. And we accept feedback, signs that tell us whether what we do works. Mistakes are part of learning. Listen to plants, soil, animals and other people, and change what is not working. - Combine errands into one trip, walk or cycle. - Read your plants. Yellow leaves, weak growth or pest outbreaks are the land telling you that something in the soil, water or your choice of plants must change.
5 Use and value renewable resources and services. Sun, wind, rain, plants and animals renew themselves. Petrol, diesel and chemical fertiliser are used once and are finished. They cost money, and burning fossil fuels adds to climate change. Plants and animals can do much of the work of a tractor or lawnmower. They give fuel and fertiliser, control weeds and pests, stop erosion, cycle nutrients and loosen the soil. They take time to build, but in time they reproduce and sustain themselves. - Animal tractors: a moving house for chickens or pigs, which scratch and dig up worms, roots and pests. Close them for a few days on an overgrown area and they clear and fertilise it, ready for planting. - Manure: animals eat plants and return nutrients as manure and manure tea. Make manure compost before it gets near plants you eat raw. - Living fences: a hedge of useful, non-invasive plants gives shelter, livestock feed and firewood without wire or fuel.
6 Produce no waste. "Waste not, want not." In nature there is no waste: the output of one part becomes the input of the next. On the farm we aim at closed loops where nutrients and energy circulate and come out as yield. What finally leaves the farm should be clean. A good rule: rethink, reduce, repair, reuse, recycle. - Kitchen scraps feed worms and are a valuable "green" part (high in nitrogen) of a compost heap. Compost is rotted plant and animal waste that has become dark, soft food for the soil. - Cut grass makes mulch around seedlings. Keeps soil cool and moist and holds weeds. Spread it thin or let it dry first, because a thick layer of fresh grass mats together and heats up/burns. - Greywater is used water from the bath, washing and basins. It goes under the mulch to fruit trees; never use it on leafy vegetables you eat raw, and kitchen water or nappy water is not put in it. Use mild soaps, low in salt.
7 Design from patterns to details. Nature is full of patterns: branching of a river or a tree, the spiral of a shell, water running down a slope. We first find the big pattern of the place, then fill in the details. Mollison thought pattern so important that he gave it a whole chapter in his book (1988). 1 Start with the shape of the land: its slopes and contour, lines crossing the slope staying at the same height. 2 Find the catchment, the area where your water comes from, and follow where it goes. 3 Notice climate and sun's path. In SA the sun is in the north, and low in winter. 4 Only after that place the details: which bed, which tree, which plant. The contour is a pattern; a swale dug alongside it is a detail. Borrow natural shapes too: winding paths like a river, spiral beds or keyhole beds.
8 Integrate rather than segregate. "Hands wash each other." When each part of the farm helps the others, the whole becomes stronger than its parts. Placing each thing (house, garden, chickens, tank, trees) where it can serve the others is the heart of permaculture design. People are the same: children and older people have much to give and learn and can be part of farm life. - Mix trees, crops and animals (agroforestry). Trees give shade and livestock feed, animals give manure, and crops give food and animal leftovers. - Build guilds: groups of plants arranged around a big plant, usually a fruit tree, that help by controlling pests, enriching the soil or shade. - Make safe places where children and grandfathers and grandmothers can work and learn in the garden.
9 Use small and slow solutions. "Little by little one arrives." Many farmers start by lifting [?] with great courage and lose hope when the piece is too big to manage. A small well-cared-for area gives more than a large one left to go wild. 1 Start at your back door with a small kitchen garden. 2 Make it work before you start the next step. 3 Each year bring a little more land into production. 4 Build soil season by season with compost, mulch and cover crops. A big tractor on a small field is expensive and compacts the soil; chickens in a moving house work little but clean and fertilise the soil with very little. Slow change gives you time to watch how the land responds, and change made this way lasts. Have a plan, and expect it to change.
10 Use and value diversity. "Do not put all your eggs in one basket." Diversity is strength. Think of a rope: one strand breaks, but many twisted strands hold. A monoculture, one crop in a big area, always from the same seed, is poor in life and open to all pests, diseases and bad seasons. A diverse farm bends and stands again. Diversity works only when the parts connect. Each plant and animal needs a place and purpose. - Plant vegetables, grains, legumes, and fruit and nut trees that give food at different times of year. If one crop fails, another still feeds. - Save seed from many very healthy plants each season. Seed adapts to your land, and saving from many plants keeps it diverse. - Keep animals that help each other. Chickens following cattle scratch the manure and eat the fly larvae in it.
11 Use edges and value the marginal. An edge is where two different areas meet: a pond and dry land, a forest and veld. Edges are often the densest and most productive, visited by creatures of both parts. As Holmgren said, "The interface between things is where the most interesting events take place." Marginal areas (corners, fences, walls, steep gullies and wet areas) are often neglected. Use them. - Plant a hedge of indigenous plants along fields. Umqokolo (Dovyalis caffra) makes a thorny hedge that bears fruit. Cape honeysuckle and plumbago attract birds and useful insects. - Make a path between two growing areas, such as a food forest (a garden planted in layers like a natural forest) and vegetable beds, so you can tend both at once. - Put the chicken area next to the garden. Weeds go in; manure comes out. - Plant climbers on walls and fences. Climbing beans, cucumbers and "iiselwa" turn an empty edge into food.
12 Creatively use and respond to change. "Vision is not seeing things as they are but as they will be." Nothing in nature stands still. We cannot stop change, but we can expect it and act at the right time. A caterpillar needs different food from the butterfly it becomes; a farm also needs different care at each stage. - A food forest grows from small trees and fast-growing plants to a shady, mature forest. What you plant and how you care for it must change too. - Rain and heat change from year to year. Let your choice of crops change too.
Try this: 1 Choose one place where you can see most of your land. Sit there 20 minutes at first light or at dusk, three times this week. 2 Write down everything you notice: birds, insects, where the sun sets first, where wind comes from, which plants look strong and which struggle. 3 Next time it rains heavily, walk your land. Follow the water. Mark where it enters, where it runs fast and where it gathers. 4 Draw a straight-on [plan view] map of your land as a bird sees it: house, paths, water flow, strong and weak areas. 5 Next to the map, write one thing each ethic asks of this place, and one principle you could start with.
Key points: permaculture, from permanent agriculture and permanent culture, was coined by Mollison and Holmgren in the mid-1970s. It designs farms that work like natural ecosystems: diverse, stable and resilient. Three ethics weigh all decisions: Earth care, people care, fair share. Holmgren's twelve principles turn those ethics into design, and the first is observing.
Keep that notebook close. The land has always been speaking; next, we learn to turn what it says into a plan.
