# 02 (blind back-translation of the isiXhosa)
Planning your farm or home.
You can plant food almost anywhere. But a garden in the wrong place costs you every day: extra steps to the tap, extra buckets climbing a hill, soil that leaves with every storm. Good design saves time, money and energy, and the farm thrives more each year.
Permaculture is a way of designing. It brings out all the strengths of the land, meeting the needs of plants, animals, water, buildings and people together. It is rare for the very rich small farmers to be the ones with more land than others.
By the end: draw a basic map of your land and mark what is already on it; read your site by water, slope, aspect (the side the slope faces) and access; divide your land into zones so that things you visit daily are near the home; use slope so gravity moves water, compost (umgquba) and manure for you.
Sector planning (natural forces entering your site, like sun, wind, fire and water), the fourth step, follows in the next chapter.
Step 1: Site survey. A mistake on paper costs nothing to fix. A mistake on the land costs sweat. So read the land before you change it.
Gather information and observe. Find out how the land was used before; its rain, frost, temperature and wind; what is on or near it (water, manure, mulch, building materials, helpers); and where you can sell what you grow. Then walk the land often, with an open mind, and do not rush to conclusions. Look at: where rainwater goes after a storm and where it gathers; how wind bends or shapes trees; which animals move across the land; places that feel warm, cold, wet or dry. Every season and storm teaches you something new.
Draw a base map. A base map is a drawing of your property seen from above. Show: boundaries, fences, main roads, small paths and gates; the house, tool sheds, animal pens and other buildings; how each part is now used: field, veld or garden; water: springs, streams, boreholes, tanks, taps and wet areas; big trees and stony areas; contour lines if you have them. A contour line joins points of the same height level. Trace a free satellite photo (e.g. from Google Earth), or if the plot is small, measure with a measuring string/tape and draw your own. Government 1:50 000 topographic maps cover all SA, with contour lines 20 m apart: they show the big shape of the land, not small shallow holes. Draw to scale, e.g. 1 cm on paper = 1 m on the land.
What to look for on your site. Water: the first thing to plan for (ch 7 discusses it in full). 1 Find your area's rain. Most of SA gets rain in summer; the Western Cape in winter. 2 Look at the catchment: the land above yours that sends water onto your land. 3 Look at the runoff, rainwater running across the land at the entrance, and its old signs: erosion gullies, bare patches and soil carried off by water. Runoff takes the soil and nutrients you have added. 4 Note the water storage you have, places where tanks, ponds or dams can be placed, and your backup sources: borehole, river, spring or tap. 5 Note the very dry and very wet places and plan how to irrigate seedlings with little effort.
Land shape and slope. The best place is slightly sloping, well-drained, facing the sun. Very steep land is hard to work and suffers erosion: soil carried off with water. Slope is usually given in percent: the fall in 100 m. A fall of 1 m in 10 m is a 10% slope. Table: 5% / 1 m in 20 m / about 3° / Easy for gardens and fields. 12% / 1 m in 8 m approx / about 7° / Protect soil with contour beds, grass strips or terraces. 32% / 1 m in 3 m approx / about 18° / Keep under permanent trees or grass. Bill Mollison advised keeping slopes steeper than 18 degrees under permanent trees. Remember this when choosing land to buy or rent.
Aspect: the side a slope or building faces. In SA the sun is in the north, so: North- and east-facing slopes get a lot of sun and warm early. Good for most crops. West-facing slopes get hot afternoon sun that can burn plants in summer. South-facing slopes are cold and shady in winter. Good for trees, and in dry areas keep moisture longer. No work can change where the sun rises. See where trees and buildings cast shadow; winter shadows are very long (ch 3).
Access: Main roads and paths almost always stay. Make new ones follow the contour. A path on the contour can be a swale wall, a dug ditch following the contour to catch rain (ch 7). Give each path the smallest fall, so rain goes to the swale or the tree basin instead of rolling down the path.
Step 2: Zone planning. The distance you walk decides what you care for. A far garden is forgotten; a garden at the kitchen door gets watered. Zone planning divides the farm into zones by how often you visit: daily things near the house, things visited less often farther away.
Table zone / what / how often visited / examples. 0 House, all the time: kitchen, seed storage place, tanks at the house. 1 Right beside the house, every day, often many times: kitchen garden, herbs, nursery, compost, small animals. 2 Near the house, most days: food forest, fruit trees, chickens, main food crops. 3 Main farming area, each week or when needed: field and market crops, orchard, pastures, dams. 4 Semi-wild, lightly managed, occasionally: firewood woodlot, pastures. 5 Wild, rarely, to watch and learn: natural veld or natural forest.
Zone 1 gives the highest yield on a small area. Put here annual vegetables (that live one season) and leafy greens you pick often, kitchen herbs, short fruit trees, small animals such as quail and rabbits, and small water stores. Cover every tank and water barrel, and fence or cover ponds, so that no child can fall in.
Zone 2 is bigger and less demanding. Its food forest (garden planted in layers like a natural forest) may include coppicing trees (cutting a tree on the ground so it re-sprouts): cutting a tree to a low stump so new sticks sprout. For firewood and sticks, plant umnga (Vachellia karroo), an indigenous tree (grows naturally here), rather than wattle or gum, which spread into natural grassland and rivers.
Zone 3 has livestock, field crops and farm dams. Zone 4 gives grazing, firewood and building timber. Zone 5 is natural land left wild, a refuge for birds and insects that help control harmful insects. Visit it to observe, learn and gather wild food with care.
No place has room for all zones. A town plot of about 1 000 square metres (a quarter of an acre) has room for zone 1 and bits of others; a corner left to birds and insects is a very small zone 5. On communal land, zones 4 and 5 may be shared grazing or shared veld. And zones are never neat circles: slope, water and paths turn them into different shapes.
Step 3: Slope planning. Water goes downhill free. Slope planning, after Bill Mollison's model, means looking at your land from the side and asking: where am I on the slope? Use slope well and gravity will do the carrying work. - Water high. Put dams and tanks above the house and garden, and gravity moves water without a pump. Every 10 m of height gives about 1 bar of pressure. - Trees on the high, very steep slopes slow runoff, help water soak in and hold soil. - Beds, paths and swales on the contour keep water in the soil. - Compost heaps and animal shelters open downhill, so compost and manure go down to the garden. - Drinking water stays clean. Keep manure, compost and animal pens downhill of boreholes, water holes and springs, and far from them. - Greywater goes to trees. Greywater, used water from washing, baths and dishwashing, goes by gravity to tree basins covered with mulch or to swales below the house, never reaching vegetables or other crops you eat uncooked (ch 7).
Try this: Start your base map this week. 1 Get a printed satellite photo of your land, or a big sheet of paper. Mark north. 2 Walk the boundaries. Draw boundaries, buildings, paths, taps, tanks, big trees. 3 After the next good rain, walk the land again. Mark with arrows where water goes and where it gathers. 4 Mark warm, cold, wet and dry places. 5 One day, count every trip you make to each place: tap, chickens, garden, firewood place. 6 With a pencil, draw uncertain zones on the map. Is the place you visit most too/very close to the house?
Key points: Watch your land through all seasons and put what you see on a base map. Read your site by water, slope, aspect and access. North- and east-facing slopes are the warmest. Zones 0 to 5 place things by how often you visit them. Put water high and let gravity move it. Keep manure away from drinking water, and greywater away from vegetables you eat uncooked.
Keep your map on the wall and add to it every season. After a few years it will be the most useful book about your land.
