# Blind back-translation (Phase A) of xh/09-balanced-ecology.md — written before opening English

L1 Building a balanced and productive nature.
L3 Nature farmed this land long before us without a bag of fertiliser. Grass catches sun and rain. Cattle and wild animals eat grass. Dung feeds soil, soil feeds grass. Nothing is thrown away and nobody sprays.
L5 We need not be scientists to farm this way. We need to understand nature's engine enough to connect our farm to its free power, without fighting it. The best tool is also the oldest: time spent watching.
L9-12 You will be able to: explain what an ecosystem is and how the pyramid of life works; use succession to heal bare or badly overgrazed soil; build homes on your farm for wild helpers such as frogs, lizards and owls; add diversity so your farm spreads risk and keeps pests under control.
L16 Ecology = studying how living things relate to each other and their surroundings. Ecosystem = group of plants, animals and soil life living together with the soil, water, air and sunlight around them. Can be small like a pond or compost heap, or big like Highveld grassland.
L18-22 Three working groups: Producers – green plants; catch sunlight and feed everything else. Consumers – animals, from aphids to cattle and people; eat plants or eat each other. Decomposers – fungi, bacteria, earthworms and other soil life; rot dead plants, dung and bodies and return nutrients to soil.
L24-29 Four processes keep it going: water cycle – rain enters soil or runs off downhill, plants use it, it returns to the air. Nutrient cycle – nutrients go from soil to plant, then to animal, and return to soil via dung and rot. Energy flow – plants catch sun's energy and pass it up the food chain. Energy is not recycled: green leaves must catch new every day. Change over time – mixture of plants and animals changes year by year (see Succession below).
L31 When these work well the land gives us free clean water, fertile soil, pollination, pest control and pasture.
L35-42 Think of farm life as a pyramid. At the base are plants, which catch sun's energy; they stand on the soil and its decomposers. Above them plant-eating animals: caterpillars, aphids, mice, cattle. Higher: predators (animals that eat others) that eat them, like ladybirds, spiders, birds and lizards. At the top few top predators like owls, eagles and jackals [impungutye = jackal/fox]. Each level up has fewer living things because energy is lost at each step. Lots of grass needed to feed one cow, lots of mice to feed one owl.
L44-48 What this means on the farm: 1 Don't try to kill all pests. If you spray all aphids with poison, ladybirds and birds that eat them starve and leave. When aphids return nothing stops them. 2 Feed at the base. Whole pyramid stands on plants and soil, so soil health decides how much life, and how much harvest, your land can carry. 3 Give predators a home (see Habitat below).
L52-56 Succession = change of plants and animals in a place, step by step over time. It's how the land heals itself. After ploughing, overgrazing, fire or flood: 1 Pioneer plants come first, usually annual weeds farmers don't like. Grow fast, cover bare soil and hold it. When they die they feed it. 2 Stronger plants follow: perennials, grasses, herbs and small shrubs, many of them legumes adding nitrogen to soil. They protect small plants and feed insects and birds. 3 Long-lasting community of living things comes last. Soil goes deeper and richer, soil has much life and many kinds of life.
L58-67 Not every place ends up forest. Books from rainy countries say all land ends up as forest. Not in Southern Africa. Natural forest covers under 1% of SA, in small pieces where rain is high and frost seldom, like Garden Route and wet eastern escarpment. Most of our land ends as something different, old and equally rich in species: Grassland covers much of Highveld, Drakensberg and Lesotho. Frost, fire and grazing keep trees out. These ancient grasslands have many rare plants and hold water that feeds our big rivers. Savanna (bushveld), grass with scattered trees, covers much of north and east. Fire, grazing and browsing keep balance between grass and trees. Fynbos in winter-rainfall Western Cape needs fire about every 10–15 years. Many fynbos plants such as proteas release and germinate seed only after fire. Karoo naturally dry shrubland. If you keep fire out and graze heavily, grassland and savanna thicken with thorny shrubs. This bush encroachment already covers over 7 million hectares in SA and costs pasture and water.
L69 Warning: Planting trees in grassland or fynbos doesn't improve it. It damages a rare ecosystem that supplies water. Plant trees around the homestead and in food forest, and leave your veld as grassland, savanna or fynbos.
L73-76 Using succession on your farm: 1 Pioneers first. On bare poor soil sow strong cover crops and green manures such as cowpea (plants sown to enrich soil). Plant demanding crops after them. 2 Build food forest in stages. Fill between small fruit trees with fast-growing supporting plants, cut them as mulch as trees take the space. 3 Choose supporting plants that stay where planted. Many "nitrogen-fixing" trees in foreign books, like black wattle, Leucaena and red sesbania, spread uncontrolled in SA. Plant pigeon pea instead, and native umnga (sweet thorn, Vachellia karroo) where suitable. Umnga spreads in overgrazed pasture so keep it within your planted areas. 4 Respect your place's end-point. In grassland areas your Zone 5, wild area, should be healthy grassland, not planted forest.
L80-82 Farming against or with nature: Most modern farming keeps soil at the first stage of having nothing: plough, spray, fertilise, plant one crop, repeat. Nature covers bare soil with weeds to start healing, the farmer sprays them. This takes time, fuel and chemicals, leaves soil unstable, poor in life and quick to be damaged by drought, pests and erosion. Permaculture goes the other direction. We build the soil and diversity so the farm becomes richer and more stable each year, with enough life to control pests and diseases.
L86-88 Habitat = home of a plant or animal: place giving food, water, shelter, space and a safe place to breed. A frog needs water to lay eggs, tall grass and stones around pond to hide from birds and snakes, plus insects to eat. That pond is its habitat. Loss of habitat is one of main reasons plants and animals die out. Think of habitat as a job advert. Farm has many jobs: eating pests, pollinating flowers, recycling waste. Instead of paying to have it done we advertise for wild helpers, and the advert must offer food, water and safe place to sleep.
L90-94 1 Give water. Bird bath or small pond attracts birds, frogs and dragonflies, which eat many insects. Put stones or sticks in water so bees can drink without drowning. Fence ponds or cover with strong mesh so small children don't fall in. Frogs, tadpoles and dragonfly larvae eat mosquito larvae; don't add mosquitofish since they eat tadpoles too. 2 Leave wild places. Keep any veld or wetland on your land. This is your Zone 5. If you have none, return one corner to native plants. Perhaps the most important thing you can do for habitat, because local plants feed more local insects and birds than non-local. 3 Plant a living fence. Dense lines along fences and field edges: Cape honeysuckle, plumbago, sand olive, Kei apple, karee and other Searsia. They give shelter, food and nest places and break wind. 4 Build a pile of stones and logs away from house and where children play. Lizards bask on warm stones and eat many insects. Logs shelter beetles, frogs and fungi. 5 Put an owl box on a pole, tree or building at least 3 m above ground, out of reach of cats. A family of barn owls can eat 1 000 or more mice and rats a year. Owls die from eating poisoned mice, so if you want owls, stop using rat poison. Where owls are feared as a bad omen talk to neighbours about the work owls do.
L98 Biodiversity = variety of life in a place: many kinds of plants, animals, insects, fungi and soil life, and differences within each kind. Walk into healthy veld or fynbos to see it. A pine plantation shows the opposite: hectare after hectare of one tree, very little grows beneath. Many pines spread into grassland and fynbos and use lots of water.
L102-104 Diversity helps: spreads risk (if one crop fails because of drought, pests or disease, others still give harvest); helps control pests (mixing plants feeds and protects predators that eat pests, and pests find it hard to find their favourite plant among many. Diverse gardens aren't at great risk of serious pest outbreaks, but will never be completely without pests); builds healthy soil (different roots and plant remains feed wide range of soil life).
L108-112 To build it: 1 polyculture: many crops together in one bed or field instead of one in long rows. 2 plant perennials (fruit trees, herbs, perennial vegetables) beside annuals. 3 plant food forest, garden with layers from tall trees to groundcovers and root crops. 4 keep mixed animals, each with a job: chickens eat pests and cattle dung feeds compost. 5 grow flowers, including native, to feed bees and other helpful insects all year.
L114 When what one part produces feeds another (dung to soil, crop residue to animals, pests to birds), the farm starts to look after itself.
L118-124 Try: make habitat corner this week. 1 pick an unused corner next to vegetable beds, at least 2 m by 2 m, away from where children play. 2 sink old basin, cut drum or plastic-lined tyre into ground. Fill with water and surround with stones. Put a few stones or sticks inside so insects can climb out. Cover with strong mesh if small children are about. 3 build small stone pile on sunny (north) side and log pile on shady side. 4 plant or leave native plants and tall grass around. 5 visit at dawn and dusk once a week. Write down every creature you see.
L128-131 Key points: pyramid stands on plants and soil. If you kill all pests you starve predators that help you. Succession is how land heals; in SA it usually ends in grassland, savanna or fynbos, not forest. Water, wild corners, living fence, stone pile and owl boxes bring wild helpers to work on your farm. Diversity spreads risk and keeps pests under control.
L133 After a month sit beside the pond and count what has come into it. Each new visitor is a worker you didn't have to hire.
