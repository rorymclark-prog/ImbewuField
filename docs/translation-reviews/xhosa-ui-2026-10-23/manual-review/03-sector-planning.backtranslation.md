# Sector planning
Your land is not an island. Wind, fire, sun, water and cold air all come from beyond your fence, and will keep coming long after you plant your first tree. You cannot stop them. You can choose where they meet your farm and what stands there when they arrive.
The farmer who knows where the hot wind comes from plants the windbreak in the right place at the start. The farmer who does not know, plants it twice.
By the end of this chapter you will be able to: find true north by compass, by sunrise or by midday shadow; draw a sector map of the forces that cut across your land; place windbreaks, firebreaks and green barriers where wind and fire usually come from; use the sun angle and the flow of cold air to place beds, trees and house; learn your climate and choose crops and protectors that suit it.

## Drawing the sector map
A sector [?"lamandla" - "of-the-energies" with wrong concord] is the energies / energy entering your land from outside: sun, wind, fire, water and floods, frost and cold air, good and bad views, wildlife paths, noise and dust.
The sector map draws each one as a slice, like a slice of cake, pointing at your home from the side it comes from.
1. Take your base map (chapter 2), mark the centre of your home or garden.
2. Mark true north.
3. For each energy draw a slice from the side it comes from. The hot, dry winter wind may be a slice from the north-west.
4. Write the time of year beside each slice. Most of these energies change with the seasons.
Then judge, for each, whether you accept it, block it or channel it. Cool summer wind: let it into the house; cold winter wind: block it. The map shows where the windbreak (a line of trees or shrubs that slows the wind) and the firebreak (a strip of bare ground that cannot burn) should go, which views must stay open, and where the winter sun sets.

## Finding north
Every sector depends on north. Here are three easy ways to find it.
### By compass
The compass shows magnetic north, not true north. In South Africa magnetic north is west of true north by about 20 to 28 degrees: in 2025 about 21 in Johannesburg, 27 in Cape Town and 28 in Durban. So true north is to the right-hand side (east) of the needle, a little. Many phone compass apps show true north.
### By sunrise
Watch the sun rise: north is on your left, south on your right. But the sunrise changes. It rises exactly east only around 21 March and 23 September. In December it rises about 26 to 29 degrees south of east, and in June about 26 to 29 degrees north of east.
### By your midday shadow
At midday the sun is north almost everywhere in southern Africa, so your shadow points south. Sun-noon falls between 11:45 and 13:00 by the watch, later in the west. Only in the far north of Limpopo in midsummer may the sun be a little south.
> Tip: Once you've found north, mark it in the garden with a painted stone or a pole, and you will never need to find it again.

## Wind
The sun heats land, sea and rocks unevenly. Warm air rises, cool air sinks, and wind moves from a high-pressure place to a low-pressure place. That is wind.
Wind dries the land and plants, breaks plants and trees, blows away topsoil (the dark, living top layer) and pushes fires forward. It drives water pumps, cools the house in summer and carries maize's fertilising powder (pollen).
### Winds of South Africa
Table (Wind | Where | When | What it does):
- Berg wind | coast and escarpment of Western Cape, Southern Cape, Eastern Cape and KwaZulu-Natal | mostly winter, often one or two days before a cold front | Very hot, dry wind from inland toward the sea. Very high fire danger.
- Cold front winds | strongest in Western Cape, then elsewhere in the country | winter | North-west winds bring rain to the Western Cape. After the front, cold south winds bring frost inland and snow on high ground.
- South-easter ("Cape Doctor") | Cape Town and south-west Western Cape | summer | Strong, dry, sometimes gusty wind that dries the land, damages crops and spreads fire.
- August winds | Highveld and inland | end of winter and early spring | Dry winds, sometimes gusty, over dry natural veld. Dust and very high fire danger.
### Find your main wind directions
1. Watch all year: bent trees, smoke, dust, washing on the line.
2. Set up a wind vane or windsock, a cloth tube on a pole.
3. Ask old people nearby which winds are worst and when they come.
4. Name each wind by where it comes from: a north-west wind comes from the north-west.
5. Draw each on your sector map with the season, and plant windbreaks across the damaging winds (chapter 6).

## Fire
Natural grasslands, savanna and fynbos need fire at the right times. But uncontrolled grass fire kills people and livestock, and burns grazing, crops, houses and fences.
In areas with summer rain, fire season begins about May and goes to October, worst at end of winter and start of spring before the rain. In the winter-rainfall Western Cape it begins about December and goes to March. In Southern Cape and KwaZulu-Natal, winter berg-wind days bring suddenly very high fire danger.
### How fire behaves
Fire can come from anywhere, but on most land it mostly comes from one side: your fire sector. Usually that is the side of the hot dry wind, and your neighbours will know it.
Fire climbs a slope much faster than it descends. Hold a burning match head-down and watch the flame climb. A usual rule: fire speed doubles for each 10° of uphill slope, so on a 20° slope it spreads about four times faster than on level ground. A house high up on a steep grassy slope is at greatest risk. Place your protectors below the slope [?"ezantsi kwethambeka" - "down the slope/at the bottom"], and in the fire sector.
Protect people and the house first, then livestock, tool sheds, stored animal feed and fruit trees.
### Ways to reduce fire risk
1. Reduce what can burn. In the fire sector and outside buildings keep grass short and remove dry leaves and dead trees. Keep firewood, hay and thatch roof away from the house.
2. Make firebreaks. Remove a strip of everything that can burn. Roads, ploughed land and dams also work as firebreaks. Burn a firebreak only on a cool, windless day when fire danger is low, never do it alone, and have enough people, water and fire beaters to hold it.
3. Plant green plants that burn little. No plant is fire-proof, but fleshy, watery leaves with little oil or resin burn little: aloes, spekboom (Portulacaria afra, which does not like heavy frost), Cotyledon, vygies, sour fig, bietou and num-num.
4. Keep oily trees away from buildings. Pines, gums and wattles burn fiercely.
5. Keep water ready: a tank and a hosepipe by the house, plus fire beaters or a knapsack sprayer.
6. Do many things at once. A watered garden or orchard around the house is a green barrier, and a strip of low-burning plants slows wind and fire.

## Sunlight
Most vegetables and fruit need at least six hours of direct sun a day, and morning sun is of great value. But bare ground under strong sun bakes dry and its life suffers: earthworms go deep or die. Keep soil covered with mulch (layer of dry grass, leaves or straw) or with plants, and give tomatoes and peppers afternoon shade in very hot places.
### Sun angles
South Africa is 22 to 35 degrees south of the Equator. At noon the sun is north: high in summer, low in winter, so winter shadows are very long.
Table (Place | noon sun height midwinter (21 June) | noon sun height midsummer (21 December) | shadow of 2 m wall at noon midwinter): Polokwane (24° S) 43°, almost straight overhead, about 2.2 m; Johannesburg (26° S) 40°, 87°, about 2.4 m; Durban (30° S) 37°, 84°, about 2.7 m; Cape Town (34° S) 33°, 80°, about 3.1 m.
In winter a garden bed on the south side of a building or big tree gets little sun. So put tall trees and buildings on the south side of the garden and short plants on the north.
### Using the sun
- Trees and vines that lose leaves in winter drop their leaves in winter. Plant them north of the house for summer shade and winter sun: grapes, fig, white stinkwood and wild pear.
- Thermal mass is heavy material like bricks, stones, soil or water that soaks up heat in the day and gives it back at night. Put it where winter sun arrives, such as behind north-facing windows.
- Roof edges. Make the north roof edge wide enough to shade windows from the high summer sun, but short enough to let the low winter sun in.

## Climate
Climate is the average movement of local weather over many years. More than anything else, it shows what you can farm.
South Africa is a dry country. It gets about 464 mm rain a year on average, compared with the world average of 860 mm, and gets drier from east to west. Most of the country, with Lesotho, Eswatini and Zimbabwe, gets summer rain, mostly from about November to March, and winter is dry. The south-west of the Western Cape gets winter rain, mostly about May to August, and hot dry summers. The Southern Cape coast gets rain all year. Height above sea level matters as much as place: the Highveld often has frost in winter, while the KwaZulu-Natal coast and Lowveld have almost no frost.
Learn your own: when the first rain comes, and when the first and last frost fall.
> Tip: Keep a rain gauge and a thermometer, record rain and lowest temperature every day. After a few years you'll know your climate better than any map.
### Microclimates
A microclimate is a small place whose weather differs from the land around it. Each lets you try something you couldn't grow before. Find them by watching where frost lingers longest and where plants flower early. Low parts of valleys and riverbanks are cold. Corners behind walls are warm and out of the wind: plant tomatoes against a north-facing wall.
To make warm microclimates in cold places:
- Build in the thermal belt. On clear, windless nights cold air flows downslope like water and collects in low places called frost pockets. The mid-slope belt stays warm. Put the house and frost-tender plants there.
- Plant trees upslope. A line of trees above the house slows and diverts the cold air coming down at night.
- Use water. Even a small pond or tank near the house evens out temperature.
- Face the house north, and put a compost heap (decaying plant material) inside a plastic tunnel to get warmth in winter.
### Warm, temperate and dry climates
Warm areas, such as the Lowveld, KZN coast and hot Limpopo valleys, have no frost or little. Grow avocado, banana, mango, litchi, papaya, macadamia and citrus.
Temperate areas have warm summers and cold winters, often with frost. Most deciduous fruit trees need winter cold, called "chill", to flower and fruit well; in warmer areas choose varieties needing little chill. Grow apple, pear, plum, peach, apricot, almond, fig, grape, pomegranate, persimmon, black mulberry and pecan.
Dry areas, such as the Karoo and much of the Northern Cape, get far less than 500 mm per year, and some places under 200 mm. Sorghum, pearl millet and cowpeas do better than maize here.

## Water flow (hydrology)
Hydrology is the story of water on your land. Before planning, find out which water you can use (streams, springs, boreholes, taps), your rainy season and how much rain per year, whether your land drains water well and holds it, how much rain you can catch from your roof, and what climate change may do to your rain.
Start with the roof. 1 mm of rain on 1 square metre gives 1 litre of water. A 50 square metre roof in a place with 500 mm rain a year can catch up to 25,000 litres a year, not counting some that is lost.
These numbers set the limits of what your land can grow.

## Frost
Frost comes most winters in most of the interior: Highveld, Free State, Karoo, Eastern Cape and central parts of KwaZulu-Natal, and Lesotho. It also stays in cold valleys in some places. Most of the coast and the Lowveld get little or none.
On clear, windless nights the ground and plants lose their heat to the open sky and become colder than the air. Water vapour freezes on them like white ice: white frost. Cold air slides down and collects, so frost is worst in low places. When the air is very dry, plants can freeze without any ice at all. This is called black frost, and gives no warning: tomorrow [next morning] the leaves and stems turn black and die.
Frost can stunt a young fruit tree's growth for many years. Prepare before winter.
1. Cover plants on frost nights with frost cloth, old sheets or straw, reaching the ground. Remove the cover in the morning.
2. Shelter the garden with a windbreak or living fence, but leave a gap on the lower side so cold air can get out rather than collect.
3. Divert cold air around the garden with a horseshoe-shaped fence on the uphill side, with open ground [?] facing down.
4. Make sun traps. A north-facing wall or curved fence holds warm air, and stones or water drums around plants keep the day's heat.
5. Plant nurse trees. A light canopy (leaf roof) of early, short-lived trees protects young fruit trees.
6. Mulch carefully. Mulch protects roots but stops the soil giving up its heat at night, so very short plants may get more frost above thick mulch. On beds at risk of frost, water the soil by day before a frost night: wet soil keeps more heat.
7. Use guilds. A guild is a group of plants planted together to help each other, including fighting frost (chapter 6).

## Climate change
Climate change is a long-term change in the state of the climate, caused mostly by human activities: more heat, changing rain, stronger weather, and water shortage.
Southern Africa is warming faster than the world average, some inland places about twice. Heat waves are more common, more droughts and more fire-danger days are expected. Climate change made the Cape Town drought of 2015 to 2017 about three times more likely.
### Designing for a changing climate
We can no longer rely on a normal year. So we design for a bad year, and build resilience: the strength to withstand shock and rise again.
1. Plant many different crops, so that the failure of one doesn't ruin you.
2. Choose tough crops, including indigenous ones that grow here naturally, and perennials that last many seasons.
3. Keep tough local livestock breeds: indigenous chickens, goats and Nguni cattle.
4. Plant windbreaks and store water to reduce the size of dangerous weather.
5. Keep the soil covered with cover crops and mulch, and help rain soak in with swales, level ditches dug across the slope.
6. Keep your surplus. Dry it, put it in plenty of vinegar, or make jam and chutney. Home-bottling low-acid foods like beans, pumpkin or meat without a tested method can cause botulism, deadly food poisoning.
7. Save your seed so it suits your soil, and share it through a community seed bank.
8. Move grazing animals often, and give the veld time to rest.
9. Build outside places prone to flooding. Keep firebreaks and water ready, and keep food, seed and animal feed for hard times.

## Try this
Draw your first sector map this week.
1. On a sunny day, stand a straight stick upright in open ground. About midday, when its shadow is shortest, the shadow points south.
2. Roughly draw your home, garden and boundary on paper, with an arrow pointing north.
3. Walk the land. Mark where strong winds come from, where past fires came from, where water enters after rain, and where frost stays longest.
4. Draw each energy as a slice pointing at your home, with the season.
5. Ask an older neighbour about the worst wind and fire they remember, and add them.
6. Circle the energy that troubles you most. That is where your first windbreak, firebreak or fence will go.

## Key points
- The sector map shows where sun, wind, fire, water, frost, views and noise come from, so you can accept, block or channel each.
- A compass in South Africa points about 20 to 28 degrees west of true north; the midday sun is north.
- Fire climbs a slope fast: put fire protection below the slope and in the fire sector.
- The low winter sun comes from the north, and cold air collects in low places. Plan beds, trees and house looking at both.
- Southern Africa is warming fast. Respond with variety, perennials, covered soil, stored water and saved seed.
Walk your land in all seasons holding the map. Each season will add a slice you had not seen.
