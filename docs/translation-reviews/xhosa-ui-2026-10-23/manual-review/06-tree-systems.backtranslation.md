# 06-tree-systems — blind back-translation (Phase A, written from the isiXhosa only; line numbers = xh file)

L1 Tree systems
L3 Trees are a long-time game. A vegetable bed feeds you for one part of the year. A well-planted tree feeds your children and their children with fruit, firewood, fodder (food for livestock), shade and medicine, and you never need to dig or sow it again.
L5 Trees also do silent work. Their roots hold the soil and reach water vegetables cannot reach. Their leaves fall and feed the soil. Their branches break the wind and shelter birds that eat insects that damage your crops. A forest is built on a forest: each plant makes the soil better for the next.
L7 Three tree systems carry most of this work on a small farm: food forest (garden planted in layers like a natural forest); windbreak (a belt of trees and shrubs that slows the wind); and a single well-planted tree. All plants recommended here are indigenous (grow naturally in southern Africa) or are not on the list of declared invasive plants in South Africa. Many trees praised in overseas permaculture books are invasive species here: plants that come from elsewhere and spread out of control. Buy by Latin name.
L9-14 By the end you can: choose a food-forest site and choose fruit trees that suit your frost and winter cold; plant a food forest in a suitable order, starting with first-to-establish (pioneer) nitrogen-fixing plants; plan a windbreak across the dangerous wind using indigenous trees and shrubs; plant a single tree so it stands with deep roots and survives its first dry season.
L16 Food forests
L18 Vegetables and main food crops ask a lot of us: digging, sowing and watering, from time to time. Because the soil is turned and replanted every year, you never get past the first stage of succession (natural change step by step from bare ground to grass, shrubs and trees). Annual plants also suffer badly from insects and dry periods.
L20 A food forest is a garden planted like a natural forest, in layers, from tall trees to low plants and root crops. It is not a collection of trees standing on bare ground. Most of its plants are perennials (plants that live more than two years). Once established, a food forest:
 - does not need replanting every season of the year
 - grows deep roots that reach water and nutrients that vegetables without deep roots cannot reach
 - keeps the soil covered all year
 - feeds itself with its fallen leaves and its pruned branches
 - gives homes to birds and insects that keep pests under control
L28 From one food forest you can harvest fruit, nuts, berries, herbs, spices, firewood, poles, fodder, root crops and mulch (grass or leaves that cover the soil so it stays moist and protected). The work that remains is pruning by season, chop and drop (cutting plants and leaving them on the ground to become mulch), filling in new plants, and moving the animal tractor (a mobile chicken house) here and there. Young trees still need water in their first dry season or two, and in dry areas most fruit trees need some water each year.
L30 People have long farmed like this. In the east of the Amazon, people looked after the forest to get food plants for at least 4,500 years, and the forest is still full of the edible trees they chose.
L32 Where to plant a food forest
L34 In many parts of South Africa, forest is not the end of nature in succession. Natural grassland, fynbos, Karoo shrubland and savanna are ancient systems, complete in themselves, not forest waiting to appear. Trees planted into healthy natural grass or fynbos crowd out scarce plants and use a lot of water.
 1 Plant on land that is already disturbed (damaged before): old fields, bare yards, eroded land, land around the homestead, or land cleared of invasive plants.
 2 Leave natural grazing, natural grass, fynbos and wetland as they are while they are healthy.
 3 Keep trees away from streams and wet places, unless they are indigenous riverbank trees.
L40 Two kinds of food forest
L42 Permaculture plans a farm in zones (areas arranged by how often you visit), by how often you visit each part.
L44 Home food forest (Zone 2). Near the house, where you pass once or twice a day. It feeds the family: fruit, nuts, berries, herbs for cooking and medicine, and annuals you sow yourselves. It holds many kinds. Where space is small, plant only a few big trees.
L46 Large food forest or orchard (Zone 3). Further away, with bigger trees, for selling. A permaculture orchard is a simpler food forest with a few kinds, planted over a bigger area and usually irrigated. It still holds nitrogen fixers (plants that add nitrogen from the air to the soil) and flowers for insects. Plant it in strips with grass between, which also makes harvesting easy.
L48 Coppicing
L50 Coppicing means cutting a tree close to the ground so it sends many new shoots. You cut the shoots again every few years for firewood, fence poles, stakes and tool handles, and the tree keeps living. Shoots grow much faster than a new tree from seed, because the roots are already big. Not all trees resprout. Wild olive (Latin) is an indigenous tree that resprouts well after cutting, and a coppice patch of it in Zone 2 gives firewood every year.
L52 Designing a food forest
L54 Design first on paper. A tree planted in the wrong place wastes years.
 1 Write down your needs. What do your family, your animals and local wild animals need? Food, firewood, fodder, medicine, shade?
 2 Match plants to your climate. Choose indigenous and overseas plants suited to your rain, frost and heat. Add a few that only just tolerate your place. As the climate changes, these give you protection.
 3 Mix kinds: fruit, nuts, berries, nitrogen fixers, medicine plants, flowers for bees and birds, and mulch plants.
 4 Spread the harvest across the year. In South Africa avocados come mostly from about autumn (March) to winter (September), citrus in autumn and winter, while grapes and peaches ripen in summer and almond ripens at the end of summer. Most fruits come in early, mid and late varieties. Plant some of each.
 5 Think of nutrition. Plan so the family eats different fruit and vegetables all year.
 6 Match the rootstock to your soil. Fruit trees are made by grafting (joining a good kind of fruit to the roots of another plant). The rootstock is that root part, and different rootstocks suit different soils. Ask the nursery which rootstock suits your soil.
 7 Choose indigenous supporting plants first. For nitrogen fixers, groundcovers and wildlife plants, choose indigenous kinds first.
L64 Tip: Before you write your plant list, walk slowly through the nearest natural grassland or forest. Notice which groundcovers grow in shade and which in sun, and which trees are first to establish on disturbed ground. Natural grassland is the best teacher of what will grow well on your land.
L66 Match fruit trees to your climate
L68 The biggest question for fruit trees in South Africa is frost.
Table (Climate | Fruit that usually grows well | Notes):
 - Cold Highveld, Free State, Lesotho and high parts of the Eastern Cape (severe frost) | apple, pear, quince, peach, nectarine, plum, apricot, fig, persimmon, grape, pecan | These leaf-losing trees need a cold winter. Late frost destroys the flowers of apricot, almond and early peach, so plant them on a slope, not in a frost pocket (low place where cold air stays).
 - Western Cape (winter rain, dry summer) | apple, pear, plum, apricot, peach, fig, grape, almond, carob, citrus, pomegranate | Needs water in summer. Almond and carob suit dry summers.
 - Lowveld and frost-free coast (Limpopo and Mpumalanga Lowveld, KZN coast) | mango, banana, papaya, avocado, litchi, citrus, black sapote, jackalberry | Most leaf-losing fruit trees do not get enough cold in winter here. Choose kinds that need little chill.
 - Light frost (middle parts of KZN, parts of Gauteng) | citrus, avocado, fig, pecan, persimmon, peach and plum needing little chill | Fuerte avocado tolerates cold more than Hass. Protect young citrus and avocado from frost for two or three winters.
L77 Fruit varieties from leaf-losing trees are sold by their chill requirement: how many cold hours they need in winter. Ask for varieties suited to your area.
L79 Planting patterns and spacing
L81 Plan the forest in groups, hedge, straight lines, or strips of plants between the rows of trees. Choose what suits your place. Whatever the pattern, each tree needs sun. In South Africa the sun is in the north, so put tall evergreen trees in the south, where their shade falls outside the garden, or among leaf-losing trees in winter. Use the edge lit by sun for sun-loving herbs, as a natural forest does.
 1 Measure your area. 2 Find the full width of each tree's canopy: the width of its leafy crown when grown. 3 Draw a plan on paper, or mark the place with stakes. 4 Leave space. A tree with a 4 m canopy needs about 6 to 7 m to the next tree, unless you are planting a hedge. The middle space holds paths, nitrogen fixers and small plants. 5 Make your shopping list from the plan.
L89 Planting a food forest
L91 A natural forest takes decades, even hundreds of years, to grow. We speed it up by planting pioneers first.
L93 Think of lighting a fire. Dry grass lights twigs, twigs light branches, and in the end the big logs burn. In a food forest, fast-growing pioneers build up the soil's fertility first, and fruit trees take over as they grow.
L95 The most useful pioneers are legumes (plants of the bean and pea family). Most fix nitrogen: their roots work with bacteria to take nitrogen from the air and add it to the soil. They also make lots of biomass (leaves and branches) for mulch.
Table (Plant | Use | Notes):
 - Sweet thorn (umnga) | nitrogen fixer, fodder, firewood, gum | Indigenous and very tough. It becomes crowded/dense in natural grazing that is overgrazed, so plant it in your food forest, do not plant it into healthy natural grass.
 - Common hook thorn | N fixer, fodder, firewood, fence | Indigenous; in summer-rain areas.
 - Keurboom | fast-growing N fixer, bee flowers | Indigenous in the south and Western Cape; does not live long.
 - Pigeon pea | N fixer, edible peas, chop and drop | A small shrub that lives a few years; killed by severe frost.
 - Cowpea | N-fixing groundcover, food | Summer annual.
 - Lablab bean | climbing N fixer, fodder | Summer; delicate in frost.
 - Lupin | winter N-fixing groundcover | Suits Western Cape winter. Bitter lupin seed is poison, so eat or feed livestock sweet kinds only.
L107 Wattle, leucaena and sesbania are used for this job in many overseas books, but they are invasive here. The indigenous thorn trees above do the same job.
L109 Planting steps
 1 Place stakes showing the plants. Measure distances by canopy size, mark each place with a stake or a little lime. Leave big distances between big trees.
 2 Mark everything. Put a name label next to each important plant. You will forget what is where.
 3 Dig all the holes and planting basins.
 4 Prepare compost and mulch. Compost is plant and animal waste rotted into dark, soft food of the soil.
 5 Start by planting big, long-living trees, then nitrogen-fixing trees.
 6 Plant medium trees between the big ones.
 7 Plant small shrubs and herbs about 1 m from the main fruit trees.
 8 Plant perennial root crops, then perennial groundcovers between the basins.
 9 Sow annual legume groundcovers last: cowpea in summer, lupin in winter. Just before sowing, wet the seed a little and mix it with the right inoculant (powder of nitrogen-fixing bacteria for that legume, sold with the seed or at farm-supply shops). Keep seed with inoculant out of the sun. Spread it thickly and cover lightly with mulch.
 10 Water everything well.
L122 How a food forest grows
L124 A food forest passes through many stages before it settles. We can think of two phases.
L126 Phase 1: establishment (years 0 to 5). The first fast-growing legumes lead. They fill open places, hold back weeds with shade and protect young fruit trees. In the rainy season, when they are big enough, chop and drop: cut their branches back heavily and leave what is cut on the ground as mulch. It rots and feeds the soil and the fungi forest trees need. Some fine roots and root nodules of the legume also die after cutting and release a little nitrogen. You will not yet harvest many fruits, but after the first year there are berries, leafy vegetables you sow, root crops, herbs and firewood from what was cut.
L128 Phase 2: established (year 5 onward). Grafted trees can bear fruit in their first three years; others only in year 5 to 7. Most food forests start maturing about year 5.
 1 As fruit trees fill in, cut the pioneers back harder each year, until only a few long-living nitrogen-fixing trees remain.
 2 Let chickens, pigs or other animals eat in between for a short time. Protect the stems of young trees from goats and pigs, which strip the bark.
 3 Prune to let light in where needed, and use the safe microclimates (small areas with their own climate) that have already formed.
 4 Start coppicing coppice trees for firewood.
L135 Wild animals come on their own. Birds, lizards and insects come in as the layers close, and fruit, nuts and berries increase year after year.
L137 Layers of a food forest
L139 A food forest is usually described by seven layers. Choose plants for each layer to suit your climate. The understorey is the layer of small trees and shrubs below the tallest trees.
Table: 1 Tall trees (canopy): the tallest trees. Plant only a few in a small food forest. Examples: wild plum, Cape chestnut, pecan; jackalberry and black sapote in hot frost-free areas. 2 Small trees (understorey): most fruit trees, kept small by pruning. Apple, pear, quince, peach, plum, apricot, fig, persimmon, citrus, avocado, carob; indigenous Kei apple, sour plum, buffalo thorn, monkey plum, red ivory. 3 Shrubs: woody plants about 0.5 to 3 m. Forest num-num, pigeon pea, wilde als, wild rosemary, spekboom, krantz aloe, rosemary, lavender, Cape gooseberry. 4 Herbs and vegetables: plants with soft stems, mostly annuals (live one season of the year). Amaranth ("imbuya") of vegetables and grain, okra, [amazimba], sunflower, kale, spinach, broad bean, basil, sage, oregano, yarrow, French marigold. 5 Groundcover: low plants covering the soil. Pumpkin, tsamma melon, sour fig, wild garlic, pennywort, strawberry, thyme, nasturtium, mint in a contained garden bed. 6 Roots: roots, tubers and bulbs. Sweet potato (ibhatata), potatoes, onion, carrot, beetroot; turmeric in warm frost-free areas. 7 Climbers: plants that climb trees, fences and supports. Grape, calabash, lablab bean.
L151 For berries, plant Kei apple, cross-berry or num-num rather than planting elderberry or white mulberry, which are invasive here. Birds carry fig seed into natural grassland, so remove fig seedlings you find far from the garden. For medicine, pepperbark suits frost-free gardens; buy plants grown in a nursery, because that tree has been stripped in the wild for its bark.
L153 Windbreaks
L155 Wind dries plants, breaks young trees and carries away uncovered soil. In the Karoo, Western Cape and the central high plateau it is one of the hardest things a garden faces.
L157 A windbreak is a line or belt of trees, shrubs or tall grass that slows the wind and protects crops, animals and houses. There is no single design; each place needs its own. A good windbreak:
 - slows strong, fast winds and stops wind erosion (soil blown by wind or washed away by water)
 - protects young fruit trees, the food forest and vegetables
 - gives fodder at its edges (keep animals out while it is young) and firewood from pruning
 - brings up nutrients with deep roots, which return on fallen leaves
 - feeds and shelters bees, insects and birds
 - gives some fruit and nuts, though wind reduces the harvest, so see fruit as a bonus
L166 How to plan a windbreak
 1 Find the dangerous wind. Look where the strong and dry winds come from in each season. In the Western Cape, for example, the south-easter blows in summer and the north-wester brings winter rain.
 2 Plant across it, in a shape standing across the dangerous wind, on the side of the garden the wind comes from.
 3 Make it long, without gaps. Wind speeds up through gaps and ends.
 4 Let a little wind pass. A windbreak as dense as half a wall protects a longer distance than a solid wall, which makes the wind fall and swirl behind it.
 5 Plant several rows: tall trees at the back (on the side the wind comes from), medium trees in the middle, dense shrubs in front.
 6 Leave room for light. The protected area reaches downwind (the side the wind goes to) many times the height of the windbreak, usually 10 to 20 times, so it need not stand right beside the garden. Tree roots compete with crops for water. Because the sun is in the north, a windbreak on the north side covers the garden with shade; on the south side it casts little shade.
L175 Beefwood (Casuarina), gum and pine are common farm windbreaks, but they spread into natural grassland and streams. Indigenous trees and shrubs do this job and also feed wild animals.
L177 Windbreaks and fire
L179 A green, irrigated windbreak can slow a fire, but it is not a firebreak (a cleared strip a natural-grass fire cannot cross), and it can carry fire to your house. Aromatic plants such as wilde als, wild rosemary, rosemary, lavender and camphor bush are full of highly flammable oil. Plants that store water in their leaves (succulents) such as spekboom, aloes and sour fig do not burn easily.
 1 Where natural-grass fires travel, keep a cleared firebreak between the grassland and the windbreak. 2 Remove dry, dead things under the windbreak before fire season. 3 Keep aromatic shrubs away from the side the fires come from.
L185 Windbreak plants
L187 Wild dagga is a fast-growing shrub, 2 to 5 m tall, with orange flowers that attract sunbirds. It tolerates drought and wind, makes a dense thicket, and can be planted near the garden. When cut or burnt it resprouts from below.
L189 False olive is a dense small tree, often 4 to 5 m tall on the Highveld, with cream flowers with a honey scent from late winter to summer that bees love. It tolerates wind, frost and drought and can be pruned into a dense hedge.
L191 Karee is one of the most tolerant trees of southern Africa. It tolerates frost and drought, grows to about 8 m, and its roots are not dangerous. Put it in the back row for shade, fodder and firewood.

L220 Planting a tree
L222 The hardest time for a young tree is its first dry season. A hole that is too small, or water that wets only the surface, is enough to kill it. Plant well once, and the tree looks after itself for decades.
L224 Plant at the start of the rains: from October to December after the first good rain in summer-rain areas, and from about April to July in the Western Cape. Plant frost-tender trees after the last frost. Leaf-losing fruit trees can be planted in midwinter, while they have no leaves.
 1 Choose a good tree. Buy a strong tree with a thick stem and healthy leaves. Avoid trees with roots circling inside the bag. For citrus, buy certified disease-free trees.
 2 Plant it soon after buying it.
 3 Scrape a circle 1 m wide clear of weeds and grass, especially kikuyu, so they do not take the water.
 4 Dig wide, not deep. Make the hole two to three times wider than the root ball and equally deep. In hard soil, dig about 60 cm wide and deep and loosen the bottom and the sides. Put the topsoil on one side and the pale soil beneath on the other.
 5 Test drainage. Fill the hole with water. If water is still standing next morning, plant the tree on a raised mound instead.
 6 Support it in windy places. Drive a stake about 50 cm into the ground on the side the wind comes from before planting, so you do not spear the roots.
 7 Mix the backfill soil: about one part compost to three of topsoil, with a little well-rotted manure. Too much compost keeps the roots inside the hole, as if in a pot.
 8 Set the height. Free any circling roots. Stand the tree so the top of the root ball is level with the surrounding soil and the graft union (joining place where the fruit tree was joined) stays well above the ground.
 9 Fill and firm gently with hands or feet. Do not stamp hard.
 10 Tie it loosely to the stake with soft string in a figure 8, so the stem can sway a little. Remove the string after about a year.
 11 Make a bowl 1 m wide with a low ridge of soil to hold water. Widen it as the tree grows.
 12 Cover the bowl with 5 to 10 cm of mulch, keeping it about 10 cm from the stem so it does not rot.
 13 Water deeply: a full bucket of 20 L or more once after planting. If there is no rain, water every two to three days for two weeks, then give a deep soaking about once a week in the first dry season, more often in sand or great heat. Put a finger in the soil: water when it is dry below 5 to 10 cm.
L240 A deep soaking from time to time sends roots down following the water. A little water every day keeps roots near the surface, where they are dried out in the first great heat, and in clay/mud the water can rot them.
L242 Try this
Mark your first food-forest patch this week. 1 Choose a patch of disturbed land near the house, at least 10 m by 10 m, with sun coming from the north. 2 Stand on it on a windy afternoon and mark where the dangerous wind comes from. 3 Choose two or three fruit trees suited to your frost, and write down the width of each tree's mature canopy. 4 Mark their positions, 6 to 7 m apart for trees with a 4 m canopy. Put a stake for a nitrogen fixer between each pair. 5 Dig one planting hole, two to three times as wide as a nursery bag, and do a drainage test. 6 Draw the patch in a notebook: stakes, wind side, north, and what the drainage test showed.
L253 Key points
 - Plant food forests on disturbed land near home. Healthy natural grassland and fynbos are complete systems, not unfinished forest.
 - Match fruit trees to your frost and winter cold, and spread the harvest across the year.
 - Start with nitrogen-fixing pioneers, chop and drop them as fruit trees grow, and choose indigenous supporting plants rather than invasives.
 - Plant windbreaks across the dangerous wind, in several rows, without gaps but letting a little wind through, and keep them off the north side of the garden.
 - Dig wide, plant at the same depth as where the tree grew, cover with mulch, and water deeply once a week rather than a little every day.
L261 Plant pioneers this season, and after them plant fruit trees. In five years you can walk in shade where there was bare ground, with birds bringing the seed of the next forest.
