# Earthworks and water

In permaculture, earthworks are the skeleton of the farm. Earthworks means shaping the land: digging, moving and mounding soil so that water goes where we want it. Our vegetable gardens, trees and animal systems are then built onto this skeleton.

Good earthworks are planned for the site and the climate. Rainfall and slope decide which method you use. For example, in a very dry area you would not make raised vegetable beds. Sunken beds, shaped like shallow basins, are better there, because they catch every drop of rain.

## What earthworks can do

Well-planned earthworks can:

- help rain soak into the soil instead of running off
- refill the groundwater (the water stored deep in the soil and rock)
- stop erosion (soil being washed or blown away)
- water trees passively, without you carrying water
- carry water directly to crops
- create habitat for birds, frogs and insects

Earthworks can also do great damage when they are badly planned or badly built. Water that is gathered in one place and then escapes can cut a gully in one storm. So always **design for the biggest storm**: every earthwork needs a safe overflow, so that extra water can leave slowly without breaking walls or banks.

Do not start large earthworks, such as dams or long swales dug by machine, without advice from someone with experience. Permaculture earthworks is a big subject that is often taught as a separate course. This chapter covers the main methods, with a focus on harvesting rain and runoff.

### Common earthworks in permaculture

- **Swales:** level ditches dug along the contour, with the dug soil piled into a bank below. They catch runoff and let it soak in.
- **Ponds:** small open-water stores, usually filled by runoff or overflow from tanks.
- **Dams:** larger stores made with an earth or stone wall. They have legal rules in South Africa (see "Water law in South Africa" below).
- **Hügelkultur:** raised mounds built over buried logs and branches. The rotting wood holds water like a sponge.
- **Net-and-pan systems:** a network of small channels (the net) that leads runoff into planting basins (the pans).
- **Infiltration basins:** shallow, level-bottomed hollows that fill with runoff and let it soak in.
- **Diversion drains and diversion mounds:** small channels or low mounds that turn runoff from a road, path or roof towards a swale, basin or pond.

> **Safety:** A trench or pit can collapse at any depth, and wet soil can bury a person in seconds. Never work alone in a hole. In any hole deeper than about knee height, slope the sides back or support them before anyone climbs in. Keep the dug soil at least 1 m back from the edge. Stay out of a hole after rain. Before you dig, find out where water pipes, electric cables and septic lines run. Keep people, and especially children, well away from working machines such as a TLB (tractor-loader-backhoe). Fence off or cover open pits at the end of each day.

## Harvesting and storing rainwater

Rainwater harvesting means catching rain from roofs, roads, paths and the land, and storing it. You can store it in drums, tanks, ponds and dams, or in the soil and groundwater.

The rule is to **catch and store rain as close as possible to where it falls**. This gives you water security and helps your farm cope with drought and climate change. It is also more reliable than piping water from far away, because a long pipe can break or the supply can stop.

The land itself is the biggest store of all. Soil that is rich in organic matter and covered with plants and mulch soaks up rain and holds it much better than bare, hard soil. Permaculture calls this soil a **living sponge**.

Harvested rainwater can be used for:

- drinking and cooking, after treatment (see the Safety box below)
- washing, hygiene and sanitation
- watering crops, trees and vegetables
- watering livestock
- controlling erosion
- building soil and growing ground cover

### Eight principles of rainwater harvesting

These principles come from Brad Lancaster's book *Rainwater Harvesting for Drylands and Beyond*. They are written here in our own words.

1. **Watch first, for a long time.** See where water flows during rain, and where it stands afterwards. Build on what already works.
2. **Start at the top of your land and work down.** Water runs downhill. At the top of the slope there is less water, and it moves more slowly, so it is easier to catch. Water caught high up can also flow to lower places by gravity.
3. **Start small and simple.** Work at a size you can build and repair yourself. Many small water-catching works do better than one big one.
4. **Slow, spread and sink the water.** Do not let water rush off your land and take soil with it. Slow it down, spread it out and let it soak in.
5. **Always plan an overflow, and use the overflow as a resource.** Every tank, basin, swale and pond needs a safe way for extra water to leave in a big storm. Lead that overflow to where it can do more good.
6. **Keep the ground covered with plants and mulch.** This builds the living sponge, so the soil holds more water every year.
7. **Make each element do several jobs.** For example, the bank of a swale can also be a dry path, and trees planted to use harvested water can also shade a house and give fruit.
8. **Keep checking and improving.** Watch how your work behaves in each rain season, then change it where needed. This takes you back to principle 1.

### Harvesting water from roofs

You can harvest rain from most roofs. The harder and smoother the roof, the more water you get. Metal sheets (corrugated iron or IBR) and tiles give much more water than thatch.

Set up your roof system like this:

1. Fit gutters along the lower edges of the roof. The gutters should slope gently towards the tank so that water does not stand in them.
2. Put a leaf screen over the gutter or the tank inlet.
3. Fit a **first-flush diverter** before the tank. This is a pipe or container that catches the first, dirtiest water from each rain, with dust, bird droppings and leaves, and keeps it out of the tank. You can buy one or make one. Empty it after each rain.
4. Fit an overflow pipe near the top of the tank. Lead the overflow to a second tank, a pond, a swale or a tree basin, not against a wall or onto a path.
5. If you have more than one tank, you can join them with a pipe near the base so that they fill and empty together.

Leaves and dirt that get into the tank rot and let harmful bacteria grow. Keep gutters, screens and the first-flush diverter clean.

> **Safety:** Rainwater is not automatically safe to drink. Before drinking it, boil it (bring it to a rolling boil) or treat it the way your clinic or local health officer advises. Do not collect drinking water from a roof painted with lead paint, or from treated timber or a roof covered in bird droppings. Keep every tank closed with a tight lid and screen all openings, to keep out children, animals and mosquitoes. A child can drown in a small amount of water, so lock or weigh down tank lids and inspection hatches.

### How much water can you catch?

One millimetre (1 mm) of rain falling on one square metre (1 m²) gives **1 litre** of water. So:

**Water from a surface (litres) = area (m²) × rainfall (mm) × runoff coefficient**

The **runoff coefficient** is the share of the rain that actually runs off and can be caught. It is a number between 0 and 1. A coefficient of 0.8 means that 80% of the rain runs off. The rest wets the roof, evaporates, splashes over the gutters or goes into the first-flush diverter.

Measure the roof as it looks **from above**, including the overhangs. Do not measure along the slope of the roof. Rain falls straight down, so a steep roof catches no more rain than a flat roof over the same floor area.

**Worked example: a roof in a 750 mm rainfall area**

1. The roof measures 12 m by 10 m from above. Area = 12 m × 10 m = **120 m²**.
2. Your area gets about 750 mm of rain in an average year.
3. Rain falling on the roof in a year = 120 × 750 = **90 000 litres**.
4. A metal roof with good gutters has a runoff coefficient of about 0.8 to 0.9. Use **0.8** to be safe.
5. Water you can harvest = 120 × 750 × 0.8 = **72 000 litres a year**.

If you use 0.95, the figure is 120 × 750 × 0.95 = 85 500 litres. That is the most you could expect from a new, clean roof with perfect gutters. It is safer to plan with 0.8.

Remember that this is an average year. In a dry year you may get much less.

### Runoff coefficients for different surfaces

You can use the same formula for any surface, such as a yard, a road or a field. Only the coefficient changes. The figures below are rough guides. Runoff is higher on steep slopes, on clay soils and in heavy storms, and lower on flat, sandy, well-covered ground.

| Surface | Runoff coefficient |
|---|---|
| Metal roof (corrugated iron, IBR) | 0.8 to 0.9 |
| Clay or concrete tile roof | 0.6 to 0.9 |
| Thatch roof | about 0.2 |
| Concrete or tar paving | 0.7 to 0.95 |
| Gravel road | 0.5 to 0.7 |
| Bare, hard-packed soil | 0.3 to 0.6 |
| Grass or veld | 0.05 to 0.35 |
| Mulched garden bed | below 0.1 |

A mulched bed or a food forest lets almost all the rain soak in. That is exactly what we want on growing land. Hard surfaces such as roofs, paving and roads are where we harvest runoff.

### How big should your tank be?

You do not need a tank that holds the whole year's rain, because you use water all through the year. The tank only needs to carry you through the dry months.

**Worked example: tank for household use**

1. A household of 5 people plans to use 25 litres per person per day for drinking, cooking and basic washing. That is 5 × 25 = **125 litres a day**.
2. Water needed in a year = 125 × 365 = **45 625 litres**. The 120 m² roof in the example above gives about 72 000 litres in an average year, so the roof is big enough.
3. In the summer-rainfall interior of South Africa, the dry season runs roughly from May to September, about **150 days**. (In the winter-rainfall Western Cape, the dry season is summer instead.)
4. Water needed to get through the dry season = 125 × 150 = **18 750 litres**.
5. So the household needs about **20 000 litres** of storage, for example two 10 000 litre tanks. This assumes the tanks are full when the rain stops.

For a better estimate, make a table of the 12 months. For each month, work out how much water the roof gives (roof area × that month's rainfall × coefficient) and how much you use. Keep a running total of what is left in the tank. The biggest shortfall you reach in the dry season is the storage you need. Ask your nearest weather station or agricultural office for monthly rainfall figures for your area.

Water for the garden is extra. A vegetable garden in the dry season can use far more water than a household. That is why we also store water in the soil, with mulch, swales and basins.

## Infiltration basins

Infiltration basins are shallow hollows dug in the ground with a level bottom. They suit gentle slopes. They are good for catching runoff from roads and paths, overflow from tanks and gutters, and greywater (see the greywater section below).

How to use them:

1. Dig a shallow, level-bottomed hollow below where the runoff comes from.
2. Give each basin an overflow, so that when it is full the water spills into the next basin. A chain of basins can pass water down the slope and finally into a swale or a safe open area.
3. Plant the edges with fruit, nut or indigenous trees, or other useful plants.
4. Keep the basins well mulched. Mulch keeps the soil open so water soaks in fast.

Keep basins shallow: no deeper than about 50 cm. Water should soak away within about 12 hours. Standing water starves roots of air, and mosquitoes need several days of standing water to breed. If a basin stays wet for longer, make it shallower, add mulch or spread the water over more basins.

## Ponds and dams

Larger amounts of water can be stored in ponds and dams. They can be filled with runoff from land, roads and big roofs, or with overflow from tanks.

Benefits of ponds and dams:

- They give water for irrigation.
- They give water for animals.
- They slow down storm water.
- They create habitat for frogs, birds and dragonflies, which eat pests.
- They can be used to farm fish.

Things to plan for:

- **Get advice.** Dams and ponds get harder to design and build as the slope gets steeper. For anything larger than a garden pond, get a trained designer. For some dams it is a legal requirement.
- **Test the soil.** Water soaks away through sandy soil. A pond holds water only if the soil has enough clay, or if you line it with compacted clay or a pond liner.
- **Hire the right machine** for a bigger pond or dam, and supervise the work.
- **Build a spillway.** Every pond and dam needs a wide, protected overflow, so that water never flows over the wall.
- **Expect evaporation.** Most of South Africa loses more than 1 400 mm of water a year to evaporation from open water, and much of the dry interior loses 2 000 mm or more. That is often more than the rainfall. On a hot day a pond can lose 5 mm of depth or more. A pond with a 20 m² surface can then lose about 100 litres a day. Deep ponds with a small surface, and shade from trees on the edges, lose less.
- **Fish.** Before you stock fish, check which species you may keep in your area. Some alien fish are listed invasive species and need a permit. Never release fish into rivers.

### Working out the volume of a pond

For a pond with sloping sides:

**Volume (m³) ≈ length (m) × width (m) × average depth (m)**

For a bowl-shaped pond, the average depth is about half of the deepest point. One cubic metre (1 m³) holds 1 000 litres.

**Worked example: a small runoff pond**

1. The pond is 5 m long and 4 m wide at the top, and 1.2 m deep in the middle.
2. Average depth ≈ 1.2 ÷ 2 = 0.6 m.
3. Volume ≈ 5 × 4 × 0.6 = **12 m³**, which is **12 000 litres**.

How big must the catchment be to fill this pond in one 40 mm rain?

1. Each square metre of catchment gives 40 mm × runoff coefficient litres.
2. From a hard surface (coefficient about 1): 12 000 ÷ 40 = **300 m²** of roof, road or paving.
3. From grazed veld (coefficient about 0.2): each m² gives 40 × 0.2 = 8 litres, so you need 12 000 ÷ 8 = **1 500 m²** of veld.

A new pond may need more rain than this, because the dry soil around it soaks up water first.

> **Safety:** Children can drown in a pond, dam, open tank or even a deep bucket. Fence ponds and dams with a gate children cannot open, or cover small ponds with strong mesh. Make at least one side gently sloping so that a person or animal that falls in can climb out. Teach children to stay away. In bilharzia (schistosomiasis) areas, which include Limpopo, Mpumalanga, the north and east of Gauteng, the lower parts of KwaZulu-Natal and the Eastern Cape coast, do not swim, wash or stand barefoot in pond or dam water, because the parasite enters through the skin.

### Water law in South Africa

Water in South Africa is managed under the **National Water Act (Act 36 of 1998)**. This is a short summary, not legal advice. Before you build, ask your nearest office of the Department of Water and Sanitation (DWS).

- **Roof water:** Schedule 1 of the Act allows you to store and use runoff from your roof without a licence.
- **Small household use:** Schedule 1 also allows reasonable domestic use, small non-commercial gardening and watering animals (but not a feedlot) from water on or next to your land, as long as the use is not excessive.
- **Storing other water:** Storing water in a dam or pond is a water use under the Act. Small amounts may be covered by a **General Authorisation**, which means you do not need a full licence if you follow its conditions. Under the 2016 General Authorisation for taking and storing water, anyone storing more than 10 000 m³ on a property must register the use. Conditions differ between catchments, so check with DWS.
- **Building in a watercourse:** A watercourse is a river, stream, spring, wetland or natural drainage line, even one that only flows after rain. Blocking or diverting its flow, or changing its bed or banks, is a water use that needs authorisation. Low-risk work may be covered by a General Authorisation after a risk assessment. Other work needs a water use licence. Digging out or filling in more than 10 m³ of soil, sand or rock in a watercourse can also need environmental authorisation under the National Environmental Management Act (NEMA).
- **Dam safety:** A dam with a wall higher than 5 m that can hold more than 50 000 m³ is a "dam with a safety risk". It must be registered with the DWS Dam Safety Office.

So **do not dig a pond, build a dam or put a wall across a stream or wetland without checking first**. In Lesotho, Eswatini and Zimbabwe, ask the national water authority, because the rules are different.

## Sand dams

A sand dam is a strong wall of reinforced stone masonry or concrete, usually 1 to 5 m high. It is built across a **seasonal sandy river**, meaning a riverbed that is dry sand for part of the year.

How a sand dam works:

1. When the river flows, it carries sand and silt. The heavy sand settles behind the wall, while the light silt is carried on downstream.
2. After one to four rainy seasons, the space behind the wall fills up with sand.
3. Water is stored in the spaces between the grains of sand. Sand can hold up to about 40% of its volume as water.
4. Most of the river's flow still passes over the wall and continues downstream. Excellent Development, a charity that has built many sand dams in Africa, estimates that 97% to 99% carries on.
5. People reach the stored water through a well dug next to the dam, or through a pipe through the wall to a tap or hand pump.

Excellent Development reports that a large sand dam can store up to 40 million litres of water, enough for more than 1 000 people all year.

Advantages of sand dams:

- The water is under the sand, so it loses far less to evaporation than an open dam.
- The sand filters the water and protects it from animals and mosquitoes.
- The water table rises in the land around the dam, so trees, crops and grazing grow better. More plants mean more water soaks in and less soil washes away.

Sand dams only work in the right place. The wall must stand on solid bedrock (or, rarely, very hard compacted subsoil). It must reach at least 1.5 m beyond the flood width of the river on each side, so floods cannot cut around it. The river must carry coarse sand, not mostly silt and clay. The wall must let the river flow as it did before. Sand dams need an experienced designer.

A sand dam is built in a riverbed, which is a watercourse. **In South Africa it therefore needs authorisation under the National Water Act, and possibly under NEMA**, before any work starts (see "Water law in South Africa" above). In Lesotho, a sand dam has been built at the Bethel Business and Community Development Centre as a demonstration.

## Swales

A swale is a ditch dug exactly **along the contour**. A contour is an imaginary line across a slope that joins points at the same height. Because the swale is level, water does not flow along it. The water spreads out along the ditch and soaks slowly into the ground.

A swale has two parts:

- **the swale**, which is the ditch
- **the berm**, which is the bank made from the dug soil, piled on the downhill side

Swales slow, spread and sink runoff. They refill the groundwater, and they encourage trees and other plants to grow deep roots. They are especially useful for tree planting.

A small garden swale can be about two spade-blades deep and one spade-blade wide. A field swale can be much bigger. Size and spacing depend on rainfall, slope and soil.

### Linking swales and ponds

Swales can lead to a pond. For example:

1. Runoff fills a swale and flows along it into a pond at the end.
2. When the pond is full, water backs up into the swale and fills it.
3. Extra water leaves the swale over a **spillway**. This is a short, level section of the bank that is lower than the rest of the berm. Protect it with stones or thick grass. Keep the top of the berm at least a hand-width higher than the spillway, so that water never flows over the berm and washes it away.
4. The spillway water can fill the next swale down, and then the next, until it reaches a lower pond or leaves the property.

Water that leaves a system like this is slower and clearer, and carries much less soil.

Swales can also catch runoff from a driveway or road. Build a low **diversion mound** across the driveway at an angle, to turn the water into the swale.

### How big and how far apart?

A swale should hold the runoff from the land above it in a big storm. You can estimate this with the same formula used for roofs.

**Worked example: swale spacing**

1. The swale is about 0.5 m wide on average and holds water 0.3 m deep below the spillway. Each metre of swale holds 0.5 × 0.3 = 0.15 m³, which is **150 litres**.
2. Plan for a big storm of **50 mm**.
3. The land above the swale is grazed veld with a runoff coefficient of about 0.3. Each square metre gives 50 × 0.3 = **15 litres** of runoff.
4. Each metre of swale can take the runoff from 150 ÷ 15 = 10 m² of land. That is a strip 1 m wide and **10 m long** up the slope.
5. So the swales should be about **10 m apart**, measured down the slope. In a bigger storm, the spillways carry the extra water safely.

In drier areas, or where the ground is well covered with plants, swales can be further apart. In wetter areas, on bare ground or on steeper slopes, put them closer together.

### Where not to dig swales

- **Steep slopes:** Swales are safest on slopes of less than about 15% (a drop of 15 m in 100 m). On steeper land, use terraces, trees and ground cover instead. Waterlogged soil on a steep slope can slide.
- **Heavy clay or waterlogged soil:** If water does not soak in, a swale just becomes a stagnant ditch. Use a pond or a diversion drain instead.
- **Near buildings:** Keep swales and basins at least 3 m from walls and foundations, and never uphill of a septic tank, pit toilet or soak-away.
- **Very wet climates:** Where the soil is already wet for much of the year, you may need drains that lead water away, not swales.

### Step 1: Build an A-frame

An A-frame is a simple tool for finding the contour.

1. Take two straight poles of the same length, about 2 m long.
2. Tie the tops firmly together to make an upside-down V. The legs must be exactly the same length below the tie.
3. Tie a third, shorter pole across the middle of the legs, to make the letter A. Tie it at the same height on each leg.
4. Tie a string to the top of the A. Hang a stone from the string so that it swings freely past the crossbar.
5. **Find the level mark.** Stand the A-frame on the ground and mark where the string crosses the crossbar. Turn the A-frame around so that the legs swap places, keeping them on exactly the same two spots. Mark where the string crosses the bar again. The level mark is exactly halfway between these two marks.

### Step 2: Mark the contour

1. Knock a peg into the ground where you want the swale to start. Place one leg of the A-frame next to it.
2. Move the other leg up or down the slope until the string hangs exactly on the level mark. The two legs are now at the same height. Put a peg or stone at the second leg.
3. Keep the second leg still. Swing the first leg around to the other side, find level again, and put in the next peg.
4. Carry on across the slope. The line of pegs is your contour.
5. Walk along the line and smooth out any sharp zigzags.

### Step 3: Dig the swale

1. Dig the ditch along the line of pegs. Keep the bottom of the ditch level.
2. Put the soil and stones you dig out on the **downhill** side, to form the berm.
3. Firm the berm by treading it down in layers.
4. Protect the ends and the spillway with stones or tough grass, so water cannot cut around the ends and cause erosion.
5. Plant and mulch the berm straight away, so rain cannot wash it away.

### Planting the swale and berm

Now the fun begins. Plant water-loving plants in the ditch, useful trees and shrubs on and below the berm, and plants that like drier conditions on top of the berm.

- **On the berm:** fast-growing pioneer trees and shrubs, such as indigenous legume trees, to hold the bank and add fertility.
- **Mulch plants on the berm:** plants you can cut often for mulch and compost, such as comfrey, lemongrass, yarrow, nettle and sterile vetiver grass. The berm then becomes a "mulching bank".
- **Soil-building plants:** clover, lucerne (alfalfa) and sunn hemp. Deep-rooted weeds such as dandelion and plantain can be left to grow and cut for mulch.
- **In the ditch:** legume ground covers such as cowpea or lupin, and water-loving crops such as amadumbe (taro).
- **Below the swale:** medium-sized fruit trees such as lemon and other citrus, bananas in frost-free areas, and tomatoes. Think about the shade they will cast on nearby crops.
- **Under the trees:** shade-tolerant ground covers such as sweet potato and New Zealand spinach.

> **Note:** Amadumbe is not a listed invasive species, but it is spreading into natural wetlands in parts of South Africa. Grow it in garden swales and beds, not in or next to rivers, streams or wetlands.

## Preparing the ground

There are several ways to prepare land for planting. Choose according to your goals, the time you have and the tools and resources available.

You can combine methods. For example, you can first let animals graze and scratch the area, and then loosen the soil by hand or with a ripper.

Always choose the method that disturbs the soil least. As your farm matures, it produces more of its own mulch and compost, and you need to disturb the soil less and less.

### 1. Preparing by hand

If the area is small to medium and you do not have time for a no-dig method or a tractor, prepare it by hand. Use a pick (pikmatok), spade and garden fork.

1. Find and remove very invasive weeds, such as kikuyu runners or couch grass, which digging would spread.
2. Cut the vegetation as close to the ground as you can.
3. Water well, or wait for rain, and let the soil drain until it is moist but not wet.
4. Remove the cut plant material and use it for compost or mulch.
5. Loosen the topsoil with a fork.
6. Rake off any surface rubbish and level the surface.

### 2. Preparing with a four-wheel tractor

Repeated ploughing is one of the main causes of soil erosion and loss of soil carbon worldwide. Heavy machines compact the soil. Ploughing every year breaks down the soil structure and forms a hard layer called a **plough pan** just below the ploughing depth. Ploughing gives a quick burst of fertility, because it lets air into the soil and speeds up the breakdown of organic matter. After that, fertility drops season by season.

In permaculture, if we use a tractor at all, we use it mainly to set up the farm. Tractors still have a place on bigger farms. **Rippers and chisel ploughs** loosen compacted soil without turning it over, and they are very useful for repairing damaged land. There are also minimum-till and no-till implements for larger plantings.

1. Find and remove very invasive weeds.
2. Cut the vegetation as close to the ground as you can.
3. Water well, or wait for rain, and let the soil drain until it is moist but not wet.
4. Let animals graze down the grass, or cut it and remove it.
5. Look at the soil. Choose one tool or a combination: ripper, subsoiler, chisel plough, disc or plough. Use the ones that turn the soil least.
6. Make as few passes as you need.

### 3. Preparing with a two-wheel tractor

A two-wheel tractor is a walk-behind machine with a fuel engine. With a rotary tiller (rotavator) attached, spinning blades break the soil into fine pieces for planting. Many models also take other implements, such as a small plough, a cultivator or a flail mower. They are much cheaper than four-wheel tractors and use less fuel, so they suit small farms that are just starting.

Use the rotavator sparingly. Frequent rotavating also breaks down soil structure.

1. Find and remove very invasive weeds.
2. Cut the vegetation as close to the ground as you can.
3. Water well, or wait for rain, and let the soil drain until it is moist but not wet.
4. Kill the grass under a tarpaulin, lift the sods, or let animals clear it.
5. Pass over the soil with the rotavator.
6. Make a few passes as needed.
7. Rake the loose grass off the surface.

### 4. Animal tractors

An animal tractor uses animals to clear and prepare land (see Chapter 5, Animal systems). Chickens, ducks, pigs, sheep, goats and cattle can all graze down vegetation. Pigs also dig the soil.

1. Cut large plants down to the ground.
2. If you want pigs to dig the soil, water the area first so that it is soft.
3. Put a movable fence around the area you want cleared. An electric fence powered by a solar energiser works well.
4. Inside the fence, give the animals shelter if they need it, for example a mobile coop for chickens to roost and lay in.
5. If you use a movable pen instead of a fence, move it step by step across the area.
6. Follow cattle or pigs with chickens. The chickens spread the manure and eat fly larvae, which breaks the fly breeding cycle.
7. If needed, loosen the soil with a chisel plough or garden fork without turning it over.
8. Rake the area level.

### 5. No-dig gardening

No-dig gardening copies the forest floor. Leaves fall, slowly break down and feed the soil, which becomes loose, fertile and spongy. No-dig methods need a lot of organic material, and on a large area they can take more work than using a machine. But they have big benefits:

- **Better soil structure.** Soil life does the digging for you. When we feed the soil with organic matter, earthworms, fungi and micro-organisms become more active and build good soil structure.
- **Deeper topsoil.** Large amounts of compost and mulch on the surface quickly build up the topsoil.
- **Fewer weeds.** Many weed seeds need light to germinate. When we do not dig, buried seeds stay in the dark and stay dormant. This saves a great deal of weeding time.

### 6. Sheet mulching (lasagne beds)

Sheet mulching means building layers of organic material on top of the soil, like a lasagne. It smothers grass and weeds, and at the same time feeds soil life from above. You do not need to dig or weed first. It is a quick way to start a small garden, and it is especially good on hard, bare soil.

You need a lot of organic material, such as manure, compost, cardboard, newspaper and hay or straw. Use what you can get free or cheaply in your area.

1. Cut down grass and bushes. Leave the cut grass and chopped branches where they fall.
2. Water the area well.
3. Spread a layer of animal manure 6 to 10 cm thick. Fresh cow manure is good here, at the bottom, because it wakes up soil life.
4. Cover it with a thick layer of cardboard or newspaper, overlapping the edges so no light gets through. Wet it well.
5. Add 10 to 15 cm of straw or other dry mulch.
6. Add a layer of compost or well-rotted manure.
7. Add another 10 to 15 cm of straw or other dry mulch that has no seeds in it.
8. Finish with a layer of compost or good topsoil for planting into.
9. Water well. Make a hole through the top layers and plant seedlings into the compost, adding extra compost or topsoil to the hole if needed.
10. Mulch around the seedlings with dry grass to protect the top layer.

> **Safety:** Fresh manure can carry germs that make people sick. Put fresh manure only in the bottom layer, under the cardboard. The layer you plant into, and anything that touches crops eaten raw, must be well-made compost or well-rotted manure.

### 7. Solarising and tarping

**Soil solarisation** uses the sun to heat the soil and kill weeds, weed seeds and some pests and diseases.

1. Cut the vegetation, then water the soil well. Moist soil carries heat better.
2. Cover it tightly with **clear** plastic, burying or weighing down the edges.
3. Leave it for at least 4 to 6 weeks in the hottest, sunniest time of the year.

Solarisation also kills some useful soil life, so use it only where you need to.

**Tarping** (also called occultation) uses a **black** plastic sheet or tarpaulin instead. It does not heat the soil as much. It works by blocking light, so the plants underneath die. Weeding takes a lot of time on an organic farm where no herbicides are used, so black tarps have become popular with market gardeners.

Farmers use tarps to make a **stale seedbed**:

1. Prepare the bed and water it.
2. Cover it with a black tarp. Weed seeds germinate under the tarp, but die without light.
3. After a few weeks, remove the tarp and sow the crop into the clean, "stale" bed.

To clear grass or weeds with a tarp:

1. Find and remove very invasive weeds.
2. Cut the vegetation as close to the ground as you can.
3. Water well, or wait for rain, and let the soil drain until it is moist but not wet.
4. Cover the area with a tarp or thick plastic, held down with rocks or sandbags. Leave it for 6 to 10 weeks, or until the plants underneath have died.
5. Loosen the topsoil with a fork. Do not turn the soil over. Push the fork in and rock it back and forth.
6. Rake up what is left.

## Forming growing beds for vegetables

These are guidelines, not fixed rules. Find what works for your place.

The raised beds below suit areas with moderate or high rainfall. In very dry areas, make **sunken beds** instead: beds a little lower than the paths, which act like basins and catch every drop of rain.

### Working with the slope

If the slope is more than about 3% (a drop of 3 m in 100 m), lay out beds and paths **along the contour**. This stops heavy rain from running down the beds and causing erosion. Paths along the contour also catch rain.

On very flat ground you can be more creative. You can make circles, spirals or the popular mandala garden, as long as the garden is easy to work and look after.

### Double-reach raised beds

"Double reach" means you can reach the middle of the bed from the paths on both sides, so you never stand on the bed. Standing on a bed compacts the soil.

- Beds are usually 80 to 120 cm wide. Market gardens often use 75 to 90 cm beds, which suit most hand tools and two-wheel tractor implements.
- Paths are usually 40 to 60 cm wide.
- Beds can be about 8 m long in a small garden and up to 30 m in a commercial garden. The longer the bed, the further you walk around it.

Benefits of raised beds:

- Where the water table is high, they lift roots out of waterlogged soil.
- In high-rainfall areas, extra water drains away from the vegetables.
- Soil from the paths is added to the beds, so the topsoil gets deeper.
- Compost and fertiliser go only onto the beds, not the paths, which saves money.
- The beds are never walked on and are hardly dug, so the soil gets better every year. In some old raised beds you can push a garden fork in right up to the handle.

### Making raised beds

1. Mark the contour with an A-frame, the same way as for a swale. Start at the top of the area. If there is space, put a small swale above the top bed.
2. Measure the rest of the beds and paths down the slope with a tape measure. Mark the edges with a line of lime or ash. For curved beds, lay a hosepipe along the edge as a guide.
3. Start at the top. Dig soil from the path area above each bed and throw it onto the bed below.
4. For an average bed, raise it by 10 to 15 cm. On very flat ground, for a no-dig garden, you can take just 2 to 3 cm of soil from the paths and cover the paths with wood chips.
5. Level the top of each bed with a rake and make neat edges.
6. Spread any soil amendments the soil test shows you need, such as lime or gypsum. Add compost about 3 cm thick.
7. Rake it level. Then work along the bed with a fork to loosen the soil, without turning it over.
8. Cover with a thick mulch and water it. You can also cover the bed with a tarp to make a stale seedbed.
9. Leave the bed for a few weeks before planting. How long depends on what amendments you added.

### Edged beds and instant beds

In small gardens you can edge raised beds with rocks, thick branches or pallet wood. These can be instant gardens:

1. Lay thick cardboard over the grass inside the edging.
2. Fill the bed with good topsoil.
3. Finish with a layer of compost.

This works well where there is no good soil, or where you have to build on concrete and bring in all the soil.

### Trench beds

A trench bed is a deep bed filled with organic material. It is a good way to grow vegetables where the soil is rocky or heavy clay, and it holds water well in dry areas. Digging it is hard work, but the bed lasts for several years.

1. Mark out a bed about **1 m wide** and 2 to 5 m long.
2. Dig it out to about **60 cm deep**, or deeper.
3. Put the topsoil (the dark upper layer) on one side and the subsoil (the lighter, often reddish, layer below) on the other side.
4. Lay sticks or coarse material in the bottom if you have them.
5. Fill the trench with layers: about 20 cm of organic material (dry leaves, grass, kitchen scraps, manure), then about 10 cm of subsoil. Water each layer well.
6. Keep adding layers until the trench is nearly full.
7. Finish with the topsoil on top, then mulch.

The bed will sink as the organic matter rots. Top it up with compost each season.

### Terrace beds

On steep land, terraces stop water and soil from washing away. A terrace is a level step cut into the slope. The bank or wall at the front of each terrace works like the berm of a swale.

- Build the terrace walls with rocks, logs or old tyres. Use whatever materials you have, to slow the runoff and build up fertile soil behind each wall.
- Where invasive wattles grow thickly, you can cut them and weave the stems into screens that hold back soil and water. Use only dry, cut stems without seed pods, so you do not spread the seed. Never plant wattles.
- In the mountains of India and Nepal, and in many other places, farmers cut terraces into hillsides. The terraces catch the monsoon rain and water the rice fields.

### Pit beds and banana circles

A pit bed is a round pit, about 50 cm deep (up to 1 m) and 1 to 2 m across, filled with organic material. Plants are planted around the edge, where they feed on the rotting material and the water that collects in the pit. A **banana circle** is a well-known kind of pit bed.

Everything the plants need is in one place, so the pit saves work, water and nutrients. You can water it with greywater, or with runoff from a roof or driveway. You can even build an outdoor shower over it: put a slatted wooden floor over the pit so that the shower water drains straight in.

1. Choose a spot near a water source, such as below a tap, near a seep, or where greywater or runoff flows.
2. Mark a circle for the pit.
3. Lift the top grass or sods and put them to one side.
4. Dig out the topsoil and put it to one side.
5. Dig a small channel to lead the water to the centre of the pit.
6. Dig the pit about 50 cm deep (up to 1 m) and fill it with organic material.
7. Put the soil back around the edge as a low ring-shaped mound, and plant it.
8. Mulch well.
9. Keep adding organic material to the centre of the pit.

Plant a mix of trees, shrubs, herbs, climbers and ground covers. Bananas, amadumbe, sweet potato, ginger and pawpaw are popular in frost-free areas.

### Mandala and keyhole beds

Horseshoe, keyhole and mandala beds are round or curved vegetable beds, usually on flat or gently sloping land. They are fun and look beautiful. They take a little more time and material to build, so they are especially good for school gardens and public places.

Use a circle as the main shape. A group of circles lets you make patterns with short paths that follow the land.

Benefits:

- Every part of the bed is easy to reach for planting and picking.
- You can stand in one place and work a large part of the bed.
- The shape can catch and hold water.
- You can put the crops you pick most often near the path, and the ones that need less care further in.
- Tall plants on the outside shelter the inner plants from wind and frost. Remember that the sun is in the north. Put tall plants on the south side so that they do not shade the smaller ones.
- They are beautiful to look at.

## Irrigation

Every irrigation method loses some water to evaporation, wind and runoff. The best method puts water only where the roots are, at a rate the soil can soak up. Water early in the morning or late in the afternoon, when less water evaporates. Mulch everything you water.

### Hand watering

Hand watering with a hosepipe or watering can is the most common method. It is easy, but it often wastes water on paths, and plants get uneven amounts.

Hand watering works best for containers and single plants. The most efficient way is to make a small basin or furrow next to each plant or row. Fill it, let the water soak in, and then move to the next one. This works especially well early in the season, while plants are small.

For seedlings, use a watering can with a fine rose, or a spray bottle with an adjustable nozzle. Water that has stood in the sun is better for seedlings than very cold water straight from a borehole.

### Bottle irrigation

This simple method saves water and time.

1. Take an old plastic bottle and cut off the bottom.
2. Make a few small holes in the cap, or in the sides near the neck.
3. Bury the bottle upside down next to the plant, with the neck in the ground and the open, cut end just above the soil.
4. Fill the bottle through the open end. Water seeps out slowly near the roots.

### Drip irrigation

Drip irrigation (also called trickle irrigation) lets water drip slowly onto the soil at the roots of the plants. Less water is lost to evaporation and runoff, and the paths stay dry, so fewer weeds grow there. Drip is the most water-efficient method: usually about 90% of the water reaches the root zone, compared with roughly 70% to 85% for sprinklers.

- Drip lines are usually 16 mm or 20 mm pipe with built-in drippers. Lay them along the beds, under the mulch.
- Space the drippers about 20 to 30 cm apart along the line.
- Space the lines about 30 to 50 cm apart. In sandy soil, water spreads less sideways, so put drippers and lines closer together.
- A 1 m wide bed usually needs 2 lines. A 1.5 m bed needs 3.
- Always fit a **filter**, because dirt blocks drippers. Flush the ends of the lines now and then.

You can buy drip kits with pipe, drippers, fittings and sometimes a timer from a hardware store or co-op.

**Pressure from a tank.** Every 10 m of height between the water level in a tank and the tap gives about 1 bar of pressure. A tank on a 1 m stand gives only about 0.1 bar. That is enough for special low-pressure (gravity) drip kits, but not for normal drippers or sprinklers, which need a higher tank or a pump. Check the pressure your system needs on the packet.

### Micro-sprinklers (micro-jets)

Micro-sprinklers suit Zones 1 and 2, the intensive areas close to the house. Each small sprinkler head sits on a stake or riser pushed into the ground, and is fed by thin tubing from a low-pressure poly pipe.

- Each head wets a circle a few metres across. Check the packet for the exact size and the pressure needed.
- How fine the spray is depends on the nozzle and the water pressure. Very fine misting nozzles are for nurseries and seedlings.
- Micro-sprinklers lose more water to wind and evaporation than drip does, so water early or late in the day.

### Impact sprinklers

Impact sprinklers are the ones that click as they turn. They throw water in a wide circle, often 10 m or more across, and wet a large area quickly. They are cheap, easy to find at garden shops and co-ops, and can be moved around on a hosepipe.

They need more pressure than drip or micro-sprinklers. They also lose the most water to wind and evaporation, and they wet paths and leaves, which can spread leaf diseases. Use them for larger areas such as pasture, green manure crops or a big field, not for small beds.

### Comparing irrigation methods

| Method | Water efficiency | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Hand watering into basins | High if done carefully | Single plants, young trees, containers |
| Bottle irrigation | High | Single plants in dry areas |
| Drip | Highest, about 90% | Vegetable beds, orchards, dry areas |
| Micro-sprinklers | Medium to high | Zone 1 and 2 beds, nurseries |
| Impact sprinklers | Lowest | Large areas, pasture, green manure |

## Using greywater for irrigation

**Greywater** is used water from baths, showers, hand basins and the washing machine. **Blackwater** is water from toilets. Blackwater carries many germs and must go to a proper sanitation system, never onto the garden.

**Kitchen sink water** is the dirtiest kind of greywater. It is full of fat, food scraps and germs. It blocks soil and pipes and attracts flies. Do not use it on the garden unless it first passes through a grease trap. Even then, use it only on trees and non-food plants.

Water from washing nappies, or from washing anyone who has diarrhoea, must be treated like blackwater and **not** used in the garden.

### Which water is suitable?

| Water source | Use on the garden? |
|---|---|
| Shower, bath, hand basin | Yes, with care |
| Washing machine (rinse water is cleanest) | Yes, with care; use low-salt, garden-friendly detergent |
| Kitchen sink, dishwasher | Only through a grease trap, and only on trees and ornamentals |
| Nappy washing | No |
| Toilet (blackwater) | No |

### Rules for safe greywater use

1. **Put it on the soil, under mulch.** Let greywater flow into a mulched tree basin, a mulched swale or a pit bed, or through a pipe that ends under the mulch. Never spray greywater with a sprinkler.
2. **Not on vegetables eaten raw.** Do not let greywater touch leafy greens such as lettuce or spinach, or root crops such as carrots and radishes. Use it on fruit trees, bananas, shrubs, ornamentals, and on crops that are cooked or whose edible part grows well above the ground.
3. **Avoid contact.** Keep people, children and animals from touching greywater. It should soak in quickly, not form pools.
4. **Do not store it.** Use greywater within a day. Stored greywater goes bad, smells and breeds germs and mosquitoes.
5. **Keep it away from drinking water.** Never put greywater near, or uphill of, a well, borehole or spring used for drinking water. Do not let it run into a stream or off your property.
6. **Choose your soaps.** Soil can cope with small amounts of normal soap, but salts, bleach, borax (boron) and harsh chemicals build up and damage soil structure, soil life and plants. Use mild, biodegradable, low-salt soaps and detergents. Liquid laundry detergents usually contain less salt than washing powders. Do not send water with bleach, drain cleaners, paint or other chemicals to the garden.
7. **Move it around.** Rotate greywater between different basins. Let rain or fresh water rinse the soil now and then, to wash out salts. Yellow leaf edges or a white crust on the soil are signs of salt build-up.
8. **Check local rules.** In towns, municipal by-laws may control greywater use.

### A simple greywater system

Greywater systems can be expensive and need a plumber, but they can also be very simple:

1. Find where the bath, shower and hand basin drain out of the house. It is usually close to the outside wall of the bathroom.
2. Connect a PVC pipe to the drain outlet and lead it to the garden. Keep the pipe sloping gently downhill all the way.
3. At the end of the pipe, fit a flexible hose, so that you can move the outlet between different mulched basins.
4. Make sure the outlet is covered by mulch, so that nobody touches the water.

Soil organisms and plant roots break down much of what is in greywater once it has soaked into healthy, mulched soil. That is why greywater should go into the soil, not onto the surface or onto plants.

## Key points

- Plan earthworks for your site and climate, start at the top of the slope, and always give water a safe overflow for the biggest storm.
- 1 mm of rain on 1 m² gives 1 litre: harvest = area × rainfall × runoff coefficient. A 120 m² metal roof in a 750 mm area gives about 72 000 litres a year.
- Size tanks to carry you through the dry season, not to hold the whole year's rain.
- Treat roof water before drinking it, and keep tanks covered and screened.
- Infiltration basins and swales should drain within about 12 hours and be protected with mulch and plants.
- Swales follow the contour exactly. Space them so each one can hold a big storm, and avoid them on steep, clay or waterlogged slopes.
- In South Africa you may store roof water freely, but dams, and any work in a river, stream or wetland, need a check with DWS and may need authorisation.
- Sand dams store water safely under sand in seasonal sandy rivers, but need bedrock, good design and authorisation.
- Fence or cover ponds, tanks and pits. Children can drown in very little water, and trenches can collapse.
- Choose the ground preparation that disturbs the soil least: no-dig, sheet mulching, tarping, animals, then machines.
- Drip irrigation under mulch uses the least water. Water early or late in the day.
- Use greywater from baths, showers and basins under mulch, never on vegetables eaten raw, never stored, and with mild, low-salt soaps.
