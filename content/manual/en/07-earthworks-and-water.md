# Earthworks and water

Rain is the one harvest that falls on every farm for free. On most of our land it runs straight off, taking the **topsoil** (the dark, living top layer) with it. The same storm that floods the road leaves the field thirsty a week later.

Earthworks change that. By shaping the land with a spade, a pikmatok or a machine, we slow the water down, spread it out and let it sink in. Earthworks are the skeleton of the farm. The gardens, trees and animals all hang on it.

**By the end of this chapter you will be able to:**

- Measure your roof and work out how much rain it can catch, and how big a tank you need
- Build an A-frame, peg out a contour and dig a swale the right distance from the next one
- Choose the ground preparation that disturbs your soil least
- Lay out beds and choose an irrigation method that suits your water
- Use bath and shower water safely on trees

## What earthworks can do

Water that races off bare land carries soil with it. Water that stands still on the land soaks in and feeds roots for weeks. Earthworks decide which of the two happens.

Good earthworks:

- let rain soak into the soil instead of running off
- refill the groundwater, the water stored deep in soil and rock
- stop **erosion**, the washing or blowing away of soil
- water trees without anyone carrying a bucket
- lead water to crops
- make homes for frogs, birds and insects

Earthworks can also do great damage. Water gathered in one place and then let loose can cut a gully in a single storm. So we **design for the biggest storm**. Every earthwork gets a safe overflow, so that extra water leaves slowly without breaking a bank.

The earthworks we use most:

- **Swales:** level ditches across the slope, with the soil piled in a bank below.
- **Infiltration basins:** shallow, level hollows that fill with **runoff** (rain that runs off the ground instead of soaking in) and let it sink.
- **Ponds and dams:** open water stores filled by runoff or tank overflow.
- **Diversion drains and mounds:** small channels or low ridges that turn water off a road or path towards a swale, basin or pond.
- **Net-and-pan systems:** small channels (the net) leading runoff into planting basins (the pans).
- **Hügelkultur:** mounds built over buried logs and branches, which soak up water like a sponge as they rot.

Rainfall and slope decide which of these suits your land.

## Harvesting and storing rainwater

Water piped from far away can stop at any time. Water you caught yourself is there when you need it. So the rule is simple: **catch rain as close as possible to where it falls**, and store it.

We store it in drums, tanks, ponds and dams. But the biggest store is the soil itself. Soil rich in **organic matter** (rotting plant and animal material), covered with plants and **mulch** (a blanket of dry grass, leaves or straw), soaks up rain and holds it far better than bare, hard ground. Permaculture calls this soil a **living sponge**.

### Eight principles of rainwater harvesting

Brad Lancaster's book *Rainwater Harvesting for Drylands and Beyond* sets out eight principles. In our own words:

1. **Watch for a long time.** Walk the land in the rain. See where water runs and where it stands. Build on what already works.
2. **Start at the top and work down.** At the top of a slope there is less water and it moves slower, so it is easier to catch. From there it can flow downhill to where you need it.
3. **Start small and simple.** Many small works you can build and mend yourself beat one big one.
4. **Slow it, spread it, sink it.** Never let water rush off the land.
5. **Plan the overflow, and use it.** Lead extra water to where it can do more good.
6. **Keep the ground covered** with plants and mulch, so the sponge grows every year.
7. **Make each work do several jobs.** A swale bank can also be a dry path; the trees watered by a basin can shade the house.
8. **Keep watching and adjusting.** Each rain season teaches you something, which brings you back to principle 1.

### Harvesting water from roofs

Hard, smooth roofs give the most water. Corrugated iron, IBR sheeting and tiles give far more than thatch.

1. Fit gutters along the low edges of the roof, sloping gently towards the tank so water never stands in them.
2. Put a leaf screen over the gutter or the tank inlet.
3. Fit a **first-flush diverter** before the tank. This is a pipe or container that catches the first, dirtiest water of each rain, with its dust, droppings and leaves. Empty it after every rain.
4. Fit an overflow near the top of the tank and lead it to another tank, a pond, a swale or a tree basin, never against a wall.
5. Join two or more tanks with a pipe near the base, so they fill and empty together.

Leaves that reach the tank rot and breed germs, so keep the gutters and screens clean. Keep every tank closed with a tight, screened lid, so that children, animals and mosquitoes cannot get in. Roof water is not clean enough to drink as it is: boil it first.

### How much water can you catch?

One millimetre of rain on one square metre gives **1 litre** of water. So:

**Water caught (litres) = area (m²) × rainfall (mm) × runoff coefficient**

The **runoff coefficient** is the share of the rain that runs off, as a number between 0 and 1. A coefficient of 0.8 means 80% runs off; the rest wets the roof, splashes past the gutter or goes to the first-flush diverter.

Measure the roof **from above**, including the overhangs. Rain falls straight down, so a steep roof catches no more than a flat one over the same floor.

**Worked example: a roof in a 750 mm rainfall area**

1. The roof measures 12 m × 10 m from above = **120 m²**.
2. Rain falling on it in an average year = 120 × 750 = 90 000 litres.
3. A metal roof with good gutters has a coefficient of 0.8 to 0.9. Plan with **0.8**.
4. Water you can harvest = 120 × 750 × 0.8 = **72 000 litres a year**.

A new roof with perfect gutters might reach 0.95 (85 500 litres), but plan with 0.8. A dry year gives much less.

The same formula works for any surface. Only the coefficient changes. Runoff is higher on steep slopes, on clay and in heavy storms.

| Surface | Runoff coefficient |
|---|---|
| Metal roof | 0.8 to 0.9 |
| Tile roof | 0.6 to 0.9 |
| Thatch roof | about 0.2 |
| Concrete or tar | 0.7 to 0.95 |
| Gravel road | 0.5 to 0.7 |
| Bare, hard-packed soil | 0.3 to 0.6 |
| Grass or veld | 0.05 to 0.35 |

Covered ground lets most rain soak in, which is what we want on growing land. Hard surfaces are where we harvest.

### How big should the tank be?

You use water all year, so the tank does not need to hold a whole year's rain. It only needs to carry you through the dry months.

**Worked example: a tank for the household**

1. Five people plan on 25 litres each a day for drinking, cooking and washing: **125 litres a day**.
2. In a year that is 125 × 365 = 45 625 litres. The 120 m² roof above gives about 72 000 litres, so the roof is big enough.
3. In the summer-rainfall interior the dry season runs roughly May to September: about **150 days**. (In the Western Cape the dry season is summer.)
4. Water needed to get through it = 125 × 150 = **18 750 litres**.
5. So the household needs about **20 000 litres** of storage, for example two 10 000 litre tanks, full when the rains stop.

For a sharper answer, write down the 12 months. For each month, work out what the roof gives (area × that month's rain × coefficient) and what you use, and keep a running total of what is left in the tank. The deepest shortfall in the dry season is the storage you need.

Garden water is extra, and a dry-season vegetable garden drinks far more than a household. So we also store water in the soil.

## Infiltration basins

An **infiltration basin** is a shallow hollow with a level floor that catches runoff and lets it soak in (**infiltration** means water sinking into the soil). Basins suit gentle slopes. They catch water from roads, paths, tank overflows and greywater (used bath and shower water).

1. Dig a shallow, level-bottomed hollow below where the water comes from.
2. Give each basin an overflow into the next, so a chain of basins passes water down the slope and finally into a swale or a safe open area.
3. Plant the edges with fruit, nut or **indigenous** trees (trees that grow naturally in our region).
4. Mulch the basin well. Mulch keeps the soil open so water sinks fast.

Keep basins no deeper than about 50 cm, and make sure the water soaks away within about 12 hours. Roots need air, and mosquitoes need several days of standing water to breed. If a basin stays wet longer, make it shallower, add mulch or share the water over more basins.

## Ponds and dams

A pond turns a single storm into weeks of water. It waters animals and crops, slows storm water, can raise fish, and brings in the frogs, birds and dragonflies that eat our pests.

Plan for these before you dig:

- **Soil.** Water drains away through sand. A pond holds water only in soil with enough clay, or with a lining of compacted clay or a pond liner.
- **Slope.** The steeper the land, the harder a pond or dam is to design and build. A dam is work for an experienced designer.
- **Spillway.** Every pond and dam needs a wide overflow, protected with stone or thick grass, so water never flows over the wall.
- **Evaporation.** Across most of South Africa an open water surface loses more than 1 400 mm a year, and over much of the dry interior 2 000 mm or more. That is often more than the rain. At 5 mm a day, a pond with a 20 m² surface loses 100 litres a day. A deep pond with a small surface, shaded by trees, loses less.
- **People.** Slope the sides as you dig so they cannot cave in, and keep everyone well away from a working machine. Fence the finished pond with a gate a child cannot open, and make one side gently sloping so a person or animal that falls in can climb out. Where bilharzia occurs, do not wash, swim or stand barefoot in pond water.

### Working out the volume of a pond

**Volume (m³) ≈ length × width × average depth.** For a bowl-shaped pond, the average depth is about half the deepest point. One cubic metre holds 1 000 litres.

**Worked example: a small runoff pond**

1. The pond is 5 m long, 4 m wide and 1.2 m deep in the middle. Average depth ≈ 0.6 m.
2. Volume ≈ 5 × 4 × 0.6 = **12 m³**, or 12 000 litres.
3. To fill it in one 40 mm rain from a hard surface (coefficient about 1), you need 12 000 ÷ 40 = **300 m²** of roof or paving.
4. From grazed veld (coefficient about 0.2), each m² gives 40 × 0.2 = 8 litres, so you need 12 000 ÷ 8 = **1 500 m²**.

A new pond needs more than this, because the dry soil around it drinks first.

## Sand dams

In dry country, rivers are sand for most of the year. Under that sand, water often still moves. A sand dam holds it there, out of reach of the sun.

A sand dam is a strong wall of reinforced stone masonry or concrete, usually 1 to 5 m high, built across a **seasonal sandy river**.

1. When the river flows, the heavy sand settles behind the wall and the light silt washes on downstream.
2. Within one to four rainy seasons, sand fills the space behind the wall.
3. Water is held in the gaps between the sand grains. Sand can hold up to about 40% of its volume as water.
4. Most of the flow still passes over the wall. Excellent Development, a charity that has built many sand dams in Africa, puts it at 97% to 99%.
5. People draw the water from a well dug beside the dam, or through a pipe in the wall to a tap or pump.

The same charity reports that a large sand dam can hold up to 40 million litres, enough for more than 1 000 people all year. Under sand, little water evaporates, and the sand filters it and keeps out animals and mosquitoes. The water table rises around the dam, and trees, crops and grazing grow better.

Sand dams work only in the right place. The wall must stand on solid bedrock and reach at least 1.5 m past the flood edge on each bank, so floods cannot cut around it. The river must carry coarse sand, not mostly silt, and must flow on as before. This is work for an experienced designer. In Lesotho, the Bethel Business and Community Development Centre built one as a demonstration.

## Swales

A **swale** is a ditch dug exactly along the **contour**, an imaginary line across a slope that joins points at the same height. Because the ditch is level, water does not run along it. It spreads out and sinks in, and the trees below grow deep roots into that stored water.

A swale has two parts: the ditch, and the **berm**, the bank of dug soil piled on the downhill side.

### Linking swales and ponds

1. Runoff fills a swale and spreads along it to a pond at one end.
2. When the pond is full, water backs up and fills the swale.
3. Extra water leaves over a **spillway**: a short, level section of the berm that is lower than the rest, protected with stones or thick grass. Keep the top of the berm at least a hand-width higher than the spillway, so water never pours over the berm.
4. The spillway feeds the next swale down, and the next, until the water reaches a lower pond or leaves the land.

Water that leaves a system like this is slower and clearer, and carries far less soil. A low **diversion mound** built at an angle across a driveway will turn its runoff into a swale too.

### How big and how far apart?

A small garden swale can be about two spade-blades deep and one wide. A field swale can be much bigger. Each swale should hold the runoff from the land above it in a big storm, and the roof formula tells you how much that is.

**Worked example: swale spacing**

1. The swale is about 0.5 m wide and holds water 0.3 m deep below the spillway. Each metre holds 0.5 × 0.3 = 0.15 m³ = **150 litres**.
2. Plan for a big storm of **50 mm**.
3. The land above is grazed veld, coefficient about 0.3. Each m² gives 50 × 0.3 = **15 litres**.
4. Each metre of swale can take the runoff from 150 ÷ 15 = 10 m² of land: a strip 1 m wide and 10 m long up the slope.
5. So the swales go about **10 m apart**, measured down the slope. In a bigger storm, the spillways carry the rest.

Where the rain is lighter or the ground well covered, swales can be further apart. On bare ground, steeper slopes or in wetter areas, bring them closer.

### Where not to dig swales

- **Steep slopes.** Keep swales to slopes under about 15% (a 15 m drop in 100 m). Wet soil on steep land can slide. Use terraces and trees there instead.
- **Heavy clay or waterlogged soil.** If water does not sink in, a swale becomes a stagnant ditch. Use a pond or a diversion drain.
- **Near buildings.** Keep swales and basins at least 3 m from walls and foundations, and never uphill of a pit toilet, septic tank or soak-away.
- **Very wet places.** Where the soil is wet most of the year, you need drains that lead water away.

### Digging the swale

First peg out the contour with an A-frame (see "Try it" at the end of this chapter). Then:

1. Dig the ditch along the pegs, keeping its floor level.
2. Pile the soil and stones on the **downhill** side and tread the berm firm in layers.
3. Protect the ends and the spillway with stones or tough grass, so water cannot cut around them.
4. Plant and mulch the berm the same day, before rain can wash it.

### Planting the swale and berm

Now the fun begins! Water-lovers go in the ditch, useful trees on and below the berm, and plants that like it drier on top.

- **On the berm:** fast-growing pioneer trees and shrubs, such as indigenous **legume** trees (plants of the bean family, many of which feed nitrogen into the soil) like *Vachellia* species, to hold the bank and build fertility.
- **Mulch plants on the berm:** comfrey, lemongrass, yarrow, nettle and sterile vetiver grass, cut often for mulch and **compost** (plant waste rotted down into dark, crumbly soil food). The berm becomes a mulching bank.
- **Soil-builders:** clover, lucerne and sunn hemp. Let dandelion and plantain grow and cut them for mulch.
- **In the ditch:** cowpea or lupin as cover, and water-lovers such as amadumbe (taro, *Colocasia esculenta*). Keep amadumbe in garden swales and beds, away from streams and wetlands, where it can spread.
- **Below the swale:** lemons and other citrus, bananas where there is no frost, and tomatoes. Think about the shade they will cast.
- **Under the trees:** shade-tolerant **groundcover** (low plants that cover the soil) such as sweet potato and New Zealand spinach.

## Preparing the ground

Every time we turn the soil, we break the tunnels, fungi and crumbs that took years to build. So choose the method that disturbs the soil least. As the farm matures and makes its own mulch and compost, you need to disturb it less and less.

### Start the same way every time

Whatever tool follows, begin like this:

1. Pull out the worst spreading weeds, such as kikuyu runners and couch grass, which digging would multiply.
2. Cut the vegetation as close to the ground as you can.
3. Water well, or wait for rain, and let the soil drain until it is moist but not wet.

### By hand

For a small or medium area, use a pikmatok, a spade and a garden fork.

1. Take the cut material off for compost or mulch.
2. Loosen the topsoil with a fork.
3. Rake off any rubbish and level the surface.

### By tractor

Repeated ploughing is one of the main causes of soil erosion and soil carbon loss worldwide. Heavy machines pack the soil down, and ploughing every year forms a hard layer just below the blade, called a **plough pan**. Ploughing gives a quick burst of fertility as air rushes into the soil, then fertility falls season after season.

So if we use a tractor at all, we use it mainly to set the farm up. **Rippers and chisel ploughs** loosen hard soil without turning it over, and they are good for repairing damaged land. Choose the tools that turn the soil least, and make as few passes as you can.

A **two-wheel tractor** is a walk-behind machine that pulls a rotavator (spinning blades that break up the soil), a small plough or a mower. It costs far less to buy and run than a big tractor. Use the rotavator sparingly; frequent rotavating breaks soil structure too.

### Animal tractors

An **animal tractor** uses animals to clear and prepare land (see Chapter 5). Chickens, ducks, sheep, goats and cattle graze it down; pigs also dig.

1. Cut large plants to the ground. If you want pigs to dig, water the area first.
2. Put a movable fence around the area. An electric fence on a solar energiser works well.
3. Give the animals shelter inside if they need it, such as a mobile coop for chickens.
4. Follow cattle or pigs with chickens. They spread the manure and eat the fly larvae in it.
5. Loosen the soil with a fork or chisel plough, without turning it, and rake level.

### No-dig gardening and sheet mulching

No-dig gardening copies the forest floor. Leaves fall, rot and feed the soil, and it becomes loose and spongy with no digging at all. Earthworms and fungi do the work. Buried weed seeds stay in the dark and stay asleep, which saves hours of weeding. The price is a lot of organic material.

**Sheet mulching** (lasagne beds) builds no-dig layers on top of the ground. It smothers grass and feeds soil life from above, and it is the quickest way to start a small garden on hard, bare soil.

1. Cut the grass and bushes and leave them where they fall. Water well.
2. Spread animal manure 6 to 10 cm thick. Fresh manure goes only here, at the bottom, to wake up soil life.
3. Cover it with cardboard or newspaper, overlapping so no light gets through. Wet it.
4. Add 10 to 15 cm of straw or dry mulch, then a layer of compost.
5. Add another 10 to 15 cm of seed-free straw.
6. Finish with compost or good topsoil. What you plant into must be well-made compost or well-rotted manure, never fresh manure.
7. Water, open small holes through the top layers, plant seedlings into the compost, and mulch around them.

### Solarising and tarping

**Solarisation** cooks weeds with the sun. Water the soil, cover it tightly with **clear** plastic, bury the edges and leave it for at least 4 to 6 weeks in the hottest weather. It also kills some useful soil life, so use it only where you must.

**Tarping** uses **black** plastic. It heats the soil less and works by blocking the light. Cover the cut, watered area, hold the sheet down with rocks or sandbags and leave it 6 to 10 weeks. Then loosen the topsoil by rocking a fork back and forth, without turning it.

Market gardeners use a tarp to make a **stale seedbed**: prepare and water the bed, cover it, let the weed seeds sprout and die in the dark, then lift the tarp after a few weeks and sow into the clean bed.

## Forming growing beds for vegetables

A bed is where water, soil and your back meet. Shape it well and the soil improves every year. These are guidelines, not rules.

Raised beds suit moderate and high rainfall. In very dry areas make **sunken beds**, a little lower than the paths, so they catch the rain like basins.

If the slope is more than about 3% (a 3 m drop in 100 m), lay beds and paths **along the contour**, so heavy rain cannot run down them. On flat ground you are free to make circles, spirals and mandalas.

### Double-reach raised beds

A **double-reach bed** is narrow enough to reach the middle from the paths on both sides, so you never stand on it and pack the soil down.

- Beds 80 to 120 cm wide. Market gardens often use 75 to 90 cm, which suits hand tools and two-wheel tractors.
- Paths 40 to 60 cm wide.
- Beds about 8 m long in a small garden, up to 30 m in a commercial one.

Raised beds lift roots out of waterlogged soil and deepen the topsoil with soil from the paths, and compost goes only where the crops grow. Never trodden and hardly dug, the soil improves every year, until a garden fork sinks in to the handle.

1. Mark the top contour with an A-frame. If there is room, put a small swale above the top bed.
2. Measure the other beds and paths down the slope, and mark the edges with lime or ash. Lay a hosepipe along curved edges.
3. Starting at the top, dig soil from the path above each bed and throw it onto the bed below. Raise an average bed 10 to 15 cm.
4. Level each bed with a rake and make neat edges.
5. Add any lime or gypsum your soil test shows you need, and about 3 cm of compost.
6. Fork the bed through without turning it, mulch it thickly and water.
7. Rest the bed a few weeks before planting.

In small gardens, edge beds with rocks, thick branches or pallet wood. Lay cardboard over the grass inside, fill with good topsoil and top with compost. This works even on concrete.

### Trench beds

A **trench bed** is a deep bed filled with organic material. It turns rocky or clay ground into growing soil and holds water in dry areas. It is hard to dig, but lasts for years.

1. Mark a bed about **1 m wide** and 2 to 5 m long, and dig it about **60 cm deep**.
2. Put the dark topsoil on one side and the lighter subsoil on the other.
3. Lay sticks or coarse material in the bottom.
4. Fill with layers: about 20 cm of organic material (dry leaves, grass, kitchen scraps, manure), then about 10 cm of subsoil. Water every layer.
5. Finish with the topsoil, then mulch.

The bed sinks as it rots. Top it up with compost each season.

### Terrace beds

On steep land, a terrace is a level step cut into the slope. Its front bank works like the berm of a swale, holding water and soil. Build the walls of rocks, logs or old tyres. Where wattle has invaded, cut stems woven into screens hold soil well; use only dry stems without seed pods, so no seed spreads.

### Pit beds and banana circles

A **pit bed** is a round pit about 50 cm deep (up to 1 m) and 1 to 2 m across, filled with organic material. Plants grow around the rim and feed on the rot and the water in the middle. A **banana circle** is the best-known kind.

1. Choose a spot where water comes: below a tap, near a seep, or where greywater or runoff flows.
2. Lift the sods and the topsoil and set them aside.
3. Dig a small channel to lead water to the centre, then dig the pit and fill it with organic material.
4. Put the soil back as a low ring around the edge, plant it and mulch well.
5. Keep feeding the centre with organic material.

Bananas, amadumbe, sweet potato, ginger and pawpaw do well in frost-free areas. You can even put a slatted wooden floor over the pit and shower on it.

### Mandala and keyhole beds

Round beds, **keyhole beds** (a round bed with a path cut into the middle) and mandalas suit flat ground. From one spot you can reach much of the bed, so put the crops you pick most near the path. Put tall plants on the south side, because the sun is in the north.

## Irrigation

Every drop you carry or pump costs you something. The best irrigation puts water only at the roots, no faster than the soil can drink it. Water early in the morning or late in the afternoon, and mulch everything you water.

### Hand watering

A hose or can wastes water on paths and waters unevenly. Instead, make a small basin or furrow beside each plant or row, fill it, let it soak in, and move on. For seedlings use a can with a fine rose; water that has stood in the sun suits them better than cold borehole water.

### Bottle irrigation

1. Cut the bottom off an old plastic bottle.
2. Make a few small holes in the cap.
3. Bury the bottle neck-down beside the plant, with the open end just above the soil.
4. Fill it. Water seeps slowly to the roots.

### Drip irrigation

**Drip irrigation** lets water drip slowly onto the soil at each plant's roots. Paths stay dry, fewer weeds grow and little water is lost: about 90% reaches the roots, against roughly 75% to 85% for sprinklers.

- Use 16 mm or 20 mm drip line with built-in drippers 20 to 30 cm apart, laid under the mulch.
- Lay lines 30 to 50 cm apart; closer in sand, where water spreads less sideways. A 1 m bed needs 2 lines, a 1.5 m bed 3.
- Always fit a filter, because dirt blocks drippers, and flush the line ends now and then.

**Pressure from a tank:** every 10 m of height between the water in the tank and the tap gives about 1 bar. A tank on a 1 m stand gives only 0.1 bar, enough for a low-pressure gravity drip kit but not for ordinary drippers or sprinklers. Read the pressure your kit needs on the packet.

### Sprinklers

**Micro-sprinklers** suit **Zones** 1 and 2, the beds closest to the house that you visit every day. Each small head sits on a stake, fed by thin tubing from a low-pressure pipe, and wets a circle a few metres across. They lose more water to wind than drip does.

**Impact sprinklers**, the ones that click as they turn, throw water in a wide circle. They need more pressure, lose the most water to wind and sun, and wet leaves, which spreads leaf disease. Keep them for pasture and big fields.

## Using greywater for irrigation

Every bath and shower sends good water down the drain. On a dry farm, that water can keep a row of fruit trees alive through the dry season.

**Greywater** is used water from baths, showers, hand basins and the washing machine. **Blackwater** is water from toilets. Blackwater and nappy water carry germs and never go on the garden. Kitchen sink water is the dirtiest greywater, full of fat and food; use it only through a grease trap, and only on trees and flowers.

| Water source | On the garden? |
|---|---|
| Shower, bath, hand basin | Yes, with care |
| Washing machine | Yes, with mild, low-salt detergent |
| Kitchen sink | Only through a grease trap, on trees and flowers |
| Nappy washing or toilet | No |

### The rules

1. **Into the soil, under mulch.** Lead greywater into a mulched tree basin, swale or pit bed, through a pipe that ends under the mulch. Never spray it.
2. **Never on food eaten raw.** Keep it off morogo, lettuce and root crops such as carrots. Use it on fruit trees, bananas, shrubs and flowers.
3. **No pools.** It must soak in fast, where people and animals will not touch it.
4. **Use it the same day.** Stored greywater goes bad and breeds germs and mosquitoes.
5. **Far from drinking water.** Never near, or uphill of, a well, borehole or spring.
6. **Mild soaps.** Salts, bleach, borax and harsh chemicals build up and damage soil and plants. Use mild, biodegradable, low-salt soaps; liquid laundry soap usually carries less salt than powder.
7. **Move it around.** Rotate between basins and let rain rinse the soil. Yellow leaf edges or a white crust mean salt is building up.

### A simple greywater system

1. Find where the bath, shower and basin drain leaves the house, usually by the bathroom wall.
2. Join a PVC pipe to the outlet and run it to the garden, sloping gently downhill all the way.
3. Fit a flexible hose at the end, so you can move the outlet between mulched basins.
4. Keep the outlet buried in mulch.

Once greywater has soaked into healthy, mulched soil, soil life and roots break down most of what it carries.

## Try it

Build an A-frame and peg out your first contour. It costs nothing but three poles and an afternoon.

1. Tie the tops of two straight poles, about 2 m long and exactly the same length, into an upside-down V.
2. Tie a shorter pole across the middle of both legs, at the same height on each, to make an A.
3. Hang a stone on a string from the top so it swings freely past the crossbar.
4. Stand the frame on the slope and mark where the string crosses the bar. Turn the frame around so the legs swap places on exactly the same two spots, and mark again. The **level mark** is halfway between the two marks.
5. Knock in a peg where you want your line to start and stand one leg beside it.
6. Move the other leg up or down the slope until the string hangs on the level mark. Peg that spot.
7. Keep that leg still, swing the first leg around to the far side, find level again and peg. Carry on across the slope.
8. Walk the line and smooth out sharp zigzags. Lay stones or branches along it, and watch what the next rain does there.

## Key points

- Design for the biggest storm: start at the top, and give every earthwork a safe overflow.
- Rain caught (litres) = area (m²) × rainfall (mm) × runoff coefficient. A 120 m² metal roof in 750 mm country gives about 72 000 litres a year.
- Size the tank for the dry season, not the whole year's rain.
- Swales follow the contour exactly; space them so each holds a big storm.
- Disturb the soil as little as possible, water at the roots, and send greywater under mulch to trees.

Walk your land in the next heavy rain and watch where the water goes. It is already showing you where the first swale belongs.
