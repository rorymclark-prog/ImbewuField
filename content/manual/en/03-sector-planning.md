# Sector planning

Your land is not an island. Wind, fire, sun, water and cold air all arrive from beyond your fence, and they will keep arriving long after you have planted your first tree. You cannot stop them. You can decide where they meet your farm, and what is standing there when they do.

A farmer who knows where the hot wind comes from plants the windbreak in the right place the first time. A farmer who does not, plants it twice.

**By the end of this chapter you will be able to:**

- Find true north with a compass, the rising sun or a midday shadow
- Draw a sector map of the forces that cross your land
- Place windbreaks, firebreaks and green buffers where wind and fire usually come from
- Use the sun's angle and the flow of cold air to place beds, trees and the house
- Read your own climate, and choose plants and defences to match it

## Drawing a sector map

A **sector** is a force or energy that comes onto your land from outside: sun, wind, fire, water and floods, frost and cold air, good and bad views, wildlife paths, noise and dust.

A sector map draws each one as a wedge, like a slice of pie, pointing at your home from the direction it comes.

1. Take your base map (chapter 2) and mark the centre of your home or garden.
2. Mark true north.
3. For each force, draw a wedge from the direction it comes from. The hot, dry winter wind might be a wedge from the north-west.
4. Write the season beside each wedge. Most forces change with the seasons.

Then decide, for each force, whether to welcome it, block it or guide it. A cool summer breeze you let into the house; a cold winter wind you block. The map shows where the **windbreak** (a belt of trees or shrubs that slows the wind) and the **firebreak** (a strip of land with nothing to burn) belong, which views to keep open, and where the winter sun falls.

## Finding north

Every sector hangs on north. Here are three simple ways to find it.

### With a compass

A compass points to magnetic north, not true north. In South Africa, magnetic north lies about 20 to 28 degrees west of true north: in 2025 about 21 degrees in Johannesburg, 27 in Cape Town and 28 in Durban. So true north is a little to the right (east) of the needle. Many phone compass apps show true north for you.

### With the rising sun

Face the rising sun: north is on your left, south on your right. But the sunrise moves. It is due east only around 21 March and 23 September. In December it rises about 26 to 29 degrees south of east, and in June about 26 to 29 degrees north of east.

### With your midday shadow

At midday the sun is in the north for almost all of Southern Africa, so your shadow points south. Midday by the sun falls between about 11:45 and 13:00 on the clock, later in the west. Only in far northern Limpopo in midsummer can it sit slightly south.

> **Tip:** Once you have found north, mark it in the garden with a painted stone or post, and you will never have to find it again.

## Wind

The sun heats land, sea and rock unevenly. Warm air rises, cool air sinks, and air rushes from high pressure to low. That is wind.

Wind dries soil and plants, breaks crops and trees, blows away **topsoil** (the dark, living top layer of the soil) and drives fires. It also pumps water, cools a house in summer and carries maize pollen.

### Winds in Southern Africa

| Wind | Where | When | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| Berg wind | Coast and escarpment of the Western, Southern and Eastern Cape and KwaZulu-Natal | Mostly winter, often a day or two before a cold front | Hot, very dry wind from the interior down to the coast. Very high fire danger. |
| Cold front winds | Western Cape most strongly, then the rest of the country | Winter | North-westerlies bring rain to the Western Cape. Behind the front, cold southerlies bring frost inland and snow on high ground. |
| South-easter ("Cape Doctor") | Cape Town and the south-western Cape | Summer | Strong, dry, gusty wind that dries soil, damages crops and spreads fire. |
| August winds | Highveld and the interior | Late winter and early spring | Dry, gusty winds over dry veld. Dust and very high fire danger. |

### Find your main wind directions

1. Watch for a year: the leaning trees, the smoke, the dust, the washing on the line.
2. Put up a wind vane or a windsock, a cloth tube on a pole.
3. Ask the older people nearby which winds are the worst, and when they come.
4. Name each wind by where it comes from: a north-westerly blows from the north-west.
5. Draw each one on your sector map with its season, and plant windbreaks across the harmful ones (chapter 6).

## Fire

Grassland, savanna and fynbos need fire at the right intervals. But a runaway veld fire kills people and livestock, and burns grazing, crops, houses and fences.

In summer-rainfall areas the fire season runs from about May to October, worst in late winter and early spring before the rains. In the winter-rainfall Western Cape it runs from about December to March. In the Southern Cape and KwaZulu-Natal, winter berg-wind days bring sudden, very high fire danger.

### How fire behaves

Fire can come from any direction, but on most land it comes mainly from one: your fire sector. It is usually the direction of the hot, dry wind, and your neighbours will know it.

Fire runs uphill much faster than down. Hold a burning match upside down and watch the flame climb. A common rule of thumb is that fire speed doubles for every 10 degrees of uphill slope, so on a 20-degree slope it spreads about four times as fast as on flat ground. A house at the top of a steep, grassy slope is in the most danger. Put your defences downhill, and in the fire sector.

Protect people and the house first, then livestock, sheds, stored feed and fruit trees.

### Ways to reduce fire risk

1. **Reduce fuel.** In the fire sector and around buildings, keep grass short and clear dry leaves and dead wood. Store firewood, hay and thatch away from the house.
2. **Make firebreaks.** Clear a strip of everything that can burn. Roads, ploughed land and dams work as firebreaks too. Burn a firebreak only on a cool, still day when the fire danger is low, never alone, and with enough people, water and fire beaters to hold it.
3. **Plant green, slow-burning plants.** No plant is fireproof, but fleshy, moist leaves with little oil or resin burn slowly: aloes, spekboom (*Portulacaria afra*, which dislikes hard frost), *Cotyledon*, vygies (*Delosperma*), sour fig (*Carpobrotus edulis*), bietou (*Osteospermum moniliferum*) and num-num (*Carissa*).
4. **Keep oily trees away from buildings.** Pines, gums and wattles burn fiercely.
5. **Keep water ready**: a tank and hosepipe near the house, and fire beaters or a knapsack sprayer.
6. **Stack functions.** A watered garden or orchard around the house is a green buffer, and a strip of slow-burning plants slows wind as well as fire.

## Sunlight

Most vegetables and fruit need at least six hours of direct sun a day, and morning sun is the most precious. But bare soil in hard sun bakes dry, and its life suffers: earthworms go deeper or die. Keep the soil covered with **mulch** (a layer of dry grass, leaves or straw) or with plants, and give tomatoes and peppers some afternoon shade in very hot areas.

### Sun angles

South Africa lies between about 22 and 35 degrees south of the Equator. At midday the sun is in the north: high in summer, low in winter, so winter shadows are much longer.

| Place | Midday sun height, midwinter (21 June) | Midday sun height, midsummer (21 December) | Midday shadow of a 2 m wall, midwinter |
|---|---|---|---|
| Polokwane (24° S) | 43° | almost overhead | about 2.2 m |
| Johannesburg (26° S) | 40° | 87° | about 2.4 m |
| Durban (30° S) | 37° | 84° | about 2.7 m |
| Cape Town (34° S) | 33° | 80° | about 3.1 m |

In winter a bed on the south side of a building or big tree gets little sun. So put tall trees and buildings on the south side of the garden, and the short plants on the north.

### Using the sun

- **Deciduous trees and vines** drop their leaves in winter. Plant them north of the house for summer shade and winter sun: grape vines, figs, white stinkwood (*Celtis africana*) and wild pear (*Dombeya rotundifolia*).
- **Thermal mass** is heavy material, such as brick, stone, earth or water, that soaks up heat by day and gives it back at night. Put it where the winter sun reaches it, such as behind north-facing windows.
- **Roof overhangs.** Make the north eave wide enough to shade the windows from the high summer sun, but short enough to let the low winter sun in.

## Climate

Climate is the average weather of a place over many years. More than anything, it decides what you can grow.

South Africa is dry. It averages about 464 mm of rain a year, against a world average of about 860 mm, and it gets drier from east to west. Most of the country, with Lesotho, Eswatini and Zimbabwe, gets **summer rain**, mainly from about November to March, and dry winters. The south-western Western Cape gets **winter rain**, mainly from about May to August, and hot, dry summers. The southern Cape coast gets rain all year. Height matters as much as place: the Highveld has frost every winter, while the KwaZulu-Natal coast and Lowveld are almost frost-free.

Learn your own: when the first rains come, and when the first and last frosts fall.

> **Tip:** Keep a rain gauge and a thermometer, and write down the rain and the coldest temperature every day. In a few years you will know your climate better than any map does.

### Microclimates

A **microclimate** is a small area whose climate differs from the land around it. Each one lets you grow something you could not grow before. Find them by watching where frost lies longest and where plants flower early. Valley floors and river banks are cold. Corners behind walls are warm and out of the wind: put tomatoes against a wall that faces north.

To make warm microclimates in cold areas:

- **Build on the thermal belt.** On clear, still nights, cold air flows downhill like water and pools in low places called **frost pockets**. A band part-way up the slope stays warmer. Put the house and frost-tender crops there.
- **Plant trees uphill.** A belt of trees above the house slows and turns aside the cold air flowing down at night.
- **Use water.** Even a small pond or tank near the house evens out the temperature.
- **Face the house north**, and put a **compost** heap (rotting plant waste) inside a plastic tunnel for winter warmth.

### Warm, temperate and dry climates

**Warm areas**, such as the Lowveld, the KwaZulu-Natal coast and the hot Limpopo valleys, have little or no frost. Grow avocado, banana, mango, litchi, papaya (paw-paw), macadamia and citrus.

**Temperate areas** have warm summers and cold, often frosty, winters. Many deciduous fruit trees need winter cold, called "chill", to flower and fruit well; in warmer places choose low-chill varieties. Grow apple, pear, plum, peach, apricot, almond, fig, grape, pomegranate, persimmon, black mulberry and pecan.

**Dry areas**, such as the Karoo and much of the Northern Cape, get well under 500 mm of rain a year, and parts less than 200 mm. Sorghum, pearl millet and cowpeas do better than maize here.

## Hydrology

Hydrology is the story of water on your land. Before you plan, find out which water you can use (streams, springs, boreholes, taps), your rainy season and yearly rainfall, how well your soil drains and holds water, how much rain your roofs can catch, and what climate change may do to your rain.

Start with the roof. 1 mm of rain on 1 square metre gives 1 litre of water. A 50 square metre roof in an area with 500 mm of rain a year can catch up to 25,000 litres a year, less some losses.

These numbers set the limits of what your land can grow.

## Frost

Frost comes every winter to most of the interior: the Highveld, Free State, Karoo, the Eastern Cape and KwaZulu-Natal midlands, and Lesotho. It also settles in cold valleys elsewhere. Most of the coast and the Lowveld get little or none.

On clear, still nights, the ground and plants lose their heat to the open sky and grow colder than the air. Water vapour freezes onto them as white ice: white frost. The cold air slides downhill and pools, so frost is worst in low places. When the air is very dry, plants can freeze with no ice at all. This is **black frost**, and it gives no warning: next day the leaves and stems turn black and die.

Frost can stunt a young fruit tree for years. Prepare before winter.

1. **Cover plants on frosty nights** with frost cloth, old sheets or straw, reaching down to the ground. Take the covers off in the morning.
2. **Shelter the garden** with a windbreak or living fence, but leave a gap on the downhill side so cold air can drain out instead of pooling.
3. **Steer cold air around the garden** with a horseshoe-shaped hedge on the uphill side, open end facing downhill.
4. **Make sun traps.** A north-facing wall or curved hedge holds warm air, and rocks or drums of water around plants store the day's heat.
5. **Plant nurse trees.** A light **canopy** (a leafy roof) of short-lived pioneer trees shelters young fruit trees.
6. **Mulch with care.** Mulch protects roots, but it stops the soil giving up its heat at night, so very low plants can get more frost above thick mulch. On frost-prone beds, water the soil the day before a frosty night: moist soil stores more heat.
7. **Use guilds.** A **guild** is a group of plants grown together to help each other, including against frost (chapter 6).

## Climate change

**Climate change** is the long-term shift in weather patterns, driven mainly by human activity: more heat, changing rain, wilder weather and less water.

Southern Africa is warming faster than the world average, parts of the interior about twice as fast. Heatwaves are more common, and more droughts and fire-danger days are expected. Climate change made the 2015 to 2017 Cape Town drought about three times more likely.

### Designing for a changing climate

We cannot count on a normal year any more. So we design for the bad one, and build **resilience**: the ability to take a shock and recover.

1. Plant many different crops, so one failure does not ruin you.
2. Choose tough plants, including **indigenous** ones that grow here naturally, and **perennials**, which live for many seasons.
3. Keep hardy local breeds: indigenous chickens, goats and Nguni cattle.
4. Plant windbreaks and store water to soften extreme weather.
5. Keep the soil covered with cover crops and mulch, and help rain soak in with **swales**, level ditches dug across the slope.
6. Preserve your surplus. Dry it, pickle it in plenty of vinegar, or make jam and chutney. Bottling low-acid food such as beans, pumpkin or meat at home without a tested method can cause botulism, a deadly food poisoning.
7. Save your own seed, so it adapts to your land, and share it through a community seed bank.
8. Move grazing animals often, and give the veld time to rest.
9. Build off the flood plain. Keep firebreaks and water ready, and store food, seed and fodder for hard times.

## Try it

Draw your first sector map this week.

1. On a sunny day, stand a straight stick upright in open ground. Around midday, when its shadow is shortest, the shadow points south.
2. Sketch your home, garden and boundary on paper, with a north arrow.
3. Walk the land. Note where the strongest winds come from, where past fires came from, where water runs in after rain, and where frost lies longest.
4. Draw each force as a wedge pointing at your home, with its season.
5. Ask an older neighbour about the worst wind and fire they remember, and add them.
6. Circle the force that worries you most. That is where your first windbreak, firebreak or hedge will go.

## Key points

- A sector map shows where sun, wind, fire, water, frost, views and noise come from, so you can welcome, block or guide each one.
- A compass in South Africa points about 20 to 28 degrees west of true north; the midday sun is in the north.
- Fire runs uphill fast: put fire defences downhill and in the fire sector.
- The low winter sun comes from the north, and cold air pools in low places. Plan beds, trees and the house around both.
- Southern Africa is warming fast. Answer it with diversity, perennials, covered soil, stored water and saved seed.

Walk your land in every season with the map in your hand. Each season will add a wedge you had not seen.
