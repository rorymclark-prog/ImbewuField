# Sector planning

Sector planning is about the forces that come from outside your land and pass through it or affect it. You cannot control these forces, but you can plan for them.

The main forces to plan for are:

- fire,
- wind,
- sun, and how its angle changes between winter and summer,
- water flows, including floods,
- frost and cold air,
- good views and bad views,
- wildlife corridors (paths that animals use to move across the land),
- noise and dust.

## Drawing a sector map

A sector map shows the direction each force comes from. Each force is drawn as a wedge, like a slice of pie, pointing at your house or garden.

1. Take your base map (see chapter 2) and mark the centre of your home or garden.
2. Mark true north on the map.
3. For each force, draw a wedge from the direction it comes from. For example, draw the hot, dry winter wind as a wedge from the north-west.
4. Write the season next to each wedge, because many forces change with the seasons.

Then decide, for each force, whether you want to welcome it, block it or guide it. In a hot area you may want to let a cool breeze into the house. In another place you may need to block a harsh, cold wind. Sector planning helps you place windbreaks and firebreaks, keep good views open and make the most of the winter sun.

## Finding north

You need to know north, south, east and west before you can map any sector. There are three simple ways to find them.

### With a compass

A compass needle points to magnetic north, not true north. In South Africa magnetic north is about 20 to 28 degrees west of true north. For example, in 2025 the difference was about 21 degrees in Johannesburg, 27 degrees in Cape Town and 28 degrees in Durban. So true north is a little to the right (east) of where the needle points. Many phone compass apps can show true north for you.

### With the rising sun

The sun rises in the east and sets in the west. Stand facing the rising sun: north is on your left, south is on your right and west is behind you.

But the place where the sun rises moves a lot during the year. It rises exactly in the east only around 21 March and 23 September. In South Africa, at midsummer (December) it rises about 26 to 29 degrees south of east. At midwinter (June) it rises about 26 to 29 degrees north of east.

### With your midday shadow

In the middle of the day the sun is in the north for almost all of Southern Africa. At that time your shadow points south. The middle of the sun's day falls between about 11:45 and 13:00 on South African clocks, and later the further west you are. The only exception is the far north of Limpopo in midsummer, where the midday sun can be slightly to the south.

> **Tip:** Once you have found north, mark it in the garden with a painted stone or post so you always remember it.

## Wind

Wind is air moving from an area of high air pressure to an area of low pressure. The sun heats land, sea and rock unevenly. Warm air rises and cool air sinks, and this creates the pressure differences that make wind.

Wind can harm your farm. It can:

- dry out soil and plants,
- break crops and trees,
- damage buildings,
- blow away topsoil,
- spread fires quickly.

Wind can also help. It can pump water with a windmill and make electricity with a wind turbine. A cool breeze can cool your house in summer. Some plants, such as maize and many grasses, rely on wind to carry their pollen.

### Winds in Southern Africa

Winds carry their own temperature and moisture. Learn the winds of your area and the season in which each one blows.

| Wind | Where | When | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| Berg wind | Coast and escarpment of the Western, Southern and Eastern Cape and KwaZulu-Natal | Mostly winter, often a day or two before a cold front | Hot, very dry wind that blows from the interior down to the coast. Very high fire danger. |
| Cold front winds | Western Cape most strongly, then the rest of the country | Winter | Strong north-westerly winds bring rain to the Western Cape. After the front passes, cold southerly winds bring frost inland and snow on high mountains. |
| South-easter ("Cape Doctor") | Cape Town and the south-western Cape | Summer | Strong, dry, gusty wind that dries soil, damages crops and spreads fires. |
| August winds | Highveld and the interior | Late winter and early spring | Dry, gusty winds, often from the west or north-west, over dry veld. Dust and very high fire danger. |
| Cool sea winds | West coast, next to the cold Benguela current | All year | Cool air that can bring fog. |

Cold fronts are storm systems that come from the ocean in the south-west. They do not start in the mountains, but they bring the most snow to high ground such as the Drakensberg, the Lesotho highlands and the Cape mountains.

### Find your main wind directions

1. Watch for a year. Look at leaning trees, smoke, dust and washing on the line.
2. Put up a wind vane or a windsock (a cloth tube on a pole). These show the wind direction and give an idea of its strength.
3. Ask neighbours and older people which winds are the worst, and when they blow.
4. Name each wind by the direction it comes from. A north-westerly wind blows from the north-west.
5. Draw each important wind on your sector map, with its season.
6. Plant windbreaks across the path of harmful winds (see chapter 6).

## Fire

### Fire in Southern African landscapes

Grassland, savanna and fynbos are adapted to fire. They need fire at the right intervals to stay healthy. But uncontrolled veldfires can kill people and livestock, and burn grazing, crops, houses, fences and trees. The smoke harms health, and burning releases greenhouse gases.

The fire season depends on where you live:

- **Summer-rainfall areas** (most of South Africa, and Lesotho, Eswatini and Zimbabwe): fires happen mostly in the dry months from about May to October. Late winter and early spring, before the first rains, is the most dangerous time.
- **Winter-rainfall Western Cape:** fires happen mostly in the hot, dry summer, from about December to March.
- **Southern Cape and KwaZulu-Natal:** berg-wind days in winter can bring sudden, very high fire danger.

As the climate gets hotter and drier, scientists expect more days of high fire danger in South Africa.

### How fire behaves

- Fire can come from any direction, but in most places it usually comes from one main direction. This is your fire sector. It is often the direction of the hot, dry winds. Ask your neighbours and your local Fire Protection Association.
- Fire runs uphill much faster than downhill. Think of a match: held upside down, it burns much faster. A common rule of thumb is that a fire's speed doubles for every 10 degrees of uphill slope. On a 20-degree slope, a fire can spread about four times faster than on flat ground.
- So a house at the top of a steep, grassy slope is at high risk. Put your fire defences on the downhill side and in the direction that fires usually come from.

When you design for fire, protect the most valuable things first: people and the house, then livestock, sheds, stored feed and the food forest.

### Ways to reduce fire risk

1. **Reduce fuel.** In the fire sector and around buildings, keep grass short, rake up dry leaves and remove dead wood. Store firewood, hay and thatch away from the house.
2. **Make firebreaks.** A firebreak is a strip of land cleared of material that can burn. Roads, ploughed land, dams and ponds can also act as firebreaks. See the law section below.
3. **Plant green, fire-resistant plants.** No plant is fireproof, but plants with fleshy, moist leaves and little oil or resin burn slowly. Good South African examples are aloes, spekboom (*Portulacaria afra*), *Cotyledon*, vygies (*Delosperma* and related plants), sour fig (*Carpobrotus edulis*), bietou (*Osteospermum moniliferum*) and num-num (*Carissa* species). Choose ones that suit your climate, because some, such as spekboom, do not like hard frost.
4. **Keep flammable trees away from buildings.** Pines, gum trees and wattles contain oil or resin and burn fiercely. Many are also listed invasive plants.
5. **Keep water ready.** Have a water tank, hosepipe or sprinklers near the house, plus fire beaters or a knapsack sprayer.
6. **Stack functions.** A watered kitchen garden or orchard around the house acts as a green buffer. A strip of fire-resistant plants can also work as a windbreak. Ask your Fire Protection Association whether it counts as a legal firebreak.

### The law: National Veld and Forest Fire Act (101 of 1998)

This Act applies to landowners in South Africa. If you farm on communal or rented land, ask the owner, the traditional council or your local Fire Protection Association who is responsible.

- **Firebreaks.** If a veldfire could start on, burn on or spread from your land, you must prepare and maintain a firebreak on your side of the boundary with each neighbour. Neighbours may agree to put the firebreak somewhere else.
- **What a firebreak must be.** It must be wide and long enough to have a reasonable chance of stopping a fire. It must not cause soil erosion. It must be reasonably free of material that could carry a fire across it. The Act does not set one fixed width, so ask your Fire Protection Association what is usual in your area.
- **Burning a firebreak.** Try to agree on a date with your neighbours. If you cannot agree, give your neighbours and the Fire Protection Association at least 14 days' written notice. Burn only when the fire danger allows.
- **Fire danger warnings.** The South African Weather Service issues fire danger ratings. When the fire danger is rated high or extreme for your area, it is a crime to light or use a fire in the open air.
- **Be ready to fight fire.** You must have the equipment, protective clothing and trained people needed to put out a fire. When you are away, responsible people must be nearby to fight a fire and warn your neighbours and the Fire Protection Association.
- **Fire Protection Associations.** These are groups of landowners who work together to prevent and fight veldfires. It is wise to join. If a fire spreads from your land and someone sues you, the court presumes you were negligent unless you prove otherwise. This presumption does not apply to members of a Fire Protection Association.

> **Safety:** Never burn a firebreak alone, on a hot or windy day, or when the fire danger is high. Have enough people, water and fire beaters ready. Get training from your Fire Protection Association or the Working on Fire programme first.

## Sunlight

Plants need sunlight to grow. Most vegetables and fruit need at least six hours of direct sun a day. Morning sun is especially valuable. In very hot areas, some shade in the afternoon helps.

Plants can also get too much sun. Leaves and fruit, for example tomatoes and peppers, can scorch. Bare soil in strong sun gets very hot and dry, and this harms soil life. Earthworms move deeper or die. Keep the soil covered with mulch or plants, and use shade cloth or taller plants where the sun is harshest.

### Sun angles

South Africa lies between about 22 and 35 degrees south of the Equator. At midday the sun is in the north. It is high in the sky in summer and low in winter, so winter shadows are much longer.

| Place | Midday sun height in midwinter (21 June) | Midday sun height in midsummer (21 December) | Midday shadow of a 2 m wall in midwinter |
|---|---|---|---|
| Polokwane (24° S) | 43° | almost overhead | about 2.2 m |
| Johannesburg (26° S) | 40° | 87° | about 2.4 m |
| Durban (30° S) | 37° | 84° | about 2.7 m |
| Cape Town (34° S) | 33° | 80° | about 3.1 m |

In the morning and afternoon, shadows are much longer than these midday lengths. In winter, a bed on the south side of a building or a tall tree gets little sun. Put tall trees and buildings on the south side of your garden, and short plants on the north side.

### Using the sun

- **Deciduous trees and vines** lose their leaves in winter. Plant them on the north side of the house for shade in summer and sun in winter. Examples are grape vines, figs, white stinkwood (*Celtis africana*) and wild pear (*Dombeya rotundifolia*).
- **Thermal mass.** Brick, stone, earth and water soak up heat during the day and give it off at night. Put them where the winter sun can reach them, for example behind north-facing windows.
- **Roof overhangs.** On the north side of a house, make the roof overhang (eave) wide enough to shade the windows from the high summer sun, but short enough to let the low winter sun shine in.
- **West-facing windows** let in hot afternoon sun. In hot areas, keep them small or shade them.

## Climate

Climate is the average weather of a place over many years: sun, rain, mist, frost, snow, wind and temperature. Climate is the main thing that limits which plants and animals you can keep.

### South Africa's climate

- South Africa is a dry country. The average rainfall is about 464 mm a year, while the world average is about 860 mm. In general, rainfall gets lower from east to west.
- **Summer-rainfall region:** most of the country, including the Highveld, Lowveld, Free State, KwaZulu-Natal and the Eastern Cape interior, and also Lesotho, Eswatini and Zimbabwe. Rain falls mainly from about November to March, often in thunderstorms. Winters are dry, and cold with frost inland.
- **Winter-rainfall region:** the south-western part of the Western Cape. Rain falls mainly from about May to August, brought by cold fronts. Summers are hot, dry and windy.
- **All-year rainfall:** the southern Cape coast, east of Cape Agulhas.
- **Height above sea level matters.** The Highveld has frost every winter. The Drakensberg and the Lesotho highlands get snow and hard frost. The KwaZulu-Natal coast and the Lowveld are warm and almost frost-free.

> **Note:** Lesotho example. Lesotho's lowlands can be hotter than 30 °C in summer, but winter nights fall well below freezing. Snow is common in the highlands above about 2,000 m.

Learn your own climate. When do the first rains usually fall? When are the first and last frosts? Is it hot and dry, or misty and wet? The answers decide which fruit trees you plant, and which vegetables you plant, where and when.

> **Tip:** Keep a simple rain gauge and a thermometer, and write down the rain and the coldest temperatures each day. After a few years you will know your climate better than any map.

### Micro-climates

A micro-climate is a small area with a climate that is different from the area around it. Slope, aspect, position on the hill, water bodies, walls, trees and rocks all create micro-climates. You can also create new ones. Different micro-climates let you grow a wider range of plants.

You find micro-climates by watching and by trial and error. Notice where frost lies first, where it melts first, and where plants flower early. Examples:

- **Low-lying areas and valley floors** collect cold air at night and get the worst frost.
- **River banks** are moist and cool, and often frosty.
- **Sheltered corners** behind walls and fences are warm and protected from wind.

Put sun-loving plants such as tomatoes against a wall that faces north (the sunny side). The wall gives them extra warmth and some protection from frost.

Ways to create good micro-climates in cooler areas:

- **Build on the thermal belt.** On clear, still nights, cold air flows downhill like water and collects in valleys. These cold spots are called frost pockets. A band part-way up the slope stays warmer. This is the thermal belt. Build your house and plant frost-tender crops there, not on the valley floor.
- **Plant trees uphill.** A belt of trees above the house slows and turns aside cold air flowing down the slope at night.
- **Use water.** Even a small pond or tank near the house helps to even out the temperature.
- **Face houses north** to catch the winter sun.
- **Use deciduous plants** for summer shade and winter sun.
- **Use compost heat.** A compost heap inside a plastic tunnel gives off heat in winter.
- **Use plants to cool.** In summer, plants cool the air around them with their shade and the water they release.

### Temperature and climate types

Temperature is measured with a thermometer in degrees Celsius (°C). Water freezes at 0 °C. Choose your plants and planting seasons to suit your temperatures and climate type.

There are formal systems for sorting climates, such as the Köppen–Geiger system. In this manual we use three simple groups.

### Warm climates (tropical and subtropical)

A true tropical climate is one where even the coldest month averages 18 °C or more. Very few places in South Africa are truly tropical. The warm subtropical areas, such as the Lowveld, the KwaZulu-Natal coast and the hot valleys of Limpopo, have little or no frost. They suit fruits that need long, hot summers.

Examples: avocado, banana, mango, litchi, papaya (paw-paw), macadamia, coffee, custard apple and citrus (orange, naartjie, lemon). Citrus also grows well in many areas with mild winters.

> **Note:** Guava, purple granadilla (*Passiflora edulis*) and tree tomato (*Solanum betaceum*) are listed invasive plants under NEMBA (2020) in several provinces, including KwaZulu-Natal, the Eastern Cape, Limpopo and Mpumalanga. White mulberry (*Morus alba*) is listed everywhere in South Africa. Check your province's rules before you plant them. Black mulberry (*Morus nigra*) is not listed.

### Temperate climates

Temperate climates have warm summers and cold winters, often with frost. Frost-hardy plants survive the winter without damage.

Many deciduous fruit trees come from places with cold winters. They need a period of winter cold (called "chill") to flower and fruit well. In warmer areas, choose low-chill varieties.

Examples: apple, pear, plum, peach, apricot, almond, fig, grape, pomegranate, persimmon, kiwi fruit, pecan nut (which needs a lot of water), strawberry and blueberry.

Cool-season vegetables such as cabbage, kale, beetroot, peas and broad beans do not need frost. Grow them in the cool months of the year.

Warm-season crops such as melons, pumpkins, beans and maize grow in the warm months almost everywhere.

### Arid and semi-arid climates

Arid areas get less than about 250 mm of rain a year. Semi-arid areas get roughly 250 to 500 mm. Much of the Northern Cape and the Karoo is arid or semi-arid. In these areas, and in most of South Africa, more water can evaporate each year than falls as rain. Drought-hardy crops such as sorghum, pearl millet and cowpeas do better here than maize.

Whatever your climate, choose plants that suit it. Then you will not waste time and money on plants that cannot thrive.

## Hydrology

Hydrology is the study of water on your land: where it comes from, how it moves and how it affects living things. When you plan your land, find out:

- which water sources you can use: streams, springs, boreholes and taps,
- the rainfall in your area: the rainy season and the average rain per year,
- how well your soil drains and how much water it can hold,
- how much water you can catch from roofs and other hard surfaces,
- what climate change is expected to do to rainfall in your area.

To work out how much water a roof can catch: 1 mm of rain on 1 square metre of roof gives 1 litre of water. For example, a roof of 50 square metres in an area with 500 mm of rain a year can catch up to 25,000 litres a year, less some losses.

This information helps you set limits for what you can grow. It also helps you choose the best ways to catch and store water, and the right plants and animals. Every plant and animal has its own water needs, and you must meet them to get a good yield.

> **Note:** Under the National Water Act (36 of 1998), you may without a licence take water for reasonable household use and a small garden that is not for business, water animals (but not a feedlot) that graze your land, and collect rainwater from your roof. Using more water, for example for business irrigation or a dam on a stream, may need registration or a licence. Ask the Department of Water and Sanitation.

## Frost

Frost is common in winter across most of the South African interior: the Highveld, Free State, Karoo, the Eastern Cape and KwaZulu-Natal midlands, and Lesotho. It also forms in cold valleys elsewhere. Most coastal areas and the Lowveld get little or none.

### How frost forms

Frost forms on clear, still, cold nights. The ground and plants lose their heat to the open sky and become colder than the air. Water vapour then freezes on them as white ice crystals. This is white frost. Cold air flows downhill and collects in low places, so frost is usually worst there.

Frost can badly damage plant tissue. It can stunt young fruit trees for years. How badly frost hits your garden depends on your height above sea level, how far south you are, your place on the slope and how close you are to a large body of water.

### Black frost

When the air is very dry, plants can freeze without any white ice forming. This is called black frost. There is no white warning. The next day the leaves and stems turn black and die. Most tender plants will not recover, but frost-hardy plants survive.

### Protecting plants from frost

Protect your plants before winter starts. It is better to be early than late.

1. **Cover plants on frosty nights** with frost cloth, old sheets, straw or dry grass. Covers that reach the ground keep in more of the soil's heat. Remove them in the morning.
2. **Shelter the garden.** A windbreak or living fence around the garden keeps out cold wind. Leave a gap on the downhill side so that cold air can drain away and does not get trapped in the garden.
3. **Steer cold air around the garden.** Plant a horseshoe-shaped hedge on the uphill side, with the open end facing downhill.
4. **Make sun traps.** A north-facing wall, a bank or a curved hedge catches the sun and holds warm air.
5. **Store heat.** Rocks, bricks or drums of water around plants soak up heat during the day and give it off at night.
6. **Plant nurse trees.** A light canopy of short-lived pioneer trees protects young fruit trees and other tender plants from frost.
7. **Mulch with care.** Mulch protects roots and soil life through winter. But mulch also stops the soil giving off its stored heat at night, so very low, tender plants can get more frost above thick mulch. On frost-prone beds, water the soil the day before a frosty night, because moist soil stores more heat.
8. **Use guilds.** Groups of plants grown together shelter each other (see chapter 6).

## Climate change

Climate change is a long-term change in weather patterns, in one area or across the world. It brings higher temperatures, changes in rainfall, more extreme weather and less water. These can damage land, lower farm yields and lead to hunger and water shortages. It is one of the biggest threats to farming today.

### What is happening in Southern Africa

- Southern Africa is warming faster than the world average. Parts of the South African interior have warmed about twice as fast as the global average.
- Very hot days and heatwaves have become more common, and cold nights less common. Scientists expect this to continue.
- Droughts that harm crops and veld are expected to become more common in southern Africa as the world gets warmer.
- Climate change made the 2015 to 2017 Cape Town drought about three times more likely.
- More days of high fire danger are expected.

### Designing for a changing climate

In permaculture we plan for variable weather and for disasters. Ways to build resilience:

- Plant many different crops, so one failure does not ruin you.
- Choose plants that can handle a wide range of conditions, including indigenous plants.
- Grow perennials: trees, shrubs and other plants that live for many seasons.
- Keep hardy local breeds of livestock, such as indigenous chickens, goats and Nguni cattle.
- Plant windbreaks and store water to buffer extreme weather.
- Keep the soil covered with cover crops and mulch.
- Help rain soak into the soil with swales, mulch and plant cover.
- Preserve surplus food by drying, pickling and making jams and chutneys.
- Save your own seed so that it adapts to local conditions, and share seed through a community seed bank.
- Use planned grazing: move animals often and give the veld time to rest and recover.

> **Safety:** Bottling low-acid foods such as beans, pumpkin, other vegetables or meat at home can cause botulism, a deadly food poisoning, unless a tested method is used. Pickle vegetables with enough vinegar, make jams from fruit, or dry your food instead. Follow a tested recipe.

Disasters such as floods, droughts, fires, severe storms and tropical cyclones can damage property and kill livestock and people. Climate change is making some of these, especially heatwaves, droughts and fire weather, more frequent or more severe in Southern Africa. Plan for them: do not build on flood plains, keep firebreaks and water ready, and store food, seed and fodder for hard times.

## Key points

- A sector map shows the direction of outside forces such as fire, wind, sun, water, frost, views and noise.
- Find true north: a compass in South Africa points about 20 to 28 degrees west of true north, and the midday sun is in the north.
- Learn your local winds: berg winds, cold fronts, the Cape south-easter and the August winds all bring fire or cold.
- The fire season is winter and spring in summer-rainfall areas, and summer in the Western Cape.
- Fire runs uphill much faster; put fire defences downhill and in the fire sector.
- By law, landowners must keep firebreaks, give 14 days' notice before burning them and not burn when fire danger is high. Join your Fire Protection Association.
- The winter sun is low in the north; keep the north side of beds and houses open to it.
- Most of South Africa gets summer rain; the Western Cape gets winter rain; the country is dry overall.
- Cold air collects in low places; build and plant frost-tender crops on the thermal belt.
- Check NEMBA before planting guava, granadilla, tree tomato or mulberry.
- Southern Africa is warming fast; plan for heat, drought and fire with diversity, perennials, soil cover and stored water and seed.
