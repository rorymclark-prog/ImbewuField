# Vegetable and staple crops

## Growing food in Southern African climates

South Africa has several different climates, so there is no single planting calendar.

- **Summer-rainfall interior** (Highveld, Free State, much of the Eastern Cape, KwaZulu-Natal Midlands, Lesotho): most rain falls from about October to March. Winters are dry and cold, with frost in many places. The main growing season is spring and summer.
- **Winter-rainfall Western Cape**: most rain falls from about May to September. Summers are hot and dry, so summer crops need irrigation.
- **Frost-free lowveld and coast** (for example the KwaZulu-Natal coast and the Limpopo and Mpumalanga lowveld): you can grow many crops for most of the year if you have water.

In the colder areas the growing season is short and winter frost is the main danger. When you design your food gardens, think about the following.

### Protect crops from frost

Cold air is heavy. On a still, clear night it flows downhill like water and collects in low places. These places are called **frost pockets**.

- Do not trap cold air behind walls, buildings or thick hedges on the uphill side of your garden. Leave a gap low down so that cold air can keep flowing downhill and away.
- Plant frost-tender crops higher up the slope, not at the bottom of a valley.
- North-facing slopes get the most sun in winter and are the warmest places for early and late crops.

### Extend the growing season

A plastic tunnel or greenhouse keeps plants warm and lets you grow for longer in autumn and winter. Glass is very expensive; plastic is cheaper.

- A tunnel or greenhouse built against the north wall of the house stores the sun's heat and can help keep the house warmer in winter too.
- Some farmers keep a few chickens in a separate, fenced part of the tunnel in winter. Their body heat adds a little warmth and their manure feeds the soil. Keep the space well ventilated, because chicken manure gives off ammonia, which harms both birds and plants.

## Plan for the life you have

Before you start, be sure that what you create is what you really want. Be honest about how much **time**, **money** and **interest** you have for farming.

1. Do a simple **lifestyle audit**. Write down how many hours a week you can give to the garden, what you want from it (food for the family, income, or both) and what money you can spend.
2. If you have little time, keep the system simple. Plant many **perennials** (plants that live for many years, such as fruit trees, herbs and asparagus) and save labour with tools such as drip irrigation and a tap timer.
3. If you want to farm for income and have the time, energy and resources, a larger and more intensive garden may suit you.

Design your gardens to match your answers. A big garden that you cannot look after will soon become a weedy, wasted one.

The four kinds of food garden below follow the permaculture **zones**: the parts of the farm you visit most are placed closest to the house.

## 1. The kitchen garden (Zone 1)

The kitchen garden is part of **Zone 1**, the area right around the house, usually within a few metres of the kitchen door. It is the food garden closest to the kitchen and the one you visit most often, so it must be quick and easy to reach.

Grow the plants you pick every day:

- salad greens you can **cut and come again** (pick a few leaves and let the plant regrow), such as lettuce, spinach and Swiss chard
- beans and peas
- cherry tomatoes
- herbs you use for cooking, such as parsley, coriander, spring onions and thyme

Most plants here are **annuals** (plants that live for one season), with a few perennial herbs.

### Plant densely, manage closely

A kitchen garden is planted very closely, using every bit of space, both on the ground and upwards (for example beans on a fence or trellis). Per square metre it is the most productive of all your gardens, so it also needs the most care. Weeding, sowing, planting, mulching, harvesting, pest control and feeding the soil are all part of the regular routine.

### Make it useful and beautiful

Kitchen gardens come in all shapes and patterns. What matters most is that the garden works well. Because it is right next to the house, make it look good too. The area is small, so you can take extra time to edge the beds with logs, old bricks or stones, and cover the paths with gravel or wood chips.

## 2. The vegetable garden (Zone 2)

The vegetable garden is larger than the kitchen garden. Here you grow vegetables that need more space or take longer to harvest, for example cabbage, beetroot, leeks, onions, peppers, tomatoes and okra.

You visit it less often than the kitchen garden, perhaps once a day or every few days, so it is placed a little further from the house, in **Zone 2**.

### How it is laid out

- Grow many kinds of crops together. Add features that help control pests, such as a small pond (for frogs and other predators) and flowering plants that attract helpful insects.
- Lay out clear growing beds with paths between them. On a slope, make the beds and paths follow the **contour** (a line across the slope at the same height), so that water soaks in instead of running off.
- Because the area is bigger, you do not need to edge the beds. Just reshape them by hand a few times a year.

Work is still high: moving sprinklers or checking drip lines, mulching, adding compost, controlling pests, weeding and regularly sowing seeds or planting out seedlings.

Large plastic tunnels, greenhouses and shade-net tunnels for the household also belong in Zone 2, near the vegetable garden.

A family vegetable garden does not have to be very big. You can make it larger later if you want to sell your surplus and add to your income.

## 3. Small-scale commercial vegetable farming (Zone 3)

A larger vegetable garden grown mainly for sale is often called a **market garden**. From about 0.4 hectares (one acre) upwards it is best placed in **Zone 3**, the main farming area.

### Organise for production

A market garden is organised to produce as much as possible with as little wasted work.

- Plant one kind of vegetable per bed. This makes sowing, harvesting and other jobs faster. Change the crop in each bed from season to season (crop rotation).
- Plant closely in well-prepared, well-composted beds. Close spacing gives more food per square metre and shades out weeds.
- Use simple tools that save time, such as a hand push-seeder and a **broadfork** (a wide fork with two handles that loosens soil without turning it over). A larger farm may use a two-wheel walk-behind tractor for light cultivation and for mowing cover crops.

### Why market gardens can work

Market gardens have become popular around the world, including in Southern Africa, because many buyers now want fresh food grown close to home.

- **Sell direct.** When you sell straight to customers (at the farm gate, a local market, to neighbours, schools, spaza shops or restaurants) you receive the **retail** price instead of the lower **wholesale** price paid by traders.
- **Lower start-up costs.** A small area with hand tools costs far less to start than a large mechanised farm.
- **Offer variety.** Successful market gardeners often sell many products: vegetables, fruit, berries, eggs, honey, meat or milk. Some also add value by making jams, pickles, dried fruit and other products.

> **Tip:** Before you plant for sale, talk to your buyers. Find out what they want, how much, how often and at what price. Grow for a market you know, not one you hope for.

## 4. The staple garden (Zone 3)

**Staple crops** are the foods that make up most of a family's diet, such as maize, sorghum, beans and sweet potato.

- **Grains, potatoes and other root crops** give **carbohydrates**: energy food.
- **Beans and other legumes** give **protein**: body-building food.

Staple crops need a lot of space for a moderate harvest. Many take several months to mature, and are often harvested all at once, as with dry maize or dry beans. So we place the staple garden in the farming area, **Zone 3**.

The staple garden needs less work than the kitchen or vegetable garden and you visit it less often. Some crops are irrigated now and then, but most are grown on rain alone. Choose crops that suit your climate, and plant them at the right time so they make the best use of the rain.

### Staple crops for Southern Africa

| Group | Crops |
|---|---|
| Grains | maize, sorghum, pearl millet; wheat, oats and barley (mainly winter crops) |
| Beans and other legumes | dry beans such as sugar beans, cowpea, jugo bean (Bambara groundnut), groundnut, soya, pigeon pea, mung bean, chickpea |
| Root and tuber crops | potato, sweet potato, amadumbe (taro), cassava (frost-free areas only) |
| Space-hungry vegetables | pumpkin, butternut, watermelon, other melons and gourds |

### Maize

Maize is the main staple in much of South Africa.

- In summer-rainfall areas, plant after good rains have wet the soil and the risk of frost has passed, usually from October to December.
- Plant in rows about 90 cm apart. In dry areas, space plants widely (about one plant every 50 cm in the row) so each plant has enough water. With better rain, irrigation or rich soil, plants can be closer (about 30 cm apart).
- **Open-pollinated** varieties can be grown again from your own saved seed. Hybrid maize gives a high yield from bought seed, but saved seed from a hybrid does not grow true and yields less.

### Sorghum

Sorghum handles heat and drought better than maize and is a good choice in dry areas. Where water is short, plant it in **wide rows** (about 0.9 m to 2.3 m apart, depending on your rain and soil) so each plant has a bigger area of soil to draw water from. In wetter areas narrower rows are fine.

### Dry beans and cowpea

- Plant dry beans when the soil has warmed (above about 15 °C) after the first good rains: in most summer-rainfall areas this is from mid-October to December, and in frosty areas up to about mid-January.
- Beans grow well in rows between or beside maize. Like other legumes, they help to feed the soil with nitrogen.
- Cowpea and jugo bean cope with heat and drought better than dry beans.

### Sweet potato

Sweet potato is grown from **vine cuttings**, not seed.

1. Cut pieces of vine 20–30 cm long from healthy plants.
2. Plant them on ridges or mounds about 1 m apart, with cuttings about 30 cm apart along the ridge.
3. In areas with frost, plant from about mid-November to early December. In frost-free areas you can plant from about August to March.

Orange-fleshed sweet potato is rich in vitamin A and is especially good for children.

### Cassava

Cassava only grows well in warm, **frost-free** areas, such as parts of KwaZulu-Natal, the Mpumalanga and Limpopo lowveld. It is very drought-tolerant once established.

> **Safety:** Cassava roots and leaves contain natural chemicals that release **cyanide**, a poison. "Bitter" varieties contain much more than "sweet" ones, and plants stressed by drought contain more. Never eat cassava raw. Peel the roots and cook them thoroughly. Bitter cassava must also be soaked in water for several days (and the water thrown away), or grated and processed, before cooking. Ask your extension officer which variety you have.

### Principles for the staple garden

There are many ways to grow staple crops. The best one for you depends on your climate, local farming traditions, the tools you have and the crops you choose. These principles help in every case:

1. **Plant polycultures, not monocultures.** Grow several crops together, for example maize with beans and pumpkin.
2. **Disturb the soil as little as possible.** Avoid deep ploughing where you can.
3. **Leave crop residues on the soil as mulch.** Stalks and leaves protect the soil and feed it as they rot.
4. **Grow cover crops** to protect and feed the soil between main crops.
5. **Plant on the contour** so rain soaks in and soil stays in place.
6. **Rotate crops.** Do not grow the same crop in the same place year after year.
7. **Rest the land.** After a few seasons, let a field rest under a cover crop or pasture.

> **Note:** Beans are a daily food in many parts of Africa. In Rwanda and Burundi, people eat on average about 27–31 kg of beans each per year, the highest in the world.

## Key points

- Know your climate: summer rain in the interior, winter rain in the Western Cape, frost-free lowveld and coast.
- Let cold air drain downhill; keep frost-tender crops out of frost pockets.
- Plan gardens to match the time, money and interest you really have.
- Kitchen garden (Zone 1): small, dense, daily greens and herbs, closest to the kitchen.
- Vegetable garden (Zone 2): bigger crops, beds and paths on contour.
- Market garden (Zone 3): one crop per bed, close spacing, sell direct for a better price.
- Staple garden (Zone 3): maize, sorghum, beans, sweet potato and other energy and protein crops, grown mostly on rain.
- Never eat cassava raw; peel, soak and cook it well.
- Mix crops, disturb the soil little, mulch, rotate, plant on contour and rest the land.
