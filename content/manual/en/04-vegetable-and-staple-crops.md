# Vegetable and staple crops

A family eats every day, so the garden must feed it every day. Some food we pick a handful at a time: morogo, parsley, a bowl of beans. Other food we harvest once a season and store: dry mielies, beans, sweet potatoes.

Each kind of food needs its own garden, in its own place. Put each one where it belongs and the whole farm runs with less effort.

**By the end of this chapter you will be able to:**

- Place your gardens to suit your climate and keep crops out of frost
- Match the size of your garden to the time and money you really have
- Lay out a kitchen garden, a vegetable garden, a market garden and a staple garden
- Plant mielies, sorghum, dry beans, sweet potato and cassava at the right time and spacing

## Growing food in Southern African climates

There is no single planting calendar here. Know your climate:

- **Summer-rainfall interior** (Highveld, Free State, much of the Eastern Cape, the KwaZulu-Natal Midlands, Lesotho): rain falls from about October to March; winters are dry and cold, often with frost.
- **Winter-rainfall Western Cape**: rain falls from about May to September. Summer crops need irrigation.
- **Frost-free lowveld and coast** (the KwaZulu-Natal coast, the Limpopo and Mpumalanga lowveld): with water, you can grow most of the year.

In cold areas, frost is the main danger. Cold air is heavy. On a still, clear night it flows downhill like water and collects in low places called **frost pockets**. Work with it:

1. Leave a gap low down in walls and hedges on the uphill side, so cold air keeps flowing past and away.
2. Plant frost-tender crops higher up the slope, never in the valley bottom.
3. Use north-facing slopes for early and late crops. They get the most winter sun.
4. Stretch the season with a plastic tunnel, cheaper than glass. Built against the north wall of the house, it helps warm the house too. Some farmers winter a few chickens in a fenced corner of it for warmth and manure; give them plenty of fresh air, because the manure gives off ammonia.

## Plan for the life you have

A big garden you cannot look after soon becomes a weedy, wasted one. Before you dig, be honest about your **time**, **money** and **interest**.

1. Do a simple **lifestyle audit**. Write down how many hours a week you can give the garden, what you want from it (food, income or both) and what you can spend.
2. If time is short, keep it simple. Plant many **perennials**, plants that live for years, such as fruit trees, herbs and asparagus. Save labour with **drip irrigation**, pipes that drip water slowly onto each plant, and a tap timer.
3. If you want income and have the time and energy, go larger and more intensive.

The four gardens below follow the permaculture **zones**: the parts of the farm you visit most sit closest to the house.

## 1. The kitchen garden (Zone 1)

The kitchen garden sits in **Zone 1**, a few steps from the kitchen door. You pass it many times a day, so pick from it many times a day.

Grow what you use daily:

- morogo and salad leaves you can **cut and come again** (pick a few leaves and let the plant regrow): spinach, Swiss chard, lettuce
- beans and peas
- cherry tomatoes
- cooking herbs: parsley, coriander, spring onions, thyme

Most are **annuals**, plants that live for one season, with a few perennial herbs.

Plant densely. Use every bit of ground, and grow upwards too, with beans on a fence. Per square metre this is your most productive garden, so it needs the most care: sowing, weeding, **mulching** (covering the soil with dry plant material), feeding the soil and picking off pests.

Make it beautiful. It is small and beside the house, so take time to edge the beds with logs, old bricks or stones, and cover the paths with gravel or wood chips.

## 2. The vegetable garden (Zone 2)

The vegetable garden is bigger. It holds crops that need more room or grow longer: cabbage, beetroot, leeks, onions, peppers, tomatoes and okra. You visit it once a day or every few days, so it goes a little further out, in **Zone 2**. Household tunnels and shade-net houses belong here too.

1. Lay out clear beds with paths between them. On a slope, run beds and paths along the **contour**, a line across the slope at one height, so water soaks in instead of running off.
2. Grow many crops together. Add a small pond for frogs and flowers that feed helpful insects (Chapter 10).
3. Skip the edging. Reshape the beds by hand a few times a year.

The work is steady: checking drip lines, mulching, adding **compost** (rotted plant and animal waste), weeding and sowing.

## 3. Small-scale commercial vegetable farming (Zone 3)

A vegetable garden grown mainly for sale is a **market garden**. From about 0.4 hectares (one acre) up, it belongs in **Zone 3**, the main farming area.

1. Plant one crop per bed. Sowing, weeding and harvesting go faster. Change the crop in each bed every season (Chapter 10).
2. Plant closely in deep, well-composted beds. Close spacing gives more food per square metre and shades out weeds.
3. Use tools that save time: a push-seeder and a **broadfork**, a wide two-handled fork that loosens soil without turning it. A bigger farm may add a two-wheel walk-behind tractor.

Market gardens work because buyers want fresh food grown nearby. Sell straight to customers, at the farm gate, a market, schools, spaza shops or restaurants, and you earn the **retail** price, not the lower **wholesale** price a trader pays. Start-up costs are low, and a spread of products (vegetables, fruit, eggs, honey, jams) keeps money coming in.

> **Tip:** Talk to your buyers before you plant. Find out what they want, how much, how often and at what price. Grow for a market you know, not one you hope for.

## 4. The staple garden (Zone 3)

**Staple crops** are the foods that fill the family's plate most days. Grains, potatoes and other roots give energy. Beans and other **legumes**, the bean family, give protein.

Staples need a lot of space for a moderate harvest, take months to ripen and come in all at once, so they go in Zone 3. It needs less work and grows mostly on rain, so choose crops that suit your climate and plant them to catch the rain.

| Group | Crops |
|---|---|
| Grains | mielies, sorghum, pearl millet; wheat, oats and barley (mainly winter crops) |
| Beans and other legumes | sugar beans and other dry beans, cowpeas, jugo beans, groundnuts, soya, pigeon peas, mung beans, chickpeas |
| Roots and tubers | potatoes, sweet potatoes, amadumbe, cassava (frost-free areas only) |
| Space-hungry vegetables | pumpkins, butternut, watermelons, melons and gourds |

### Mielies

1. In summer-rainfall areas, plant after good rains have wet the soil and the frost is over, usually October to December.
2. Plant in rows about 90 cm apart. In dry areas leave about 50 cm between plants in the row, so each has enough water. With good rain, irrigation or rich soil, plant them about 30 cm apart.
3. Choose **open-pollinated** seed if you want to save your own. Hybrid seed yields well the first time, but seed saved from a hybrid crop yields about a third less.

### Sorghum

Sorghum takes heat and drought better than mielies. Where water is short, plant it in wide rows, about 0.9 m to 2.3 m apart depending on rain and soil, so each plant has more soil to drink from.

### Dry beans, cowpeas and jugo beans

1. Plant dry beans after the first good rains, once the soil is warmer than about 15 °C: mid-October to December in most summer-rainfall areas, and up to about mid-January where there is frost.
2. Grow them beside or between the mielies. They feed the soil with nitrogen.
3. In hot, dry places, grow cowpeas and jugo beans, which handle drought well.

### Sweet potato

1. Cut pieces of vine 20 to 30 cm long from healthy plants.
2. Plant them on ridges about 1 m apart, 30 cm apart along the ridge.
3. Where there is frost, plant from mid-November to early December. In frost-free areas, plant from August to March.

Grow the orange-fleshed kind. It is rich in vitamin A and good for children.

### Cassava

Cassava grows only in warm, frost-free areas, such as the KwaZulu-Natal coast and the lowveld, and stands drought well once established. Its roots release cyanide, a poison, so we never eat cassava raw: peel the roots and cook them well, and soak bitter kinds in water for several days first, throwing the water away.

### Principles for the staple garden

1. **Mix crops.** Grow mielies with beans and pumpkins, not one crop alone.
2. **Disturb the soil as little as possible.**
3. **Leave stalks and leaves on the soil as mulch.** It protects the soil and feeds it as it rots.
4. **Grow cover crops** between main crops.
5. **Plant on the contour** so rain soaks in and the soil stays put.
6. **Rotate crops** so no crop returns to the same place year after year.
7. **Rest the land** under a cover crop or pasture after a few seasons.

## Try it

Map your four gardens this week.

1. Draw your house and the land around it on a sheet of paper.
2. Stand at the kitchen door and pace out 10 steps. Mark the best spot for a kitchen garden inside that distance.
3. Mark a spot for a vegetable garden, within an easy walk and close to water.
4. Mark the space for staples, and note which way the land slopes.
5. On a still, cold evening, feel where the cold air settles. Mark that frost pocket.
6. Beside each garden, write the hours a week you can give it.

## Key points

- Know your climate, and let cold air drain away from tender crops.
- Size your gardens to the time, money and interest you really have.
- Kitchen garden close and dense; vegetable garden on contour; market garden in single-crop beds, sold direct.
- Plant staples to catch the rain, and never eat cassava raw.
- Mix crops, disturb the soil little, mulch, rotate and rest the land.

Start with the garden by the kitchen door. When it feeds you every day, you will know when you are ready for the next.
