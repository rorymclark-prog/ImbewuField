# What is permaculture?

## What the word means

The word **permaculture** joins two words: **perma**nent and agri**culture**. It can mean farming (food production) that can carry on for generations. It can also mean a **culture** — a way of living and doing things — that lasts.

The word was made up in the mid-1970s by two Australians, **Bill Mollison** (1928–2016) and **David Holmgren**, who was then his student in Tasmania. In 1978 they published the first book about it, *Permaculture One*. Since then permaculture has spread to farms, gardens and communities all over the world, including Southern Africa.

## Definitions

Bill Mollison described permaculture as "the conscious design and maintenance of agriculturally productive ecosystems, which have the diversity, stability and resilience of natural ecosystems".

In plain words: permaculture is a way of planning and running a farm or garden so that it works like nature. A natural forest or grassland has many kinds of plants and animals (**diversity**), it keeps going year after year (**stability**), and it recovers after drought, fire or damage (**resilience**). A permaculture farm aims to do the same, while also feeding people.

Permaculture links land, water, plants, animals and people so that each part helps the others. Like a natural system, it tries to waste nothing: what one part gives off, another part uses.

## Why permaculture matters in Southern Africa

Many families in Southern Africa depend on the land for their food and income. They are also the people most at risk from two big problems.

- **Climate change.** Farmers are already seeing hotter days, rain that is harder to predict, longer droughts and more heavy storms and floods. Most smallholders depend on rain rather than irrigation, so these changes hit them hardest.
- **Land degradation.** When soil is left bare, overgrazed or ploughed on steep slopes, rain and wind wash and blow it away. Research shows that soil erosion has affected more than 70% of South Africa's land. Lesotho, where this manual was first written, also has severe soil erosion and gullies.

South Africa is also a dry country. It receives on average about 450 mm of rain a year, roughly half the world average of about 860 mm. Every drop matters.

When land loses its soil and its water, it can no longer feed the people who live on it, and families are forced to leave. The warning signs are already here. We cannot carry on farming in the same old way.

Permaculture gives us a map and a set of tools to change this. It teaches us to protect the soil, catch and keep the rain, grow a wide range of crops and work with nature instead of against it. It helps us become part of the solution.

## The benefits of permaculture

Permaculture is more than a set of farming methods. By working closely with the land we also reconnect with it, and this can change our lives for the better.

A permaculture home and farm can give food through the whole year, tanks and ponds full of rainwater, and gardens full of life. It helps a family move from just surviving to living well, and to share that with others.

- **Better health and nutrition.** You can grow a wide variety of fresh vegetables, fruit, legumes and grains without harmful chemical pesticides and weedkillers. A varied diet is a healthier diet.
- **New ideas and motivation.** Permaculture helps you see the links between the different parts of your farm, and how to use them to your advantage.
- **Meaningful work.** Many people find real purpose in caring for the land.
- **Income and savings.** Growing many different products makes household income less risky: if one crop fails, others still yield. You spend less on bought inputs, and you can sell your surplus or add value to it (for example by drying, bottling or processing it).

## The three ethics of permaculture

Permaculture rests on three **ethics** — values that guide every decision. Bill Mollison and David Holmgren put these at the heart of permaculture.

### 1. Care for the Earth

Care for the Earth means looking after all living things, such as plants, animals and the life in the soil. It also means looking after non-living things, such as land, water and air. We depend on all of these to stay alive.

### 2. Care for people

Care for people means meeting people's needs — food, water, shelter, health, learning and friendship — so that they can live a good life, without damaging the Earth. It is about building self-reliance and taking responsibility for our families and our wider community.

### 3. Share fairly

The Earth has limited resources. Sharing fairly (often called **fair share**) means limiting what we consume, and sharing our surplus — food, seed, time, skills and money — so that it goes back into caring for the Earth and for people.

When we care for the Earth and its people and share fairly, we also start to deal with bigger problems such as poverty, hunger and soil erosion. All forms of life have a right to exist, so as permaculture designers we work to protect and support life in all its forms.

> **Note:** "Nature does nothing without purpose." — Aristotle, Greek philosopher. In permaculture we look for the purpose and use of every part of a system.

## The design principles of permaculture

The design principles are a set of guidelines that help us plan and run a permaculture farm. We use them to design the farm, to understand the links between its parts, and to find lasting solutions to problems. The principles connect to each other and work together.

Different permaculture teachers use slightly different lists of principles. The twelve principles below come from David Holmgren, co-founder of permaculture, in his book *Permaculture: Principles and Pathways Beyond Sustainability* (2002). Holmgren linked many of them to a well-known saying, and some of these are given below. The principles also apply beyond farming, for example to health, building and running a household or business.

### 1. Observe and interact

Spend time watching nature, especially the land around your home. Before we act, we need to understand what is already happening: where the water flows, where the frost lies, which plants grow well, which animals visit. Good design comes from careful, patient observation.

Books, courses and teachers can help, but nothing replaces your own direct experience of your land. The more closely you know your land, the better your decisions will be.

Ways to practise observation:

- Walk your land, and the veld or bush nearby, in different seasons.
- Sit quietly and watch the plants, birds, insects and animals on your land or in your neighbourhood.
- Keep a simple notebook of the weather, rainfall, first and last frosts, and the seasons. Many farmers also record the phases of the moon.
- Walk your land during and after heavy rain to see where water runs and collects.

### 2. Catch and store energy

Saying: *Make hay while the sun shines.*

Nature moves in cycles: times of plenty are followed by times of shortage. You can see this in the seasons. Across most of South Africa's interior there is a growing season in the warm, wet summer and a cold, dry winter with little growth. In the Western Cape, the rain comes in winter and the summer is dry.

In permaculture we catch and store energy and resources while they are plentiful, so we have them later when they are scarce. The same is true of our own energy: use your busy, energetic times well, and plan for the times when you have less energy.

Examples:

- In the growing season, when the sun is strongest, we catch the sun's energy as crops — vegetables, fruit, nuts, firewood, fodder and meat.
- When it rains, catch and store the water in tanks, ponds and in the soil itself (for example with mulch and swales).
- Dry, bottle or preserve extra vegetables and fruit for the season when fresh food is scarce.

### 3. Obtain a yield

Saying: *You can't work on an empty stomach.*

Make your time and work count. A **yield** is anything useful that your system produces: food, fodder, firewood, shelter, warmth, medicine, seed or income.

Some yields take years. A fruit tree may take several years before it bears. So plan a mix of quick and slow yields, so that you always have something to show for the work and resources you put in now. We deliberately design for surplus — enough for our family, for the plants and animals, to share with neighbours and to sell.

Examples:

- Sprouts of mung beans, lentils or other seeds are ready to eat in about 2 to 5 days, and they are nutritious.
- Fruit trees are a long-term investment, but they give a harvest for many years once they start to bear.
- Plant flowering plants that give nectar and pollen to bees and other useful insects. Many indigenous aloes, for example, flower in winter when little else does. Bees then pollinate your crops and can give honey.

> **Safety:** Sprouts grow in warm, damp conditions that also suit harmful germs. Use clean water, rinse the sprouts at least twice a day, and throw away any that smell bad or look slimy. Cook sprouts before giving them to young children, pregnant women, older people or anyone who is ill.

### 4. Apply self-regulation and accept feedback

Natural systems keep themselves in balance. In a healthy forest or grassland, no single plant or animal takes over for long. People, however, often take far more than they need. Our overuse of resources is a major cause of climate change and the loss of plants and animals.

This principle asks us to **set limits** — to live simply and not take more than we need — so that our children inherit healthy land.

> **Note:** "Earth provides enough to satisfy every man's needs, but not every man's greed." — a saying widely credited to Mahatma Gandhi.

It also asks us to **accept feedback**. Mistakes are part of learning. Feedback is information that tells us whether what we are doing is working — from our crops, our soil, our animals, and from other people. We need to listen to it with an open mind and change what is not working, even when that is hard.

Examples:

- Avoid unnecessary trips by car or taxi. Combine errands into one trip, walk, or use a bicycle. This saves money and fuel.
- Think twice before buying new things, such as a new phone, when the old one still works. Making them uses many natural resources.
- When people are speaking to you, or when you are observing in the fields, clear your mind and listen calmly. You will notice more and learn more.
- Watch your plants: yellow leaves, pests or poor growth are feedback that something in the soil, water or plant choice needs to change.

### 5. Use and value renewable resources and services

Look to nature for the things we need. Plants, animals, sunlight, wind and rain renew themselves. Non-renewable resources such as petrol, diesel and chemical fertiliser are used once and then gone, they cost money, and burning fossil fuels adds to climate change.

Plants and animals can give us fuel and fertiliser, and help with insect control, weed control, fire control, erosion control, recycling nutrients and loosening the soil. They can take the place of tractors, brush cutters and lawnmowers that burn fuel.

It takes time to build up these living resources, so see this as a long-term investment. In time, they will reproduce and keep themselves going.

Examples:

- **Animal tractors.** Chickens and pigs naturally scratch and dig for worms, roots and insects. Keep them for a few days in a fenced, overgrown area, and they will clear the weeds and fertilise the ground, ready for planting.
- **Fertiliser from animals.** Animals eat plants and return nutrients to the soil in their manure. Manure and liquid manure (manure tea) feed garden plants.
- **Living fences and windbreaks.** Hedges of useful, non-invasive plants give shelter, fodder and firewood without fuel or wire.

> **Safety:** Fresh animal manure can carry germs that cause serious illness. Compost it well before you use it on crops that are eaten raw, such as lettuce, spinach and herbs. If you do use raw manure, dig it into the soil at least 4 months (120 days) before harvesting crops that touch the soil. Wash your hands after handling manure. Keep pigs in a strong fence so they cannot escape into neighbours' crops.

### 6. Produce no waste

Saying: *Waste not, want not.*

In permaculture we aim for **closed loops**, where the output of one part of the system is the input of another. Energy and nutrients cycle round the farm and are turned into a yield or a service. We take responsibility for our so-called waste and turn it into a resource. Anything that finally leaves the farm should be clean and harmless.

A good rule is: **rethink, reduce, repair, reuse, recycle.** Think about how you can use kitchen scraps, crop leftovers and animal manure in the garden. This saves time and money.

Examples:

- **Kitchen scraps.** Peels, skins and vegetable leftovers are good food for worms and a valuable "green" (nitrogen-rich) ingredient in the compost heap.
- **Grass cuttings.** Use them as mulch around seedlings to keep the soil cool and moist, stop weeds and slowly add nutrients as they rot. Spread them in thin layers, or let them dry first, because a thick layer of fresh grass mats together and heats up. Do not use cuttings from lawns sprayed with weedkiller.
- **Greywater.** Instead of throwing away washing and bath water, use it to water fruit trees and ornamental plants. Use mild, biodegradable soaps and washing powders that are low in salt.

> **Safety:** Greywater can carry germs. Use water from the bath, shower and hand basin rather than kitchen water, which is greasy and full of germs. Never use water from washing nappies. Pour greywater onto the soil or mulch around trees, not onto leaves, and never onto vegetables that are eaten raw, such as lettuce and spinach. Do not let it stand in puddles, and do not use it if someone in the household has a stomach illness. Water with clean water from time to time to wash out salts.

### 7. Design from patterns to details

Nature is full of patterns — the branching of a river or a tree, the spiral of a snail shell, the way water always flows downhill. In permaculture we try to find the best pattern for our site first, and only then fill in the details.

Start with the big picture: the shape of the land (its slopes and contours), where water comes from and where it goes (the **catchment**), and the climate. Draw the main pattern of your design from these. Then work out the details, such as which crops go in which bed. Bill Mollison thought pattern so important that he gave it a whole chapter of his book *Permaculture: A Designers' Manual* (1988).

You can also borrow nature's patterns directly: paths that curve gently like a river, or garden beds shaped like a spiral or a keyhole. Look at the shapes around you for ideas.

Examples:

- Use the **contours** of your land (lines at the same height) to build earthworks, such as a **swale** — a ditch dug along the contour to catch and soak up runoff water. The contour is the pattern; the swale is the detail.
- Follow the sun's path through the year. In Southern Africa the sun is in the north, and it is lower in winter. Place crops, trees and buildings where they get the sun they need.
- Watch the cycles around you, such as the seasons and the rains. Note how they affect plants and animals, and plan your planting and animal care around them.

### 8. Integrate rather than segregate

Saying: *Many hands make light work.*

When the parts of your farm are linked so that each helps the others, the whole system becomes stronger, more productive and better able to survive hard times. Together, the parts achieve more than they could on their own.

Placing each part — the house, the garden, the chickens, the water tank, the trees — in the right place, where it can support the others, is the heart of permaculture design.

The same is true of people. Communities are stronger when everyone takes part. Children and older people have much to give and to learn, and should be included in the life of the farm, not kept apart from it.

Examples:

- Create safe spaces where children and older people can help and learn in the garden.
- Combine trees, crops and animals (**agroforestry**) so that each gives its products and services to the others. For example, trees give shade and fodder, animals give manure, and crops give food and crop leftovers for the animals.
- Create **guilds** — groups of plants that grow well together and help each other with pest control, fertility, shade or support.

### 9. Use small and slow solutions

Saying: *Slow and steady wins the race.*

It is tempting to start big. But it is much wiser to start with what you can manage. A small area that is well cared for gives more than a large area that gets out of control. Start at your back door, do one thing at a time and build on each step. Only begin the next step once the first is working.

Many farmers start with great enthusiasm and then lose heart because the area they chose is too big to manage. Natural systems change slowly, over time, and become richer and more productive as they do.

Taking your time lets you observe the land, the seasons and how the land responds to your work. The changes you make this way will last. Permaculture needs patience. Still, have a plan to work towards, even though it will change over time.

Examples:

- Start with a small kitchen garden near the house, especially if you have not grown vegetables before. Bring more of your land into production each year.
- Use tools and machines that suit the size of your land. A big tractor on a small field is costly, compacts the soil and is hard to turn. Chickens in a movable pen (a **chicken tractor**) work slowly, but they clear and fertilise the ground cheaply.
- Build soil slowly, season by season, with compost, mulch and cover crops.

### 10. Use and value diversity

Saying: *Don't put all your eggs in one basket.*

Diversity is strength. Nature is very diverse, and this is why it can keep working even after great damage. Think of the parts of a diverse system as the threads of a rope: one thread alone snaps easily, but many threads twisted together are very strong.

We apply this principle by having many kinds of crops, animals, and ways of harvesting water and energy on our farm. If one crop fails, another will still give a harvest, so the family is less at risk.

Diversity works best when the parts are connected. Simply adding many different things is not enough; each one should have a place and a use, and should support the others.

Large farms that grow a single crop over a big area (a **monoculture**), often from genetically very similar seed, are poor in wildlife and at high risk from pests, disease and bad weather. A well-designed permaculture farm is far richer in life and better able to cope with shocks.

Examples:

- Plant a mix of crops — vegetables, grains, legumes (beans, peas and groundnuts), fruit and nut trees — that give food at different times of the year. This builds food security and helps you cope with drought and a changing climate.
- Save seed from your best, healthiest plants each season. Over the years your seed becomes adapted to your local conditions, and saving from many plants keeps it genetically diverse.
- Keep animals that help each other. For example, chickens that follow cattle scratch through the dung, spread it and eat fly larvae.

### 11. Use edges and value the marginal

An **edge** is the place where two different natural areas meet — for example, where a pond meets dry land, or where forest meets grassland. Edges are often busier, more productive and home to more kinds of plants and animals than either area on its own. They get the benefits of both sides and are visited by creatures from both.

> **Note:** "The interface between things is where the most interesting events take place. These are often the most valuable, diverse and productive elements in the system." — David Holmgren

The **marginal** places — corners, fences, walls, steep banks and wet patches that are usually ignored — can also become productive. We apply this principle by adding useful plants and habitats along edges and margins.

Examples:

- Plant hedgerows of indigenous plants along the edges of crop fields. For example, Kei apple (*Dovyalis caffra*) makes a thorny, fruiting hedge, and Cape honeysuckle (*Tecoma capensis*) and plumbago (*Plumbago auriculata*) attract birds and useful insects.
- Make paths that run between two growing areas, such as a food forest and a vegetable garden, so you can tend both at the same time.
- Place animal pens next to crop areas so that the two can share their outputs. For example, weeds and damaged plants from the garden can be fed to the chickens, and their manure goes back to the garden.
- Grow food on walls and fences (**vertical gardening**). Climbing beans, cucumbers and gourds turn an unused edge into a productive space.

### 12. Creatively use and respond to change

Saying: *Vision is not seeing things as they are but as they will be.*

Everything is always changing and growing. Nature never stands still. If we ignore change, we fail. Instead, we should expect change, look for the opportunities it brings, and act at the right time.

Our permaculture farm will keep changing too, and we must adapt our plans as it does. A caterpillar needs different food from the butterfly it becomes. In the same way, the needs of our farm change at each stage, and we must provide the right resources and work at each stage.

Examples:

- A food forest changes over many years, from small young trees and fast-growing plants to a mature, shady forest. What you plant and how you care for it must change with it.
- Farm animals need different care at different stages of life, from young to adult to old.
- The seasons, and changes in rainfall and temperature from year to year, affect which crops you can grow and how much they produce. Plan your crop choices around them.

> **Tip:** A common saying reminds us: "If you fail to plan, you are planning to fail." The next chapter shows you how to plan your farm.

## Key points

- Permaculture means "permanent agriculture" and "permanent culture": farming and living in ways that last.
- The word was coined in the mid-1970s by Bill Mollison and David Holmgren; their book *Permaculture One* came out in 1978.
- Permaculture designs farms that work like natural ecosystems: diverse, stable and resilient.
- It helps Southern African farmers cope with drought, climate change and soil erosion, while improving food, health and income.
- The three ethics are: care for the Earth, care for people, and share fairly.
- David Holmgren's twelve principles are: observe and interact; catch and store energy; obtain a yield; apply self-regulation and accept feedback; use and value renewable resources and services; produce no waste; design from patterns to details; integrate rather than segregate; use small and slow solutions; use and value diversity; use edges and value the marginal; creatively use and respond to change.
- Start small, observe carefully, and let the land teach you.
