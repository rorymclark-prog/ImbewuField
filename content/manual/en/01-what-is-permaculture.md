# What is permaculture?

Most of us learnt to farm by fighting: the weeds, the pests, the weather. We plough, we spray, we buy fertiliser, and each year the soil gives a little less.

Permaculture starts from a different place. It asks what the land is already doing, and how we can work with it. It is a way of thinking, built on three ethics and twelve principles, that you can carry to any piece of land, from a township backyard to a mountain field.

**By the end of this chapter you will be able to:**

- Explain in plain words what permaculture is and where the word comes from
- Name the three ethics and use them to test a decision on your farm
- Name David Holmgren's twelve design principles and give a farm example of each
- Start observing your own land the way a designer does

## What the word means

**Permaculture** joins two words: **perma**nent and agri**culture**. It means farming that can feed a family for generations. It also means a **culture**, a way of living, that lasts.

Two Australians made up the word in the mid-1970s: **Bill Mollison** (1928–2016) and **David Holmgren**, then his student in Tasmania. Their first book, *Permaculture One*, came out in 1978. Permaculture has since spread worldwide, Southern Africa included.

Mollison described permaculture as "the conscious design and maintenance of agriculturally productive ecosystems, which have the diversity, stability and resilience of natural ecosystems".

In plain words: we plan and run the farm so that it works like nature. An **ecosystem** is a community of plants, animals and soil life living together with the soil, water and air around them. A healthy veld has many kinds of life (**diversity**). It keeps going year after year (**stability**). It recovers after drought, fire or damage (**resilience**). A permaculture farm aims for the same three things, and feeds people too.

## Why permaculture matters in Southern Africa

Families who live from the land feel the weather first. Two pressures are growing.

- **Climate change** is the long-term shift in weather caused mainly by people. Farmers already see hotter days, rain that comes late or all at once, longer droughts and heavier storms. Most smallholders farm with rain, not irrigation, so they carry the most risk.
- **Erosion** is soil washed or blown away. Bare, overgrazed or steep ploughed land loses its topsoil with every storm. Soil erosion has affected more than 70% of South Africa's land. Lesotho, where this manual began, also has severe erosion and deep gullies.

South Africa is a dry country too. It gets about 450 mm of rain a year on average, roughly half the world average of about 860 mm. Every drop counts.

Land that loses its soil and water stops feeding its people, and they leave. We cannot farm the old way and expect a different result.

Permaculture gives us a map and tools: protect the soil, catch the rain, grow many crops, work with nature. It pays back in daily life.

- **Food through the year.** Vegetables, fruit, grains and **legumes** (the bean and pea family), grown without poisons. A varied plate is a healthier plate.
- **Money kept and money earned.** Fewer bought inputs, and many products, so one failed crop does not sink the household. Surplus can be sold, dried or bottled.

## The three ethics

Every choice on a permaculture farm is tested against three **ethics**, the values that guide us. Bill Mollison and David Holmgren put them at the heart of permaculture.

### 1. Care for the Earth

Look after all living things (plants, animals, the life in the soil) and the land, water and air that hold life up. We depend on all of them.

### 2. Care for people

Meet people's needs for food, water, shelter, health, learning and friendship without damaging the Earth. Build self-reliance in your family and community.

### 3. Share fairly

The Earth has limits. Sharing fairly, often called **fair share**, means taking what we need and putting the surplus (food, seed, time, skills and money) back into caring for the Earth and for people.

Use the ethics as a test. Before you plough a slope, buy a spray or fence off a spring, ask three questions. Is it good for the land? Is it good for people? Is it fair? If any answer is no, look for another way.

## The design principles of permaculture

The ethics tell us what matters. The principles are thinking tools for designing it: to plan a farm, see how its parts connect and solve problems for good.

Teachers use slightly different lists. The twelve here come from David Holmgren's book *Permaculture: Principles and Pathways Beyond Sustainability* (2002). Holmgren tied each principle to an old saying, and we give some of those sayings here.

### 1. Observe and interact

You cannot design for land you do not know. Where does the water run? Where does the frost lie? What grows well without help? Books and teachers help, but nothing replaces your own time on your own land.

1. Walk your land, and the veld around it, in every season.
2. Sit quietly and watch the plants, birds, insects and animals.
3. Keep a notebook of the rain, the first and last frosts, and when things flower. Many farmers note the phases of the moon as well.
4. Walk out during and after heavy rain. See where the water runs and where it collects.

After a year of watching, you will know your land better than any visitor. Your design will fit it instead of fighting it.

### 2. Catch and store energy

*Make hay while the sun shines.*

Nature moves in cycles: plenty, then shortage. Across most of South Africa's interior, the warm, wet summer is the growing season and the winter is cold, dry and slow. In the Western Cape the rain comes in winter and the summer is dry. We catch what is plentiful and store it for the lean time.

- Catch the summer sun as crops: vegetables, fruit, nuts, fodder, firewood and meat.
- Catch the rain in tanks, ponds and the soil itself, with **mulch** (a cover of dry grass or leaves on the soil) and **swales** (level ditches dug along a slope to soak up rain).
- Dry, bottle and preserve summer's surplus for the winter.

A farm that stores well does not panic when the rain stops.

### 3. Obtain a yield

*You can't work on an empty stomach.*

A **yield** is anything useful the land gives you: food, fodder, firewood, shade, medicine, seed or income. A fruit tree may need several seasons before it bears, so plant quick and slow yields together, and there is always something to show for this season's work. We design for surplus: enough for the family, the soil, the animals, the neighbours and the market.

- Sprouts of mung beans or lentils are ready to eat in about 2 to 5 days. Use clean water, rinse them twice a day, and throw away any that smell bad or feel slimy.
- Fruit trees are slow to start, but they give a harvest for many years once they bear.
- Plant flowers for bees. Many indigenous aloes flower in winter, when little else does. The bees then pollinate your crops and can give you honey.

### 4. Apply self-regulation and accept feedback

Natural systems keep themselves in balance. In a healthy veld no single plant or animal takes over for long. People are different: we often take far more than we need, and that overuse drives climate change and the loss of wild plants and animals.

So we **set limits**: we live simply and take what we need, so that our children inherit living land. And we **accept feedback**, the signals that tell us whether what we do is working. Mistakes are part of learning. Listen to the crops, the soil, the animals and other people, and change what is not working.

- Combine errands into one trip, walk, or ride a bicycle.
- Read your plants. Yellow leaves, weak growth or a pest outbreak are the land telling you that something in the soil, the water or your plant choice needs to change.

### 5. Use and value renewable resources and services

Sunlight, wind, rain, plants and animals renew themselves. Petrol, diesel and chemical fertiliser are used once and gone. They cost money, and burning fossil fuels adds to climate change.

Plants and animals can do much of the work of a tractor or brush cutter. They give fuel and fertiliser, control weeds and insects, stop erosion, recycle nutrients and loosen the soil. They take time to build up, but in time they breed and keep themselves going.

- **Animal tractors.** An **animal tractor** is a movable pen of chickens or pigs, which scratch and dig for worms, roots and insects. Pen them for a few days on an overgrown patch and they clear and manure it, ready for planting.
- **Manure.** Animals eat plants and give the nutrients back as manure and manure tea. Compost manure before it goes near crops you eat raw.
- **Living fences.** A hedge of useful, non-invasive plants gives shelter, fodder and firewood without wire or fuel.

### 6. Produce no waste

*Waste not, want not.*

In nature there is no rubbish: the output of one part is the input of the next. On the farm we aim for **closed loops**, where nutrients and energy cycle round and come out as a yield. What finally leaves the farm should be clean. A good rule: **rethink, reduce, repair, reuse, recycle.**

- **Kitchen scraps** feed worms, and they are a valuable "green" (nitrogen-rich) ingredient in the **compost** heap. Compost is plant and animal waste rotted down into dark, crumbly food for the soil.
- **Grass cuttings** make mulch around seedlings. They keep the soil cool and moist and hold back weeds. Spread them thin or let them dry first, because a thick layer of fresh grass mats together and heats up.
- **Greywater** is used water from baths, showers and basins. It goes under mulch to fruit trees, never onto morogo you eat raw, and kitchen or nappy water never goes in it. Use mild, low-salt soaps.

### 7. Design from patterns to details

Nature is full of patterns: the branching of a river or a tree, the spiral of a shell, water running downhill. We find the big pattern of the site first, then fill in the details. Mollison thought pattern so important that he gave it a whole chapter of his book *Permaculture: A Designers' Manual* (1988).

1. Start with the shape of the land: its slopes and **contours**, the lines across a slope that stay at one height.
2. Find the **catchment**, the area your water comes from, and follow where it goes.
3. Note the climate and the path of the sun. In Southern Africa the sun is in the north, and lower in winter.
4. Only then place the details: which bed, which tree, which crop.

The contour is the pattern; the swale dug along it is the detail. Borrow nature's shapes too: paths that curve like a stream, beds shaped like a spiral or a keyhole.

### 8. Integrate rather than segregate

*Many hands make light work.*

When each part of the farm helps the others, the whole becomes stronger than its parts. Putting each thing (house, garden, chickens, tank, trees) where it can serve the others is the heart of permaculture design. People too: children and older people have much to give and to learn, and belong in the life of the farm.

- Mix trees, crops and animals (**agroforestry**). Trees give shade and fodder, animals give manure, and crops give food and leftovers for the animals.
- Build **guilds**: groups of plants set around a main plant, usually a fruit tree, that help it with pest control, fertility or shade.
- Make safe spaces where children and grandparents can work and learn in the garden.

### 9. Use small and slow solutions

*Slow and steady wins the race.*

Many farmers begin big, with fire in the belly, and lose heart when the patch is too big to manage. A small area well cared for gives more than a big one run wild.

1. Start at your back door with a small kitchen garden.
2. Get it working before you begin the next step.
3. Bring a little more land into production each year.
4. Build the soil season by season with compost, mulch and cover crops.

A big tractor on a small field is costly and packs the soil hard; chickens in a movable pen work slowly but clear and fertilise the ground for very little. Slow change gives you time to watch how the land responds, and changes made that way last. Have a plan, and expect it to change.

### 10. Use and value diversity

*Don't put all your eggs in one basket.*

Diversity is strength. Think of a rope: one thread snaps, but many threads twisted together hold. A **monoculture**, one crop over a big area and often from near-identical seed, is poor in life and open to every pest, disease and bad season. A diverse farm bends and recovers.

Diversity works only when the parts connect. Each crop and animal needs a place and a use.

- Grow vegetables, grains, legumes, and fruit and nut trees that give food at different times of the year. If one crop fails, another still feeds you.
- Save seed from many of your healthiest plants each season. The seed adapts to your land, and saving from many plants keeps it diverse.
- Keep animals that help each other. Chickens that follow cattle scratch the dung apart and eat the fly larvae in it.

### 11. Use edges and value the marginal

An **edge** is where two different areas meet: a pond and dry land, forest and grassland. Edges are often the busiest and most productive places, visited by creatures from both sides. As Holmgren put it, "The interface between things is where the most interesting events take place."

The margins (corners, fences, walls, steep banks and wet patches) are usually ignored. Put them to work.

- Plant hedgerows of indigenous plants along crop fields. Kei apple (*Dovyalis caffra*) makes a thorny, fruiting hedge. Cape honeysuckle (*Tecoma capensis*) and plumbago (*Plumbago auriculata*) bring birds and helpful insects.
- Run a path between two growing areas, such as a **food forest** (a garden planted in layers like a natural forest) and the vegetable beds, so you tend both at once.
- Put the chicken run next to the garden. Weeds go in; manure comes out.
- Grow up walls and fences. Climbing beans, cucumbers and gourds turn a bare edge into food.

### 12. Creatively use and respond to change

*Vision is not seeing things as they are but as they will be.*

Nothing in nature stands still. We cannot stop change, but we can expect it and act at the right time. A caterpillar needs different food from the butterfly it becomes; a farm, too, needs different care at each stage.

- A food forest grows from young trees and fast-growing plants into a shady, mature forest. What you plant and how you tend it must change with it.
- Rain and temperature shift from year to year. Let your crop choices shift with them.

## Try it

1. Choose one spot where you can see most of your land. Sit there for 20 minutes at dawn or dusk, three times this week.
2. Write down everything you notice: birds, insects, where the sun first lands, where the wind comes from, which plants look strong and which struggle.
3. The next time it rains hard, walk your land. Follow the water. Mark where it comes in, where it runs fast and where it collects.
4. Draw a rough map of your land as a bird would see it: house, paths, water flows, strong and weak patches.
5. Next to the map, write one thing each ethic asks of this land, and one principle you could start with.

## Key points

- Permaculture, from permanent agriculture and permanent culture, was named by Bill Mollison and David Holmgren in the mid-1970s.
- It designs farms that work like natural ecosystems: diverse, stable and resilient.
- Three ethics test every decision: care for the Earth, care for people, share fairly.
- Holmgren's twelve principles turn those ethics into a design, and the first of them is to observe.

Keep that notebook close. The land has been talking all along; next, we learn to turn what it says into a plan.
