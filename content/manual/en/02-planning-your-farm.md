# Planning your farm or homestead

You can grow food almost anywhere. But a garden in the wrong place costs you every day: extra steps to the tap, extra buckets up the hill, soil washing away with every storm. A good design saves time, money and energy, and the farm gets more fertile every year.

Permaculture is a way of designing. It brings out the full potential of the land and meets the needs of plants, animals, water, buildings and people together. The richest smallholders are rarely those with the most land.

**By the end of this chapter you will be able to:**

- Draw a base map of your land and mark what is already there
- Read your site for water, slope, aspect and access
- Divide your land into zones, so the things you visit daily are closest to home
- Use the slope so that gravity moves water, compost and manure for you

Sector planning, the fourth step, follows in the next chapter.

## Step 1: Site assessment

A mistake on paper costs nothing to fix. A mistake on the ground costs sweat. So learn the land before you change it.

### Collect and observe

Find out how the land was used before; its rainfall, frost, heat and wind; what is on or near it (water, manure, mulch, building materials, helpers); and where you could sell what you grow.

Then walk the land often, with an open mind, and do not decide too early. Look for:

- where rainwater flows after a storm, and where it collects
- how trees lean or are shaped by the wind
- which animals move across the land
- which places feel warm, cold, wet or dry.

Every season and every storm teaches you something new.

### Draw a base map

A **base map** is a drawing of your property seen from above. Show:

- boundaries, fences, roads, paths and gates
- the house, sheds, kraals and other buildings
- how each part is used now: field, veld or garden
- water: springs, streams, boreholes, tanks, taps and wet spots
- big trees and rocky areas
- **contour** lines if you have them. A contour line joins points at the same height.

Trace a free satellite image (for example from Google Earth), or for a small plot measure with a tape and draw your own. Government 1:50 000 topographic maps cover all of South Africa, with contours 20 m apart: they show the big shape of the land, not the small dips. Draw to scale, for example 1 cm on paper for 1 m on the ground.

## What to look at on your site

### Water

Water is the first thing to plan for (chapter 7 covers it in detail).

1. Find the rainfall for your area. Most of South Africa gets its rain in summer; the Western Cape gets it in winter.
2. Look at the **catchment**: the land above yours that drains water onto your land.
3. Look for **runoff**, rainwater that flows over the surface instead of soaking in, and for its old signs: gullies, bare patches and washed-away soil. Runoff carries off soil and the nutrients you added.
4. Mark the water storage you have, places where tanks, ponds or dams could go, and your back-up sources: borehole, river, spring or tap.
5. Mark very dry and very wet areas, and plan how to water seedlings with the least effort.

### Terrain and slope

The best site is a gentle, well-drained slope facing the sun. Steep land is hard to work and suffers **erosion**: soil washed away by water.

Slope is often given as a percentage: the drop over 100 m. A drop of 1 m over 10 m is a 10% slope.

| Slope | Drop | Angle | What it means |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5% | 1 m in 20 m | about 3° | Easy for gardens and fields |
| 12% | 1 m in about 8 m | about 7° | Protect the soil with contour beds, grass strips or terraces |
| 32% | 1 m in about 3 m | about 18° | Keep under permanent trees or grass |

Bill Mollison, one of the founders of permaculture, advised keeping slopes steeper than about 18 degrees under permanent trees. Remember this when you choose land to buy or rent.

### Aspect

**Aspect** is the direction a slope or building faces. In South Africa the sun is in the north, so:

- **North- and east-facing slopes** get the most sun and warm up early. They are best for most crops.
- **West-facing slopes** get the hot afternoon sun, which can scorch plants in summer.
- **South-facing slopes** are cooler and shaded in winter. They suit trees, and in dry areas stay moist longer.

No amount of work will change where the sun rises. Check where trees and buildings throw their shadows; winter shadows are much longer (chapter 3).

### Access

Roads and paths are almost permanent. Run new ones along the contour. A path on the contour can form the bank of a **swale**, a ditch dug along the contour to catch rainwater (chapter 7). Give each path a very gentle fall, so rain drains off into a swale or tree basin instead of running down the path.

## Step 2: Zone planning

How far you walk decides what you look after. A garden at the far end gets forgotten; a garden by the kitchen door gets watered. Zone planning divides the farm into **zones** by how often you visit: daily things close to the house, rarely visited things further out.

| Zone | What it is | How often you visit | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | The house | All the time | Kitchen, seed store, tanks on the house |
| 1 | Right next to the house | Every day, often many times | Kitchen garden, herbs, nursery, compost, small animals |
| 2 | Close to the house | Most days | Food forest, fruit trees, chickens, staple crops |
| 3 | Main farming area | Weekly, or when needed | Field and market crops, orchard, pasture, dams |
| 4 | Semi-wild, lightly managed | Now and then | Woodlot, grazing |
| 5 | Wild | Rarely, to watch and learn | Natural veld or forest |

**Zone 1** gives the highest yield from the smallest area. Put here the **annual** vegetables (those that live one season) and morogo you pick often, kitchen herbs, dwarf fruit trees, small animals such as quail and rabbits, and small water stores. Cover every tank and well, and fence or cover ponds, so that no child can fall in.

**Zone 2** is larger and less intensive. Its **food forest**, a garden planted in layers like a natural forest, can include trees for **coppicing**: cutting a tree back to a low stump so that it grows new poles. For firewood and poles, plant sweet thorn (*Vachellia karroo*), an **indigenous** tree (one that grows naturally here), rather than wattle or gum, which spread into veld and streams.

**Zone 3** holds livestock, field crops and farm dams. **Zone 4** gives grazing, firewood and timber. **Zone 5** is natural land left wild, a refuge for the birds and insects that help control pests. Visit it to watch, learn and gather wild foods with care.

Not every site has room for all the zones. A town plot of about 1 000 square metres (a quarter of an acre) has space for Zone 1 and pieces of the others; a corner left for birds and insects is a tiny Zone 5. On communal land, Zones 4 and 5 may be shared grazing or veld. And zones are not neat circles: slope, water and paths bend them into other shapes.

## Step 3: Slope planning

Water runs downhill for free. Slope planning, after Bill Mollison's model, means looking at your land from the side and asking: where am I on the hill? Use the slope well and gravity does the carrying.

- **Water up high.** Put dams and tanks above the house and garden, and gravity moves the water without a pump. Every 10 m of height gives about 1 bar of pressure.
- **Trees on the steep upper slopes** slow runoff, help water soak in and hold the soil.
- **Beds, paths and swales on the contour** keep water in the land.
- **Compost heaps and animal shelters open downhill,** so compost and manure go down to the garden.
- **Drinking water stays clean.** Keep manure, compost and animal pens downhill of, and well away from, boreholes, wells and springs.
- **Greywater down to trees.** **Greywater**, used water from washing, baths and basins, flows by gravity to mulched tree basins or swales below the house, never onto morogo or other vegetables you eat raw (chapter 7).

## Try it

Start your base map this week.

1. Get a satellite print of your land, or a large sheet of paper. Mark north.
2. Walk the boundary. Draw the edges, buildings, paths, taps, tanks and big trees.
3. After the next good rain, walk the land again. Mark with arrows where water runs and where it collects.
4. Mark the warm, cold, wet and dry spots.
5. For one day, tally every trip you make to each place: the tap, the chickens, the garden, the woodpile.
6. With a pencil, draw rough zones over the map. Does the place you visit most sit closest to the house?

## Key points

- Watch your land through the seasons, and put what you see on a base map.
- Read your site for water, slope, aspect and access. North- and east-facing slopes are the warmest.
- Zones 0 to 5 place things by how often you visit them.
- Put water high and let gravity move it. Keep manure away from drinking water, and greywater away from vegetables eaten raw.

Keep your map on the wall and add to it every season. In a few years it will be the most useful book about your land.
