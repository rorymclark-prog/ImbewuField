# Planning your farm or homestead

Permaculture is a way of designing. You can grow food almost anywhere, but a good design saves you time, money and energy. It gives you a farm that makes little waste and gets more fertile every year.

A good design does two things:

- It brings out the full potential of the land.
- It meets as many needs as possible for every part of the system: plants, animals, water, buildings and people.

This chapter covers three planning steps: site assessment, zone planning and slope planning. The fourth step, sector planning, is in the next chapter.

## Step 1: Site assessment

### Collect information

Before you change anything, find out as much as you can about the land:

- **History:** how the land was used before, and what grew there.
- **Climate:** rainfall, frost, heat and wind.
- **Resources on or near the site:** water, soil, manure, mulch, building materials and helpers.
- **Markets:** where you could sell what you grow.

### Observe the land

Walk the land often, with an open mind. Do not decide too early. Look for:

- where rainwater flows after a storm, and where it collects,
- how trees lean or are shaped by the wind,
- which animals move across the land,
- which places feel warm, cold, wet or dry.

Observation never stops. Every season and every storm teaches you something new. The longer you watch, the better your decisions will be.

### Draw a base map

A base map is a drawing of your whole property as seen from above. Show:

- boundaries and fences,
- roads, paths and gates,
- the house, sheds, kraals and other buildings,
- how each part is used now, for example field, veld or garden,
- water: springs, streams, boreholes, tanks, taps and wet spots,
- big trees and rocky areas,
- contour lines if you have them. A contour line joins points that are at the same height.

Where to get a map:

- Your local municipality may have a map of your property.
- You can print a free satellite image (for example from Google Earth) and trace it.
- Government topographic maps at a scale of 1:50 000 show contour lines for the whole of South Africa.
- For a small plot, you can measure with a tape and draw your own map.

Draw your map to scale. For example, 1 cm on the paper can stand for 1 m on the ground. A scale ruler helps you measure and plan accurately.

## What to look at on your site

### Water

Water is the most important thing to plan for. Chapter 7 covers it in detail.

1. Find the rainfall figures for your area. Most of South Africa gets its rain in summer. The Western Cape gets most of its rain in winter.
2. Look at the catchment. This is the land above yours that drains water onto your land.
3. Look for runoff: water that runs off the surface after rain. Also look for signs of old runoff, such as gullies, bare patches and washed-away soil. Runoff carries away soil and the nutrients you added to it.
4. Note the water storage you have now, and places where you could add tanks, ponds or dams.
5. Note your back-up water sources: borehole, river, spring or municipal tap.
6. Mark very dry and very wet areas on your map.
7. Think about how you will water new seedlings with the least effort, and how you can use less water.

### Terrain and slope

The best site is a gentle slope with good drainage that faces the sun. Steep land is hard to work and erodes easily.

Slope is often given as a percentage. It tells you how many metres the land drops over 100 m. For example, if the land drops 1 m over 10 m, the slope is 10%.

| Slope | Drop | Angle | What it means |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5% | 1 m in 20 m | about 3° | Easy for gardens and fields |
| 12% | 1 m in about 8 m | about 7° | Protect the soil with contour beds, grass strips or terraces |
| 20% | 1 m in 5 m | about 11° | South African law needs written permission to cultivate |
| 32% | 1 m in about 3 m | about 18° | Keep under permanent trees or grass |

> **Note:** Under the Conservation of Agricultural Resources Act (43 of 1983), you need written permission from the Department of Agriculture before you cultivate land steeper than 20%. In some erosion-prone areas listed in the regulations, the limit is 12%. Ask your extension officer.

If your land is steep, plan terraces or contour beds. Bill Mollison, one of the founders of permaculture, advised that slopes steeper than about 18 degrees (about 32%) should stay under permanent tree cover to protect the soil. Keep this in mind when you choose land to buy or rent.

If you can get a topographic map, copy its contour lines onto your base map. They help you decide where each land use and each water store should go.

### Aspect

Aspect is the direction that a slope or a building faces. In the Southern Hemisphere the sun is in the northern part of the sky, so:

- **North- and east-facing slopes** are best for most crops. They get the most sun and warm up early in the day.
- **West-facing slopes** get the hot afternoon sun. This can scorch plants in summer.
- **South-facing slopes** are cooler and more shaded, especially in winter when shadows are long. They suit trees. In dry areas they also stay moist for longer.

No amount of work will change where the sun rises, so plan well. Check where nearby trees and buildings throw their shadows. In winter, shadows are much longer than in summer. Chapter 3 explains sun angles.

### Access

Roads and paths are almost permanent. Once they are built, they are hard to move. Mark all existing roads and paths on your base map.

When you plan new paths or roads, run them along the contour. A path on the contour can form the bank of a swale. A swale is a ditch dug along the contour to catch rainwater (see chapter 7). This harvests water, reduces runoff and helps control erosion. Give each path a very gentle fall so that rain drains off into a swale or tree basin, and does not run down the path.

## Step 2: Zone planning

Zone planning places each part of the farm according to how often you need to visit it. Things you visit every day go closest to the house. Things you visit rarely go further away. This saves walking, time and energy.

| Zone | What it is | How often you visit | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | The house | All the time | Kitchen, seed store, rainwater tanks on the house |
| 1 | Right next to the house | Every day, often many times | Kitchen garden, herbs, nursery, compost, small animals, small ponds |
| 2 | Close to the house | Most days | Mixed food forest, fruit trees, bigger vegetable garden, chickens, staple crops |
| 3 | Main farming area | Weekly, or when needed | Field and market crops, larger orchard, pasture, fish ponds, dams |
| 4 | Semi-wild, lightly managed | Now and then | Woodlot for firewood, poles and timber, grazing, large dams |
| 5 | Wild | Rarely, to watch and learn | Natural veld, bush or forest left for wildlife |

### Zone 1

Zone 1 is the busiest zone. It gives the highest yield from the smallest area. You manage it closely, spend the most time in it and harvest from it often. It has the greatest variety of plants and animals.

- Annual vegetables and leafy greens that you pick often.
- Herbs and medicinal plants that you use every day in the kitchen.
- Small or dwarf fruit trees.
- Small animals such as quail, guinea pigs and rabbits.
- Small water stores: rainwater tanks and small ponds.

> **Safety:** A small child can drown in a few centimetres of water. Cover every tank and well, and fence or cover ponds.

### Zone 2

Zone 2 is further from the house, larger and less intensive. You visit it most days to feed the chickens, collect eggs and pick fruit and vegetables that have a longer harvest. The food forest here has many species. In cooler areas it can include coppiced trees for firewood. Coppicing means cutting a tree back to a low stump so that it grows new poles.

> **Note:** Do not plant black wattle, Port Jackson, leucaena or gum trees (many *Eucalyptus* species) for firewood. They are listed invasive plants under South Africa's biodiversity law (NEMBA, 2020), and planting them is either banned or needs a permit. Indigenous trees such as sweet thorn (*Vachellia karroo*), karee (*Searsia lancea*) and wild olive (*Olea europaea* subsp. *africana*) can give firewood, poles and shelter.

### Zone 3

Zone 3 is bigger than Zone 2. Larger livestock graze pasture here. It also holds market vegetables and field crops, and a larger, simpler food forest with fewer species than in Zone 2. Water storage can be large, such as farm dams.

### Zone 4

Zone 4 is for farm forestry and grazing: firewood, poles and timber. It can be very large, and you manage it lightly.

### Zone 5

Zone 5 is natural land left for wildlife. It is a refuge for plants and animals, including the birds and insects that help control pests. Visit it to watch, to learn and to gather wild foods with care.

### Zones on real land

Not every site has room for all the zones.

- A town plot of about 1,000 square metres (a quarter of an acre) has space for Zone 1, plus small pieces of other zones. For example, a corner can be planted for birds and insects as a tiny Zone 5.
- A bigger rural plot can hold Zones 2, 3 and 4, and even some forestry.
- Only very large properties have a real Zone 5.
- On communal land, Zones 4 and 5 may be shared grazing land or veld.

Zones are not neat circles. Slope, aspect, water and paths bend them into other shapes. Draw a zone map over your base map. It will help you decide where each part of the farm should go.

## Step 3: Slope planning

Slope planning means looking at your land from the side and asking: where am I on the hill? If you use the slope well, gravity does much of the work for you. Look at the side view of your land to decide where to put dams, water tanks, roads, drains and diversions.

This approach follows Bill Mollison's slope-planning model:

- **Water up high.** Place dams, tanks and water catchments above the house and garden. Gravity then moves the water, and you do not need an electric pump. Every 10 m of height gives about 1 bar of water pressure.
- **Trees on the steep upper slopes.** Permanent trees on steep ground slow runoff, help water soak in and hold the soil.
- **Work along the contour.** Beds, paths and swales along the contour reduce erosion and keep water in the land.
- **Compost and animal shelters face downhill.** Build compost heaps and animal shelters with their openings facing downhill. Then you can move compost and manure down to the garden with the help of the slope.
- **Protect your drinking water.** Keep manure, compost and animal pens well away from, and downhill of, boreholes, wells and springs.
- **Greywater down to trees.** Let used water from the house flow by gravity into swales or tree basins below the house.

> **Safety:** Greywater can carry germs. Do not use kitchen water or water from washing nappies on vegetables eaten raw, such as lettuce and spinach. Send greywater to trees and other plants whose edible parts do not touch it. Chapter 7 explains greywater in detail.

## Key points

- Observe your land through the seasons before you make big changes.
- Draw a base map showing boundaries, buildings, water, roads, land use and contours.
- Check water, slope, aspect and access on your site.
- In South Africa the sun is in the north, so north- and east-facing slopes are the warmest and best for most crops.
- Slopes above 12% need soil protection; cultivating land steeper than 20% (12% in some areas) needs written permission.
- Zones 0 to 5 place things by how often you visit them: daily things close to the house, rarely visited things further away.
- Do not plant listed invasive trees such as black wattle or gum trees for firewood; use indigenous trees instead.
- Put water stores high and use gravity; keep manure away from drinking water; use greywater only on trees and plants not eaten raw.
