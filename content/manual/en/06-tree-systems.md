# Tree systems

Trees and shrubs that live for many years can give food, fuel, fodder, shelter and medicine for a lifetime. This chapter covers three tree systems: the **food forest**, the **windbreak** and how to **plant a single tree** well.

> **Note:** Many trees that are popular in permaculture books from other countries are **declared invasive plants in South Africa**. It is against the law to plant them. This chapter recommends only plants that are indigenous (naturally found in Southern Africa) or that are not on South Africa's invasive species lists. Before you buy any plant, ask the nursery for its botanical (Latin) name and check that it is not a listed invader.

## Food forests

### What a food forest is

A **food forest** (also called a **forest garden**) is not the same as an ordinary fruit orchard. It copies the way a natural forest is built: many plants of different heights grow together in **layers**, from tall trees down to ground covers and roots. Most of the plants are **perennials** (plants that live for more than two years).

Once it is established, a well-designed food forest needs less work than a vegetable garden. Leaf fall and pruned branches feed the soil. Birds, insects and other animals that live there help to control pests. And it gives you food as well.

People have grown food forests for thousands of years. For example, scientists have found that people in the eastern Amazon managed parts of the forest for food plants for at least 4,500 years.

In one food forest you can harvest fruit, nuts, berries, herbs, spices, firewood, poles, mulch, fodder, vines, root crops and mushrooms. For a home food forest, the main jobs once it is established are:

- seasonal pruning
- "chop and drop" mulching (cutting branches and leaving them on the ground as mulch)
- propagating new plants
- moving a chicken tractor (a movable chicken run) through the forest from time to time

A food forest can also be grown as a narrow strip along a fence line, where it can act as a windbreak.

### Why grow trees as well as vegetables?

Vegetables and staple crops take a lot of time, water and resources. The soil is dug and replanted every season, so it never develops beyond an early stage. Annual crops are often badly affected by pests and diseases and need frequent watering.

Perennial systems such as food forests:

- do not need to be replanted every season
- grow deeper roots, so once established they can reach water and nutrients that shallow-rooted vegetables cannot
- support many kinds of life at many levels
- protect the soil all year round
- can keep producing for many years

Young trees still need regular watering for their first one or two dry seasons. In dry areas, most fruit trees will need some watering every year.

### Where to plant a food forest

In a natural forest, one stage of plant life follows another over many years. This process is called **succession**. Pioneer plants (the first plants to grow on bare ground) improve the soil and give shelter, and longer-lived trees follow later.

In South Africa, forest is **not** the natural end-point everywhere. Grassland, fynbos, Karoo shrubland and savanna are complete, ancient natural systems in their own right. They are not "unfinished forest". Planting trees into healthy indigenous grassland or fynbos destroys rare plants, uses more water and can reduce the flow of streams.

- Plant your food forest on land that is **already disturbed**: old fields, bare yards, land around the homestead, eroded ground, or land cleared of invasive plants.
- **Do not** plough up or plant trees into healthy natural veld, grassland, fynbos or wetland.
- Keep trees well back from streams and wetlands unless they are indigenous riverbank trees.

### Two kinds of food forest

**Homestead food forest (Zone 2).** This is grown close to the house, where you visit it once or twice a day. It is mainly for the household: fruit, nuts, berries, medicinal and cooking herbs, most of your perennial plants and some annuals that seed themselves. It has many different species. If space is small, plant only a few big trees. You can also grow coppiced firewood in Zone 2.

**Larger food forest (Zone 3).** A Zone 2 food forest can produce a lot, but it may be too small for selling produce. In Zone 3 you can plant a larger food forest with bigger trees, or a **permaculture orchard**.

A permaculture orchard is a simpler food forest with fewer species, planted on a bigger scale for selling. It still includes support plants such as nitrogen-fixing trees and flowers for insects. It is usually managed more intensively and is often irrigated. You can plant it in strips with pasture in between, which also makes harvesting easier.

### Coppicing

**Coppicing** means cutting a tree down close to the ground so that it sends up many new shoots. You can cut these shoots again every few years without killing the tree. In many species coppicing even helps the tree live longer.

- Coppiced shoots give firewood, fence poles, stakes and tool handles.
- The shoots grow back much faster than a new tree grows from seed, because the roots are already big.
- Not every tree will re-sprout. Indigenous trees that coppice well include wild olive (*Olea europaea* subsp. *cuspidata*).

## Designing a food forest

1. **List your needs.** What do your family, your farm, your animals and local wildlife need? This helps you decide which plants to grow.
2. **Match plants to your climate.** Choose indigenous and exotic plants that suit your rainfall, frost and heat. Add a few that are only just tolerant of your climate. As the climate changes, these give you a buffer against unusual seasons.
3. **Choose a mix of plant types:** fruit, nuts, berries, nitrogen fixers, medicinal plants, plants that feed bees and birds, and plants for mulch.
4. **Spread the harvest over the year.** Look at when each plant is harvested. For example, citrus is mostly harvested in autumn and winter, and avocados in autumn and winter, while grapes and peaches are harvested in summer and almonds in late summer. Most fruits have early, mid-season and late varieties. Include some of each.
5. **Think about nutrition.** Plan a mix that gives your family a range of vitamins and minerals through the year.
6. **Check your soil.** Fruit trees are grafted onto rootstocks (the root part of a grafted tree) that suit different soils. Ask the nursery which rootstock suits your soil.
7. **Check every plant is not invasive.** Many useful exotic plants are declared invaders in South Africa. Planting them is against the law.
8. **Favour indigenous support plants.** For nitrogen fixers, ground covers and wildlife plants, choose indigenous species first.

> **Tip:** Visit natural bush or forest near you and take your time. Look at which ground covers grow in the shade and in the sun, and which pioneer trees grow first on disturbed ground. You may collect a little wild seed only with the landowner's permission. You need a permit to collect plants or seed in nature reserves and to collect protected species. Your local nursery can give you prices and advice when you make your plant list.

### Match fruit trees to your climate

South Africa has very different climates. The biggest question for fruit trees is **frost**.

| Climate | Fruit that usually does well | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Cold Highveld, Free State, Lesotho, Eastern Cape uplands (hard winter frost) | Apple, pear, quince, peach, nectarine, plum, apricot, fig, persimmon, grape, pecan | These deciduous trees need cold winters. Late spring frost can kill the flowers of apricot, almond and early peaches, so plant them on a slope, not in a frost pocket. |
| Western Cape (winter rain, dry summer) | Apple, pear, plum, apricot, peach, fig, grape, almond, carob, citrus, pomegranate | Summer irrigation is needed. Almonds and carob suit the dry summers. |
| Frost-free lowveld and coast (Limpopo and Mpumalanga lowveld, KwaZulu-Natal coast) | Mango, banana, papaya (pawpaw), avocado, litchi, citrus, black sapote, custard apple, jackalberry, turmeric | Most deciduous fruit trees do not get enough winter cold here. Choose low-chill varieties. |
| Areas with light frost (KwaZulu-Natal midlands, parts of Gauteng) | Citrus, avocado (cold-tolerant types such as Fuerte), fig, pecan, persimmon, low-chill peach and plum | Protect young citrus and avocado trees from frost for their first two or three winters. |

> **Tip:** Deciduous fruit varieties are sold by their chill requirement (how many cold hours they need in winter). Ask the nursery for varieties that match your area.

### Planting patterns and spacing

There are many ways to lay out a food forest: a mosaic of clumps, hedges, straight lines, alley cropping (crops grown in strips between rows of trees) or hedgerows. Choose a pattern that suits your site and needs.

- Make sure all trees get enough sun. In Southern Africa the sun is in the **north**, so put tall evergreen trees on the **south** side of the food forest, where their shade falls outside the garden.
- You can also place evergreen trees between deciduous trees (trees that lose their leaves in winter).
- Use the sunny edges of the forest for sun-loving herbs and perennials, as in the edge of a natural forest.

Work out how much space each tree needs when it is fully grown.

1. Measure your site.
2. Find out the full canopy width (the width of the leafy crown) of each tree.
3. Draw a simple plan on paper, or peg out the site.
4. Leave extra space between trees. For example, a tree with a canopy 4 m wide needs about 6 to 7 m between it and the next tree, unless you are planting a hedge. The space in between is used for paths, nitrogen fixers and smaller plants.
5. From your plan, make your plant shopping list.

## Planting a food forest

### Start with nitrogen fixers

A natural forest takes decades or even hundreds of years to grow. We can speed this up by planting many **pioneer plants** first.

It is like making a fire. First you light the kindling, which lights the twigs, then the branches, and finally the big logs catch. In the same way, we first build up the soil's fertility with fast-growing pioneers. Then the main fruit trees take over as they grow.

The best pioneers are **legumes** (plants of the bean family). Many legumes work with bacteria in their roots to take nitrogen from the air and add it to the soil. This is called **nitrogen fixing**. Legumes of all sizes also produce a lot of leaves and branches (biomass) for mulch.

| Plant | Use | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Sweet thorn *Vachellia karroo* | Nitrogen-fixing tree, fodder, firewood, gum | Indigenous and very hardy. It can spread into overgrazed veld, so do not plant it into healthy grassland. |
| Common hook thorn *Senegalia caffra* | Nitrogen-fixing tree, fodder, firewood, hedge | Indigenous; summer-rainfall areas. |
| Paperbark thorn *Vachellia sieberiana* | Nitrogen-fixing shade tree, fodder | Indigenous; a large tree for bigger spaces. |
| Keurboom *Virgilia oroboides* | Fast pioneer nitrogen fixer, flowers for bees | Indigenous to the southern and Western Cape; short-lived. |
| Pigeon pea *Cajanus cajan* | Nitrogen fixer, edible peas, chop-and-drop mulch | Short-lived shrub (a few years); killed by hard frost. |
| Cowpea *Vigna unguiculata* | Annual nitrogen-fixing ground cover, food | Summer crop. |
| Lablab bean *Lablab purpureus* | Annual or short-lived climber, nitrogen fixer, fodder | Summer crop; frost-tender. |
| Lupin *Lupinus angustifolius* | Winter nitrogen-fixing ground cover | Suits the Western Cape winter. Some lupin seed is bitter and poisonous; do not eat or feed it unless it is a sweet (edible) variety. |

> **Note:** Do not plant these well-known permaculture nitrogen fixers in South Africa. They are declared invaders: black wattle and other wattles (*Acacia mearnsii* and other Australian *Acacia* species), leucaena (*Leucaena leucocephala*), red sesbania (*Sesbania punicea*), black locust (*Robinia pseudoacacia*) and stinkbean (*Paraserianthes lophantha*). Use the indigenous thorn trees above instead.

### Planting steps

1. **Lay out your plants.** Measure the distances between them according to their canopy sizes. Mark the spots with pegs or a little lime powder. Leave larger gaps between the trees.
2. **Label everything.** Put a T-marker or label with the name next to each important plant. Otherwise you may forget what is where.
3. **Dig all the holes and planting basins.**
4. **Get compost, mulch and any soil improvers ready.**
5. **Plant the large fruit trees and other long-lived trees first.** Then plant the nitrogen-fixing trees.
6. **Plant medium-sized trees** in between the large-canopy trees.
7. **Plant shrubs and herbs**, about 1 m away from the main fruit trees.
8. **Plant perennial root crops.**
9. **Plant perennial ground covers** between the planting basins.
10. **Sow annual legume ground covers** last, such as cowpea in summer or lupin in winter. Just before sowing, wet the seed slightly and mix it with the right **inoculant** (a powder of the nitrogen-fixing bacteria for that type of legume; it is sold with legume seed or at farm stores). Keep inoculated seed out of the sun. Scatter the seed thickly on the soil and cover it lightly with mulch.
11. **Water** the trees, plants and seeds well.

## How a food forest grows

Your food forest will pass through several stages before it becomes a stable system. We can describe two main phases.

### Phase 1: Establishing (years 0 to 5)

- Fast-growing pioneer legumes are the main plants. They build fertility and biomass, fill the open spaces, shade out weeds and shelter the young fruit trees.
- In the rainy season, once the pioneer trees are big enough, **chop and drop**: cut their branches back hard and leave the cut branches and leaves on the ground.
- The mulch rots down and feeds the soil and its fungi, which forest trees need to thrive. Some of the legume's fine roots and root nodules also die back after pruning and release some nitrogen into the soil.
- You will not harvest many nuts or much fruit yet. But after the first year you can harvest fast-growing berries, self-seeding greens, perennial root crops, herbs and firewood from the chop and drop.

### Phase 2: Established (year 5 onwards)

- Grafted fruit trees may start bearing in the first three years. Some only start in year 5 to 7. On average, a food forest starts to mature around the fifth year.
- As the fruit trees fill out, slowly cut back the pioneer trees. In the end, only a few medium- and long-lived nitrogen-fixing trees remain.
- You can now let pigs, chickens or other animals forage in the forest for short periods to help with maintenance. Protect young tree trunks from goats and pigs.
- You will see much more wildlife as your plants give food and shelter.
- Prune to let more light in where it is needed, and make use of the sheltered microclimates (small areas with their own climate) that have formed.
- Fruit, nuts and berries increase as the trees mature, and coppiced trees can now be harvested for firewood.

## Windbreaks

### What a windbreak does

A **windbreak** (or **shelter belt**) is a row or belt of trees, shrubs and other plants that protects homes, crops and animals from the wind. There is no single design that fits every site. Plan your windbreak to suit your land and your needs.

Windbreaks are especially useful in very windy, dry places such as parts of the Western Cape, the Karoo and the central plateau of Southern Africa, where wind dries out crops and blows away the soil.

A good windbreak can:

- slow down strong, gusty winds
- reduce soil erosion by wind
- protect young fruit trees, food forest plants and vegetables
- give extra fodder: large animals can browse the edge, and chickens can forage underneath (keep animals out while the windbreak is young)
- give firewood and poles when the lower branches are pruned
- bring up nutrients from deep in the soil with its roots, which return to the surface in fallen leaves
- give fruit and nuts, but only as a side benefit, because wind lowers the size and quality of the crop
- give shelter, nectar and pollen for bees, insects and birds

### How to plan a windbreak

1. **Find the damaging wind.** Watch which way the strongest and driest winds come from in each season. Use a compass or the position of the sun to name the direction. Winds can change with the season; for example, in the Western Cape strong south-easterly winds blow in summer and north-westerly winds bring the winter rain.
2. **Plant across the wind.** Plant the windbreak at right angles to the damaging wind, on the side of the garden that the wind comes from.
3. **Make it long and without gaps.** Wind speeds up through gaps and around the ends of a windbreak.
4. **Let some wind through.** A windbreak that lets some wind through (about half as dense as a solid wall) works better than a solid wall. A solid barrier makes swirling winds on the sheltered side.
5. **Use several rows.** Plant tall trees at the back (on the windward side), medium trees in the middle and dense shrubs at the front.
6. **Leave space for light.** The sheltered area stretches downwind for many times the height of the windbreak. So there is no need to plant it right against the garden. Tree roots also compete with crops for water. In Southern Africa the sun is in the north, so a windbreak on the **north** side of a garden casts shade over it. On the south side it casts little shade.

> **Note:** Commercial farms often plant beefwood (*Casuarina* spp.), gum trees (*Eucalyptus*) or pines as windbreaks. Beefwood (*Casuarina cunninghamiana* and *C. equisetifolia*) and many gum and pine species are **Category 2 invaders**. They may only be grown with a government permit, inside a marked area, and never within 30 m of a river, dam or wetland. Smallholders should use indigenous windbreak plants instead.

### Windbreaks and fire

A green, well-watered windbreak can slow a fire. But it is **not** a firebreak, and it can even carry fire into your homestead. Many aromatic plants, such as wilde als, wild rosemary, rosemary, lavender and camphor bush, contain oils that burn fiercely. Succulents such as spekboom, aloes and sour fig burn less easily.

- Where there is fire risk, keep a cleared firebreak between the veld and your windbreak.
- Keep dry, dead material cleared from under the windbreak.
- Under the National Veld and Forest Fire Act, landowners in fire-risk areas must make and maintain firebreaks on their boundaries. Ask your local Fire Protection Association.

### Four good indigenous windbreak plants

**Wild dagga** (*Leonotis leonurus*). A fast-growing shrub, usually 2 to 3 m tall, with orange flowers that attract sunbirds. It tolerates drought and wind and forms a full, dense bush. It can be planted closer to the garden. It is used in traditional medicine. If it is cut or burnt, it grows again from the base.

**False olive** (*Buddleja saligna*). A dense, bushy small tree with small, sweetly scented cream flowers from late winter into summer. Bees and other insects visit the flowers. It grows in most soils and tolerates wind, frost and drought. It can reach 4 m or more if not pruned, so use it in the back row. It can also be pruned into a neat, thick hedge.

**Karee** (*Searsia lancea*, formerly *Rhus lancea*). One of Southern Africa's hardiest trees. It grows in many habitats, tolerates frost and drought, and has a non-aggressive root system. It can reach about 7 to 9 m, so plant it in the back row. It gives shade, fodder and firewood.

**King protea** (*Protea cynaroides*), for fynbos areas only. Proteas grow naturally in the windy Cape and cope well with wind. The king protea is a compact shrub, usually 0.5 to 2 m tall, with broad leaves and very large flowers. Plant it in the middle row. It needs acid, well-drained soil, full sun and good air movement, so it will not grow well on the clay soils of most summer-rainfall farms. Cut flowers may bring extra income, but you need a permit from your provincial conservation authority to pick flowers from the wild or to sell protected plants. Mature plants re-sprout after veld fires.

## Planting a tree

Plant at the start of the rainy season. In summer-rainfall areas this is usually October to December, after the first good rains. In the Western Cape, plant in late autumn and winter (about April to July). Plant frost-tender trees after the last frost. Deciduous fruit trees can also be planted in mid-winter while they have no leaves.

1. **Choose a good tree.** Buy from a reputable nursery. Choose a sturdy tree with a thick stem and healthy leaves. Avoid trees with roots circling around the inside of the bag. Buy certified disease-free trees where you can, especially citrus.
2. **Plant it soon.** Plant as soon as possible after buying, so the roots can start growing.
3. **Clear the competition.** Remove weeds and grasses, especially kikuyu grass, in a circle about 1 m wide, so they do not compete for water and food. (Kikuyu is itself a listed invader in protected areas and wetlands.)
4. **Dig the hole.** Make it at least **two to three times as wide** as the root ball, and about as deep as the root ball. In hard or compacted soil, dig a hole about 60 cm wide and deep and break up the soil at the bottom and sides.
5. **Keep the soils apart.** Put the dark topsoil on one side and the lighter subsoil on the other.
6. **Put in a stake (in windy places).** Knock a stake about 50 cm into the ground, on the side the wind comes from, **before** you plant, so you do not damage the roots.
7. **Test the drainage.** Fill the hole with water and let it drain. If water is still standing the next day, the drainage is poor: plant the tree on a raised mound instead.
8. **Mix the backfill.** Mix the topsoil with some compost, a little well-rotted manure and worm castings, about one part compost to three parts soil. Too much compost in the hole can make the roots stay inside the hole.
9. **Take the tree out of its bag.** Handle the root ball gently. Loosen any roots that circle around the outside.
10. **Set the height.** Stand the tree in the hole so that the top of the root ball is level with the surrounding soil. Keep the graft union (the bump where a fruit tree was grafted) well above the soil.
11. **Fill the hole.** Fill in with the soil mix and firm it gently around the roots with your hands or feet. Do not stamp it hard.
12. **Tie the tree.** Tie the stem loosely to the stake with a soft tie in a figure of eight, so it can move a little. Remove the tie after about a year.
13. **Make a basin.** Make a shallow basin around the tree, about 1 m wide, with a low ridge of soil around it to hold water. Make it wider as the tree grows.
14. **Mulch.** Cover the basin with a thick layer of mulch (5 to 10 cm), but keep it about 10 cm away from the stem, to prevent rot.
15. **Water well.** Water deeply straight after planting, about a full bucket (20 L) or more.
16. **Keep watering, deeply.** If there is no rain, water every two to three days for the first two weeks. Then give a deep soak about once a week through the first dry season, more often in sandy soil or very hot weather. Push a finger into the soil beside the tree: water when it feels dry 5 to 10 cm down. Deep watering now and then grows deep roots; a little water every day grows shallow roots and can rot the roots in clay soil.

## Key points

- A food forest copies a natural forest, with layers of trees, shrubs, herbs, ground covers, roots and climbers.
- Plant food forests on land that is already disturbed. South Africa's grasslands and fynbos are natural systems, not unfinished forest; do not plant trees into healthy veld.
- Match fruit trees to your frost and winter chill: deciduous fruit for cold areas, subtropical fruit only for frost-free areas.
- Start with nitrogen-fixing pioneers such as sweet thorn, hook thorn, pigeon pea and cowpea, then chop and drop them as the fruit trees grow.
- Never plant listed invaders such as wattles, leucaena, sesbania, elderberry, beefwood or white mulberry. Choose indigenous plants for support, windbreak and wildlife roles.
- Plant windbreaks across the damaging wind, in several rows, without gaps and a little open. Keep them away from the north side of the garden, where they cast shade.
- A windbreak is not a firebreak. Aromatic shrubs burn fiercely.
- Plant a tree in a wide hole, at the same depth it grew in the bag, mulch it, and water deeply once a week rather than a little every day.
