# Tree systems

Trees are the long game. A vegetable bed feeds you for one season. A tree planted well feeds your children and their children, with fruit, firewood, **fodder** (feed for animals), shade and medicine, and it never has to be dug or sown again.

Trees also do quiet work. Their roots hold the soil and reach water that vegetables cannot. Their leaves fall and feed the ground. Their branches break the wind and shelter the birds that eat your pests. A forest is built upon a forest: each plant makes the ground better for the next one.

Three tree systems carry most of this work on a smallholding: the **food forest**, a garden planted in layers like a natural forest; the **windbreak**, a belt of trees and shrubs that slows the wind; and the single tree, planted well. Every plant recommended here is **indigenous** (it grows naturally in Southern Africa) or is not a declared invader in South Africa. Many trees praised in overseas permaculture books are **invasive species** here: plants from elsewhere that spread out of control. Buy by the Latin name.

**By the end of this chapter you will be able to:**

- Choose a site for a food forest and pick fruit trees that suit your frost and winter cold
- Plant a food forest in the right order, starting with nitrogen-fixing pioneers
- Plan a windbreak across your damaging wind, with indigenous trees and shrubs
- Plant a single tree so that it roots deep and survives its first dry season

## Food forests

Vegetables and staple crops ask a lot of us: digging, sowing and watering, season after season. Because the soil is turned and replanted every year, it never grows past the first stage of **succession** (the natural, step-by-step change from bare ground to grass, shrubs and trees). Annual crops also suffer badly from pests and dry spells.

A food forest is a garden planted like a natural forest, in layers, from tall trees down to low plants and roots. It is not an orchard of trees standing in bare ground. Most of its plants are **perennials** (plants that live for more than two years). Once it is established, a food forest:

- needs no replanting each season
- grows deeper roots that reach water and nutrients shallow vegetables cannot
- keeps the soil covered all year
- feeds itself with its own fallen leaves and prunings
- gives homes to birds and insects that keep pests in check

From one food forest you can harvest fruit, nuts, berries, herbs, spices, firewood, poles, fodder, root crops and **mulch** (material spread over the soil to keep it moist and protected). The work that remains is seasonal pruning, **chop and drop** (cutting plants and leaving them on the ground as mulch), propagating new plants and moving an **animal tractor** (a movable chicken pen) through now and then. Young trees still need water through their first one or two dry seasons, and in dry areas most fruit trees need some water every year.

People have farmed this way for a very long time. In the eastern Amazon, people tended the forest for food plants for at least 4,500 years, and the forest there is still full of the edible trees they favoured.

### Where to plant a food forest

In much of South Africa, forest is not the natural end of succession. Grassland, fynbos, Karoo shrubland and savanna are ancient, complete systems in their own right, not forest waiting to happen. Trees planted into healthy grassland or fynbos crowd out rare plants and use more water.

1. Plant on land that is **already disturbed**: old fields, bare yards, eroded ground, land around the homestead, or land cleared of invasive plants.
2. Leave healthy veld, grassland, fynbos and wetland as they are.
3. Keep trees back from streams and wetlands, unless they are indigenous riverbank trees.

### Two kinds of food forest

Permaculture plans a farm in **zones**, by how often you visit each part.

**Homestead food forest (Zone 2).** Close to the house, where you pass once or twice a day. It feeds the household: fruit, nuts, berries, cooking and medicinal herbs, and self-seeding annuals. It holds many species. Where space is tight, plant only a few big trees.

**Larger food forest or orchard (Zone 3).** Further out, with bigger trees, for selling. A **permaculture orchard** is a simpler food forest with fewer species, planted on a larger scale and often irrigated. It still carries **nitrogen fixers** (plants that add nitrogen from the air to the soil) and flowers for insects. Plant it in strips with pasture between them, which also makes harvesting easier.

### Coppicing

**Coppicing** means cutting a tree back close to the ground so that it sends up many new shoots. You cut the shoots again every few years for firewood, fence poles, stakes and tool handles, and the tree lives on. The shoots grow back far faster than a new tree from seed, because the roots are already big. Not every tree re-sprouts. Wild olive (*Olea europaea* subsp. *cuspidata*) is an indigenous tree that coppices well, and a coppice patch of it in Zone 2 gives firewood year after year.

## Designing a food forest

Design on paper first. A tree in the wrong place costs years.

1. **List your needs.** What do your family, your animals and the local wildlife need? Food, firewood, fodder, medicine, shade?
2. **Match plants to your climate.** Choose indigenous and exotic plants that suit your rain, frost and heat. Add a few that are only just hardy where you live. As the climate changes, these give you a buffer.
3. **Mix the types:** fruit, nuts, berries, nitrogen fixers, medicinal plants, flowers for bees and birds, and plants for mulch.
4. **Spread the harvest.** In South Africa avocados come mostly from about March to September and citrus through autumn and winter, while grapes and peaches ripen in summer and almonds in late summer. Most fruits come in early, mid-season and late varieties. Plant some of each.
5. **Think about nutrition.** Plan so that the family eats a range of fruit and greens through the whole year.
6. **Match the rootstock to your soil.** Fruit trees are made by **grafting** (joining a good fruit variety onto the roots of another plant). The **rootstock** is that root part, and different rootstocks suit different soils. Ask the nursery which rootstock suits yours.
7. **Favour indigenous support plants.** For nitrogen fixers, **groundcovers** (low plants that spread over the soil) and wildlife plants, choose indigenous species first.

> **Tip:** Before you write your plant list, walk slowly through the nearest wild veld or forest. Notice which groundcovers grow in shade and which in sun, and which pioneer trees come up first on disturbed ground. The veld is the best teacher of what will thrive on your land.

### Match fruit trees to your climate

The biggest question for fruit trees in South Africa is **frost**.

| Climate | Fruit that usually does well | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Cold Highveld, Free State, Lesotho, Eastern Cape uplands (hard frost) | Apple, pear, quince, peach, nectarine, plum, apricot, fig, persimmon, grape, pecan | These deciduous trees need cold winters. Late frost kills the flowers of apricot, almond and early peach, so plant them on a slope, not in a **frost pocket** (a low place where cold air settles). |
| Western Cape (winter rain, dry summer) | Apple, pear, plum, apricot, peach, fig, grape, almond, carob, citrus, pomegranate | Needs summer water. Almond and carob suit the dry summers. |
| Frost-free lowveld and coast (Limpopo and Mpumalanga lowveld, KwaZulu-Natal coast) | Mango, banana, papaya (pawpaw), avocado, litchi, citrus, black sapote, jackalberry | Most deciduous fruit does not get enough winter cold here. Choose low-chill varieties. |
| Light frost (KwaZulu-Natal midlands, parts of Gauteng) | Citrus, avocado, fig, pecan, persimmon, low-chill peach and plum | Fuerte avocado takes cold better than Hass. Protect young citrus and avocado from frost for two or three winters. |

Deciduous fruit varieties are sold by their chill requirement: how many cold hours they need in winter. Ask for varieties that match your area.

### Planting patterns and spacing

Lay the forest out as clumps, hedges, straight lines, or strips of crops between rows of trees. Choose what suits your site. Whatever the pattern, every tree needs sun. In Southern Africa the sun is in the **north**, so put tall evergreen trees on the **south** side, where their shade falls outside the garden, or between trees that lose their leaves in winter. Use the sunny **edge** (the place where two areas meet) for sun-loving herbs, as a natural forest does.

1. Measure your site.
2. Find the full **canopy** width of each tree: the width of its leafy crown when grown.
3. Draw a plan on paper, or peg the site.
4. Leave room. A tree with a 4 m canopy needs about 6 to 7 m to the next tree, unless you are planting a hedge. The space between holds paths, nitrogen fixers and smaller plants.
5. Make your shopping list from the plan.

## Planting a food forest

A natural forest takes decades, even centuries, to grow. We speed it up by planting pioneers first.

Think of lighting a fire. The kindling lights the twigs, the twigs light the branches, and at last the big logs catch. In a food forest the fast pioneers build fertility first, and the fruit trees take over as they grow.

The best pioneers are **legumes** (plants of the bean and pea family). Most of them fix nitrogen: their roots work with bacteria to take nitrogen from the air and add it to the soil. They also make masses of **biomass** (leaves and branches) for mulch.

| Plant | Use | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Sweet thorn *Vachellia karroo* | Nitrogen fixer, fodder, firewood, gum | Indigenous and very hardy. It thickens into overgrazed veld, so plant it in your food forest, never into healthy grassland. |
| Common hook thorn *Senegalia caffra* | Nitrogen fixer, fodder, firewood, hedge | Indigenous; summer-rainfall areas. |
| Keurboom *Virgilia oroboides* | Fast nitrogen fixer, flowers for bees | Indigenous to the southern and Western Cape; short-lived. |
| Pigeon pea *Cajanus cajan* | Nitrogen fixer, edible peas, chop and drop | Shrub that lives a few years; killed by hard frost. |
| Cowpea *Vigna unguiculata* | Nitrogen-fixing groundcover, food | Summer annual. |
| Lablab bean *Lablab purpureus* | Climbing nitrogen fixer, fodder | Summer; frost-tender. |
| Lupin *Lupinus angustifolius* | Winter nitrogen-fixing groundcover | Suits the Western Cape winter. Bitter lupin seed is poisonous, so eat or feed only sweet varieties. |

Wattles, leucaena and sesbania fill this role in many overseas books, but they are invaders here. The indigenous thorn trees above do the same job.

### Planting steps

1. **Peg out the plants.** Measure the gaps by canopy size and mark each spot with a peg or a little lime. Leave the larger gaps between the big trees.
2. **Label everything.** Put a name marker by each important plant. You will forget what is where.
3. **Dig all the holes and planting basins.**
4. **Have compost and mulch ready.** **Compost** is plant and animal waste rotted down into dark, crumbly soil food.
5. **Plant the large, long-lived trees first,** then the nitrogen-fixing trees.
6. **Plant medium-sized trees** between the large ones.
7. **Plant shrubs and herbs** about 1 m from the main fruit trees.
8. **Plant perennial root crops,** then **perennial groundcovers** between the basins.
9. **Sow annual legume groundcovers last:** cowpea in summer, lupin in winter. Just before sowing, wet the seed slightly and mix it with the right **inoculant** (a powder of the nitrogen-fixing bacteria for that legume, sold with the seed or at farm stores). Keep inoculated seed out of the sun. Scatter it thickly and cover it lightly with mulch.
10. **Water** everything well.

## How a food forest grows

A food forest passes through several stages before it settles. We can think of two phases.

**Phase 1: establishing (years 0 to 5).** The fast pioneer legumes lead. They fill the open spaces, shade out weeds and shelter the young fruit trees. In the rainy season, once they are big enough, chop and drop: cut their branches back hard and leave the cut material on the ground as mulch. It rots down and feeds the soil and the fungi that forest trees need. Some of the legume's fine roots and nodules also die back after the cut and release a little nitrogen. You will not pick much fruit yet, but after the first year there are berries, self-seeding greens, root crops, herbs and firewood from the prunings.

**Phase 2: established (year 5 onwards).** Grafted trees may bear in their first three years; others only in year 5 to 7. Most food forests begin to mature around year 5.

1. As the fruit trees fill out, cut the pioneers back further each year, until only a few long-lived nitrogen-fixing trees remain.
2. Let chickens, pigs or other animals forage through for short spells. Guard young trunks from goats and pigs, which strip bark.
3. Prune to let light in where it is needed, and use the sheltered **microclimates** (small pockets with their own climate) that have formed.
4. Start cutting the coppice trees for firewood.

The wildlife arrives on its own. Birds, lizards and insects move in as the layers close, and fruit, nuts and berries build year by year.

## The layers of a food forest

A food forest is usually described in seven layers. Choose plants for each layer that suit your climate. The **understorey** is the layer of smaller trees and shrubs beneath the tallest trees.

|---|---|---|
| 1. Tall trees (canopy) | The tallest trees. Plant only a few in a small food forest. | Wild plum *Harpephyllum caffrum*, Cape chestnut *Calodendrum capense*, pecan; jackalberry *Diospyros mespiliformis* and black sapote in hot, frost-free areas |
| 2. Small trees (understorey) | Most fruit trees, kept small by pruning | Apple, pear, quince, peach, plum, apricot, fig, persimmon, citrus, avocado, carob; indigenous Kei apple *Dovyalis caffra*, sour plum *Ximenia caffra*, buffalo thorn *Ziziphus mucronata*, monkey plum *Diospyros lycioides*, red ivory *Berchemia zeyheri* |
| 3. Shrubs | Woody plants from about 0.5 to 3 m | Forest num-num *Carissa bispinosa*, pigeon pea, wilde als *Artemisia afra*, wild rosemary *Eriocephalus africanus*, spekboom *Portulacaria afra*, krantz aloe *Aloe arborescens*, rosemary, lavender, Cape gooseberry |
| 4. Herbs and vegetables | Soft-stemmed plants, many of them **annual** (living one season) | Amaranth for morogo and grain, okra, sorghum, sunflower, kale, Swiss chard, broad bean, basil, sage, oregano, yarrow, French marigold |
| 5. Groundcover | Low plants that cover the soil | Pumpkin, tsamma melon *Citrullus lanatus*, sour fig *Carpobrotus dimidiatus*, wild garlic *Tulbaghia violacea*, pennywort *Centella asiatica*, strawberry, thyme, nasturtium, mint in a contained bed |

For berries, grow Kei apple, cross-berry or num-num rather than elderberry or white mulberry, which are invaders here. Birds carry fig seed into the veld, so pull out fig seedlings you find away from the garden. For medicine, pepperbark *Warburgia salutaris* suits frost-free gardens; buy nursery-grown plants, because the tree has been stripped from the wild for its bark.

## Windbreaks

Wind dries out crops, snaps young trees and carries away bare soil. In the Karoo, the Western Cape and on the high central plateau, it is one of the hardest things a garden faces.

A windbreak is a row or belt of trees, shrubs or tall grass that slows the wind and protects crops, animals and homes. There is no single design; each site needs its own. A good windbreak:

- slows strong, gusty winds and stops wind **erosion** (soil blown or washed away)
- shelters young fruit trees, the food forest and vegetables
- gives fodder at its edge (keep animals out while it is young) and firewood from pruning
- brings up nutrients with deep roots, which return to the surface in fallen leaves
- feeds and shelters bees, insects and birds
- gives some fruit and nuts, though wind lowers the crop, so treat fruit as a bonus

### How to plan a windbreak

1. **Find the damaging wind.** Watch where the strongest, driest winds come from in each season. In the Western Cape, for example, the south-easter blows in summer and the north-wester brings the winter rain.
2. **Plant across it,** at right angles to the damaging wind, on the side of the garden it comes from.
3. **Make it long, with no gaps.** Wind speeds up through gaps and around the ends.
4. **Let some wind through.** A windbreak about half as dense as a wall shelters a longer stretch than a solid wall, which makes the wind tumble and swirl behind it.
5. **Plant several rows:** tall trees at the back (the windward side), medium trees in the middle, dense shrubs in front.
6. **Leave space for light.** The shelter reaches downwind for many times the windbreak's height, often 10 to 20 times, so it need not stand against the garden. Tree roots also compete with crops for water. Because the sun is in the north, a windbreak on the **north** side shades the garden; on the south side it casts little shade.

Beefwood (*Casuarina*), gum and pine are common farm windbreaks, but they spread into veld and rivers. Indigenous trees and shrubs do the job and feed wildlife too.

### Windbreaks and fire

A green, watered windbreak can slow a fire, but it is not a **firebreak** (a cleared strip that a veld fire cannot cross), and it can carry fire to your house. Aromatic plants such as wilde als, wild rosemary, rosemary, lavender and camphor bush are full of oils that burn fiercely. Succulents such as spekboom, aloes and sour fig burn less easily.

1. Where veld fires come through, keep a cleared firebreak between the veld and the windbreak.
2. Clear dry, dead material from under the windbreak before the fire season.
3. Keep the aromatic shrubs away from the side the fires come from.

### Plants for windbreaks

**Wild dagga** (*Leonotis leonurus*) is a fast shrub, 2 to 5 m tall, with orange flowers that bring sunbirds. It stands drought and wind, forms a dense bush, and can go close to the garden. Cut or burnt, it grows again from the base.

**False olive** (*Buddleja saligna*) is a dense small tree, usually 4 to 5 m on the Highveld, with honey-scented cream flowers from late winter into summer that bees love. It tolerates wind, frost and drought, and can be clipped into a thick hedge.

**Karee** (*Searsia lancea*) is one of Southern Africa's hardiest trees. It stands frost and drought, grows to about 8 m, and its roots are gentle. Put it in the back row for shade, fodder and firewood.

## Planting a tree

A young tree's hardest time is its first dry season. A hole that is too small, or water that only wets the surface, is enough to kill it. Plant it right once, and the tree looks after itself for decades.

Plant at the start of the rains: October to December after the first good rain in summer-rainfall areas, and about April to July in the Western Cape. Plant frost-tender trees after the last frost. Deciduous fruit trees can go in during mid-winter, while they have no leaves.

1. **Choose a good tree.** Buy a sturdy tree with a thick stem and healthy leaves. Avoid trees with roots circling inside the bag. For citrus, buy certified disease-free trees.
2. **Plant it soon** after you buy it.
3. **Clear a circle** about 1 m wide of weeds and grass, especially kikuyu, so they do not steal water.
4. **Dig wide, not deep.** Make the hole two to three times as wide as the root ball and about as deep. In hard ground, dig about 60 cm wide and deep and loosen the bottom and sides. Put the dark **topsoil** (the top, darkest layer) on one side and the pale subsoil on the other.
5. **Test the drainage.** Fill the hole with water. If water still stands the next day, plant the tree on a raised mound instead.
6. **Stake it in windy places.** Knock a stake about 50 cm into the ground on the windward side before you plant, so you do not spear the roots.
7. **Mix the backfill:** about one part compost to three parts topsoil, with a little well-rotted manure. Too much compost keeps the roots inside the hole, as if it were a pot.
8. **Set the height.** Loosen any circling roots. Stand the tree so the top of the root ball is level with the soil around it, and the graft union (the bump where a fruit tree was grafted) stays well above the soil.
9. **Fill and firm** gently with your hands or feet. Do not stamp.
10. **Tie it** loosely to the stake with a soft tie in a figure of eight, so the stem can sway a little. Remove the tie after about a year.
11. **Make a basin** about 1 m wide with a low ridge of soil to hold water. Widen it as the tree grows.
12. **Mulch** the basin 5 to 10 cm thick, keeping the mulch about 10 cm from the stem so it does not rot.
13. **Water deeply:** a full 20 L bucket or more straight after planting. If no rain comes, water every two to three days for two weeks, then give a deep soak about once a week through the first dry season, more often in sand or great heat. Push a finger into the soil: water when it is dry 5 to 10 cm down.

A deep soak now and then sends roots down after the water. A little every day keeps them near the surface, where they dry out in the first hot spell, and in clay it can rot them.

## Try it

Peg out your first food-forest patch this week.

1. Choose a patch of disturbed ground near the house, at least 10 m by 10 m, with sun from the north.
2. Stand on it on a windy afternoon and note where the damaging wind comes from.
3. Pick two or three fruit trees that suit your frost, and write down each one's grown canopy width.
4. Peg their positions, 6 to 7 m apart for trees with a 4 m canopy. Put a peg for a nitrogen fixer between each pair.
5. Dig one planting hole, two to three times as wide as a nursery bag, and do the drainage test.
6. Draw the patch in a notebook: pegs, wind direction, north, and what the drainage test showed.

## Key points

- Plant food forests on disturbed land near the home. Healthy grassland and fynbos are complete systems, not unfinished forest.
- Match fruit trees to your frost and winter cold, and spread the harvest across the year.
- Start with nitrogen-fixing pioneers, chop and drop them as the fruit trees grow, and choose indigenous support plants over invaders.
- Plant windbreaks across the damaging wind, in several rows, without gaps and a little open, and keep them off the north side of the garden.
- Dig wide, plant at the depth the tree grew, mulch, and water deeply once a week rather than a little every day.

Plant the pioneers this season and the fruit trees behind them. In five years you may walk in shade where there was bare ground, with birds bringing in the seed of the next forest.
