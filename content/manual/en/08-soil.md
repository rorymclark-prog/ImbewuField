# Soil: feed the soil, and it will feed you

## Why soil matters

Soil is the start of every garden and farm. Healthy soil grows healthy plants, and healthy plants feed healthy animals and people. In permaculture we say: **feed the soil, not the plant.**

Soil forms very slowly. Rock breaks down into small pieces through sun, rain, frost and roots. Plants, animals, fungi and tiny living things (**micro-organisms**) add dead leaves, roots and dung, and slowly turn them into dark, rich **organic matter**. It can take hundreds of years, and sometimes up to 1 000 years, to form just 1 cm of soil. Rain and wind can wash or blow that same soil away in one storm. That is why we must protect it.

Deep-rooted plants, such as trees and plants with a long **taproot** (one thick root that grows straight down), reach into the deeper soil. They take up water and minerals from there, and when their leaves fall and rot, some of those minerals end up in the topsoil.

Healthy soil is alive. One handful holds many millions of bacteria, fungi and other tiny creatures. They break down dead material and release the nutrients that plants need. When soil is covered and left undisturbed, organic matter slowly builds up and the soil gets deeper and richer.

As a farmer, your soil is your most important resource. Get to know it. Dig into it, look at it, feel it and smell it.

## The layers of the soil

If you dig a deep hole or look at the side of a road cutting or gully, you will see that soil has layers. This is called the **soil profile**.

| Layer | What it is |
|---|---|
| Leaf litter | Dead leaves and plant material on the surface, starting to rot. |
| A: Topsoil | Usually the darkest layer. It holds most of the organic matter, roots and soil life. This is where fertility lives. It is often only 10 to 30 cm deep. |
| B: Subsoil | Lighter in colour, with less organic matter. Clay, iron and minerals such as calcium and magnesium that wash down from above often collect here. Deep roots take water and nutrients from this layer. |
| C: Weathered rock | Broken-down parent rock mixed with some soil. Very little life. |
| R: Bedrock | Solid rock. |

A deep, dark topsoil is a good sign. Topsoil holds water well because it is rich in organic matter and has a crumbly structure with many small spaces for air and water. Without soil life, soil quickly loses its fertility.

## Soil texture: sand, silt, clay and loam

Soil is made of mineral particles of different sizes, plus organic matter, air and water. The mix of particle sizes is called the **soil texture**.

- **Sand** is the biggest particle. It feels gritty.
- **Silt** is medium-sized. It feels smooth and floury, like talcum powder.
- **Clay** is the smallest particle. It feels sticky when wet and hard when dry.

### Sandy soil

- Loose and easy to dig. Feels gritty and falls apart in your hand.
- Water drains through it quickly, so it dries out fast.
- It holds few nutrients, because they wash down with the water.
- Often light in colour and low in organic matter.
- Improve it by adding plenty of compost, manure and mulch. Organic matter helps sandy soil hold water and nutrients like a sponge.

### Clay soil

- Heavy, and hard to dig when dry. Sticky and slippery when wet.
- When you squeeze a wet handful, it holds together in a ball and can be rolled into a long, thin sausage.
- It holds a lot of water and nutrients, but water drains through slowly. Clay soil can become waterlogged, with too little air for roots.
- It cracks when it dries and forms a hard crust on the surface.
- Clay soils can be red, brown, yellow, grey or black. The colour comes mostly from iron and from how well the soil drains, not from the clay itself. Grey or blue-grey patches often mean the soil stays wet for long periods.
- Improve it by adding compost and mulch, and by not walking or digging on it when it is wet. Organic matter makes clay more crumbly so that air and water can move through it.

### Loam soil

- A mix of sand, silt and clay, usually with good organic matter.
- Dark, soft and crumbly. It holds together a little when squeezed but breaks up easily.
- It drains well but also holds enough water and nutrients.
- It smells sweet and earthy.
- Loam is the best soil for most vegetables. With time, compost and mulch, you can move sandy or clay soil closer to loam.

> **Note:** You cannot change your soil texture, but you can change your soil's **structure** and its organic matter. Organic matter is the answer for both sandy and clay soils.

## Testing your soil

### The feel test (ribbon test)

This test takes two minutes and needs no equipment.

1. Take a small handful of soil from about 10 cm deep. Remove stones and roots.
2. Add water a little at a time and knead the soil with your fingers until it is like putty and just stops sticking to your hand.
3. Feel it. Gritty means sand. Smooth and silky means silt. Sticky means clay.
4. Hold the ball between your thumb and first finger. Push it out with your thumb to make a flat ribbon, and let it hang over your finger until it breaks.
5. Measure the ribbon before it breaks.

| Ribbon length | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| No ribbon, falls apart | Sand or loamy sand |
| Less than 2.5 cm | Sandy loam or loam |
| 2.5 to 5 cm | Clay loam |
| More than 5 cm | Clay |

### The jar test

The jar test shows how much sand, silt and clay are in your soil.

1. Take soil from several places in the bed, from the top 15 cm. Mix it and remove stones, roots and leaves.
2. Fill a clear glass jar with straight sides about one-third full with soil.
3. Fill the jar almost to the top with clean water. Add one teaspoon of dishwashing liquid. This helps the particles separate.
4. Close the lid and shake hard for 3 to 5 minutes.
5. Put the jar down where it will not be moved.
6. After **1 minute**, mark the height of the bottom layer on the side of the jar. This is **sand**.
7. After **2 hours**, mark the top of the next layer. This is **silt**.
8. After **1 to 2 days**, when the water is mostly clear, mark the top layer. This is **clay**. Very clay-rich soil can take a week or more to clear.
9. Anything floating on top is organic matter.
10. Measure each layer and compare it with the total height of soil in the jar.

A loam has roughly 40% sand, 40% silt and 20% clay. If more than half the soil in the jar is sand, you have a sandy soil. If the clay layer is thick and the water stays cloudy for days, you have a clay soil.

### Observe the land

When you study your soil, also look around and make notes:

- What colour is the soil? Dark soil usually has more organic matter.
- What plants grow on it naturally? Plants tell a story. For example, sedges and reeds show wet soil.
- What rock is the soil made from? Look for stones such as sandstone, shale, granite or basalt (dark volcanic rock, often found in Lesotho and the Drakensberg).
- What does it smell like? Healthy soil smells sweet and earthy. A sour or rotten smell can mean waterlogging.
- What lives in it? Count the earthworms in a spadeful. Look for beetles, ants and fungi.
- How does it handle water? Does rain soak in quickly, or run off and form puddles? Does the surface form a hard crust?
- How was the land used before? Old kraals, fields or rubbish sites all affect the soil.

> **Safety:** Do not taste soil. Soil can hold animal dung, germs, worm eggs and chemicals. Always wash your hands after handling soil and manure.

### Soil pH: acid or alkaline?

**pH** measures how acid or alkaline (sweet) the soil is. The scale goes from 0 to 14. A pH of 7 is neutral. Below 7 is acid, and above 7 is alkaline.

- Most vegetables grow best in slightly acid to neutral soil, around pH 6 to 7 (measured in water).
- In very acid soil, nutrients such as phosphorus become locked up and aluminium can poison roots.
- In South Africa, many soils in the higher-rainfall east are acid. Soils in the dry west and in low-lying areas can be alkaline or salty.

The best way to know your pH is a soil test. You can buy a simple pH test kit, or send a sample to a soil laboratory. Ask your local agricultural extension office or co-operative where to send it.

> **Note:** South African laboratories often report **pH (KCl)**. This reading is usually about 0.5 to 1 unit lower than pH measured in water. A pH (KCl) below about 4.5 means the soil is too acid for most crops. The lab report will also show "acid saturation" and will recommend how much lime to add.

To correct acid soil, add agricultural lime or dolomitic lime, following the lab's advice. Adding compost helps too, because it buffers the soil against big swings in pH.

## Caring for your soil

- **Keep it covered.** Bare soil bakes in the sun, crusts in the rain and blows away in the wind. Use mulch or living plants.
- **Feed it.** Add compost, manure and mulch regularly so that soil life has food.
- **Return plant material.** Put crop leftovers back on the soil or in the compost heap instead of burning them.
- **Grow many kinds of plants.** Different plants have different roots at different depths, and they feed different soil life.
- **Dig less.** Every time you dig or plough, you break up the soil structure and burn up organic matter. Loosen the soil with a fork instead of turning it over.
- **Do not walk on growing beds**, especially when the soil is wet. Use paths.
- **Let the soil rest.** Grow a green manure or leave a bed under mulch for a season.

There are five main ways to build soil fertility: green manure, animal manure, mulch, compost and worms.

## Way 1: Green manure

A **green manure** is a crop that you grow to feed the soil, not to harvest. You grow it, cut it down before it sets seed, and return it to the soil.

Green manures:

- cover bare soil and protect it from erosion
- add organic matter as they rot
- add nitrogen, if they are **legumes** (plants in the bean and pea family). Bacteria in small lumps (**nodules**) on legume roots take nitrogen from the air and make it available to plants
- break up hard soil with their roots. Many green manures have deep roots that reach down into the subsoil
- smother weeds
- feed bees and other helpful insects when they flower.

### How to use a green manure

1. Sow the green manure thickly in a bed you are not using, or between crops.
2. Let it grow until it starts to flower. This is when it has the most leaf and nitrogen.
3. Cut it down before it sets seed. Chop it into small pieces with a spade or slasher.
4. Either leave it on the surface as mulch (**chop and drop**), or lightly dig it into the top 10 to 15 cm of soil.
5. Wait 3 to 4 weeks before you sow or plant into the bed. This gives the green material time to break down. In cool weather, wait a little longer.

> **Tip:** If you grow a legume in a field for the first time, buy the right **rhizobium inoculant** (a powder of the helpful root bacteria) from your seed supplier and mix it with the seed before sowing. The plant will make more nitrogen.

### Green manures for Southern Africa

| Season | Plant | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Summer | Sunn hemp (*Crotalaria juncea*) | Legume. Grows fast and tall, with deep roots. Lots of leaf. |
| Summer | Cowpea (*Vigna unguiculata*) | Legume. Handles heat and drought. You can also eat the leaves and beans. |
| Summer | Lablab (*Lablab purpureus*) | Legume. Drought-tolerant creeper. Good fodder for animals. |
| Summer | Velvet bean (*Mucuna pruriens*) | Legume. Very heavy cover that smothers weeds. The pods have itchy hairs, so wear gloves. |
| Summer | Buckwheat (*Fagopyrum esculentum*) | Not a legume. Very quick, flowering in about 6 weeks. Bees love it. |
| Winter | Oats (*Avena sativa*) | A cereal. Lots of organic matter. Often sown with vetch. |
| Winter | Vetch (*Vicia* species) | Legume. A good partner for oats. |
| Winter | Lupin (*Lupinus angustifolius*) | Legume with deep roots. Suits sandy, acid soils. Do not eat the seed. |
| Winter | Fodder radish (*Raphanus sativus*) | Long taproot that breaks up hard soil. |

In the summer-rainfall areas, sow summer green manures from about October to January. In the winter-rainfall Western Cape, or where you can irrigate, sow the winter green manures in autumn (March to May).

Some plants that we call weeds, such as clover, are good green manures too. Instead of pulling them out and throwing them away, cut them before they seed and leave them on the soil or add them to the compost.

> **Note:** Some plants sold as "green manure" or "soil builders" overseas are declared invaders in South Africa. Mexican sunflower (*Tithonia diversifolia*), often praised in permaculture books, is a Category 1b invader under NEMBA: it is against the law to plant or sell it. Do not use it. Check any unfamiliar plant with your local extension office before planting it.

## Way 2: Animal manure

Animal manure adds organic matter and plant nutrients, especially nitrogen. It also feeds soil life. Cattle, sheep, goat, horse, rabbit and chicken manure can all be used.

- **Chicken manure** is the strongest. Fresh chicken manure can burn plant roots, so always compost it first.
- **Cattle, goat, sheep, horse and rabbit manure** is milder, but still contains germs when fresh.
- Chickens and other animals in a **chicken tractor** (a movable pen, see the chapter on animal systems) spread their manure directly onto beds that are being rested.

Manure is best **composted** before you use it on vegetables. Composting kills most germs and weed seeds, stops the manure burning plants, and turns it into crumbly **humus** (dark, well-rotted organic matter).

> **Safety: using manure on food crops**
>
> Fresh manure carries germs such as *E. coli* and *Salmonella* that can make people very sick.
>
> **Compost manure before using it** on vegetables, especially leafy greens and root crops that are eaten raw.
>
> If you must use uncomposted manure, dig it into the soil **at least 4 months (120 days) before harvesting** crops that touch the soil or are eaten raw, such as lettuce, spinach, carrots and radishes. For crops that grow off the ground, such as maize and fruit trees, wait at least 3 months (90 days).
>
> Never put fresh manure on or around vegetables that are already growing.
>
> **Never use human faeces, or dog or cat droppings, in the food garden.** Dog and cat droppings can carry worms and other parasites that infect people, especially children. Human waste is only safe after long, careful treatment in a properly managed composting or urine-diversion toilet system; follow official guidance for these.
>
> **Do not use raw pig manure.** It can carry roundworm eggs that infect people and can survive for years. Use pig manure only after thorough hot composting and long storage, and not on crops eaten raw.
>
> Wear gloves or wash your hands well after handling manure. Wash all vegetables before eating.

Also take care where your manure comes from:

- Do not use manure from sick animals.
- Manure from animals that were recently dosed against worms (for example with ivermectin) can harm dung beetles and earthworms. If you can, use manure collected a few weeks after dosing.
- Some long-lasting weedkillers sprayed on grazing land pass through animals unchanged and end up in their manure. Even tiny amounts damage tomatoes, beans, peas, potatoes and lettuce. Composting does not break them down. Ask the supplier whether the animals grazed sprayed pasture or ate hay from sprayed fields.
- Manure can contain weed seeds. A hot compost heap kills most of them.

## Way 3: Mulch

**Mulch** is a blanket of material spread on top of the soil. It is one of the most important things you can do for your soil, and a good way to recycle organic waste. Organic mulches include straw, dry grass, leaves, wood chips, crop leftovers and shredded bark.

Soil, worms and plants all love mulch. Mulch:

- protects the soil from sun, wind and hard rain, so the soil does not crust or erode
- helps rain soak into the soil instead of running off
- greatly reduces evaporation, so the soil stays moist for longer and you water less
- keeps the soil cooler in summer and warmer in winter
- slows down weeds
- feeds soil life as it rots, and slowly turns into humus that adds fertility
- gives a home to many helpful creatures.

### How to mulch

1. Water the bed well first, or mulch after good rain.
2. Spread organic mulch **5 to 10 cm deep**. Use a thinner layer of fine material such as grass clippings, which can pack down into a wet mat.
3. Keep mulch about 5 cm away from the stems of vegetables and about 10 cm away from tree trunks. Mulch piled against stems keeps them wet and can cause rot.
4. Top up the mulch as it breaks down.

> **Tip:** Dry woody mulch such as wood chips or sawdust uses nitrogen from the soil surface as it rots. Use it on paths and around trees rather than on vegetable seedlings, or put a thin layer of manure or compost underneath it.

### Sheet mulching

Sheet mulching turns grass or weeds into a new garden bed without digging.

1. Cut down the grass or weeds and leave them on the ground.
2. Water the area well.
3. Spread a thin layer of manure or compost.
4. Cover with flat cardboard or thick layers of newspaper, overlapping by 15 cm so that no light gets through. Remove plastic tape and staples. Do not use glossy or coloured printed paper.
5. Wet the cardboard well.
6. Cover with 10 cm or more of mulch such as straw, leaves or dry grass.
7. To plant, push a spade through the mulch and cut a hole in the cardboard. Fill the hole with a few handfuls of compost and plant your seedling, shrub or tuber into it. Water well.

The cardboard smothers the grass and slowly rots. Worms move in underneath and loosen the soil.

> **Warning:** Slugs and snails love the dark, moist conditions under mulch. They come out at night and can quickly eat young seedlings. Check under the mulch, pick them off by hand in the evening, and let ducks or chickens hunt them (but keep chickens out of beds with small seedlings, because they scratch). Keep mulch a little back from young seedlings.

### Mulch banks

A **mulch bank** is an area planted with fast-growing plants that you cut regularly for mulch and compost. It is very useful where soil is poor or eroded and there is not enough mulch material. A mulch bank can also:

- act as a windbreak
- provide a home for helpful insects
- hold soil and water on a slope
- give you plenty of material for mulch and compost.

Good plants for a mulch bank include indigenous grasses such as thatch grass (*Hyparrhenia hirta*), red grass (*Themeda triandra*) and weeping love grass (*Eragrostis curvula*); sterile, non-invasive vetiver grass (*Chrysopogon zizanioides*) for contour strips on slopes; pigeon pea (*Cajanus cajan*); and indigenous shrubs that grow back after cutting, such as Cape honeysuckle (*Tecoma capensis*) and sand olive (*Dodonaea viscosa*).

Many books say that deep-rooted "dynamic accumulator" plants pull up special minerals for your compost. There is little scientific evidence for this. The real value of a mulch bank is the large amount of organic material it produces.

## Way 4: Compost

**Compost** is a mix of organic materials that have been piled together and left to rot until they become dark, crumbly and sweet-smelling. Making compost copies what happens on a forest floor, but faster.

Compost:

- improves soil structure in both sandy and clay soils
- helps soil hold water
- feeds plants slowly and naturally
- feeds soil life
- can reduce some plant diseases
- recycles kitchen and garden waste instead of burning or dumping it.

### What compost needs

Compost needs four things: **browns**, **greens**, **air** and **water**. And then it needs **time**.

**Browns** are dry, carbon-rich materials. **Greens** are fresh, nitrogen-rich materials. The micro-organisms that make compost need both. For a hot, fast heap, aim for about 25 to 30 parts carbon to 1 part nitrogen. In practice, this means roughly **2 to 3 buckets of browns for every 1 bucket of greens**.

| Browns (carbon) | Greens (nitrogen) |
|---|---|
| Straw and dry grass | Fresh grass clippings |
| Dry leaves | Green weeds (without seeds) |
| Maize stalks, chopped | Vegetable and fruit peels |
| Shredded cardboard and newspaper | Animal manure |
| Wood chips and sawdust (a little) | Green leaves and green manure crops |

- Too many greens: the heap gets wet, slimy and smells of ammonia. Add more browns.
- Too many browns: the heap stays cool and rots very slowly. Add more greens or manure.
- Too dry: nothing happens. Add water.
- Too wet: the heap smells rotten because air cannot get in. Turn it and add dry browns.

**Air.** The micro-organisms need oxygen. Turning the heap lets air in. A layer of sticks at the bottom, or a "chimney" of sticks pushed into the heap, also helps.

**Water.** Keep the heap about as damp as a wrung-out sponge. If you squeeze a handful, only a drop or two of water should come out.

> **Warning: do not put these in your compost**
>
> Dog and cat droppings, or human faeces.
>
> Meat, fish, bones, fat, oil and dairy products. They smell and attract rats and flies.
>
> Cooked food in large amounts, for the same reason.
>
> Plants with serious diseases, and weeds that have gone to seed, unless your heap gets properly hot.
>
> Roots of spreading grasses such as couch grass. Drown them in a bucket of water for a few weeks first.
>
> Invasive plants with seeds, which you may spread around your farm.
>
> Treated, painted or varnished wood, coal ash and ash from briquettes. Small amounts of plain wood ash are fine.
>
> Plastic, glass, metal and glossy paper.

### How to build a hot compost heap

A **hot compost heap** heats up to 55 to 65 °C. This heat kills most germs and weed seeds and makes compost fast. To heat up, the heap must be big enough: at least 1 m wide, 1 m long and 1 m high, and not more than about 1.5 m high, so that you can still turn it.

1. Choose a shady, level spot close to the garden and to water. Build the heap directly on the soil so that worms and soil life can move in.
2. Lay down about 20 cm of coarse material such as twigs, small branches or maize stalks. This lets air in and water drain out.
3. Add a layer of browns, about 10 to 15 cm deep.
4. Add a layer of greens, such as fresh grass, weeds and kitchen scraps, about 5 to 10 cm deep. Put kitchen scraps in the middle of the heap, not on the edges, to keep flies and rats away.
5. Add a thin layer of manure, about 5 cm.
6. Sprinkle a handful or two of garden soil or old compost. This adds the micro-organisms that start the rotting.
7. Water the layers as you go, until they are damp but not soaking.
8. Repeat steps 3 to 7 until the heap is 1 to 1.5 m high.
9. Cover the heap with a layer of straw, then with old sacks, a tarpaulin or plastic sheeting. This keeps moisture in and stops heavy rain from soaking the heap. Leave the sides a little open so that air can get in.

You can add effective micro-organism products to the water if you have them, but a handful of old compost or good soil works well too.

### Looking after the heap

- The heap should heat up within 2 to 4 days.
- Check the heat by pushing a metal rod or a sharpened stick into the middle. Leave it for 5 minutes, then pull it out and feel it. If it is too hot to hold comfortably, the heap is working.
- The heap stays hot for about a week, then starts to cool. **Turn it when it starts to cool,** usually after 5 to 7 days.
- To turn it, use a fork to move the heap to a new spot next to it. Put the outside material in the middle and the middle material on the outside, so that every part gets hot. Add water if it is dry.
- The heap will heat up again. Keep turning it each time it cools, about once a week for the first month. Turning 4 or 5 times while it is hot is enough to kill most germs and weed seeds.
- If the heap gets hotter than about 70 °C, turn it or add water to cool it down. Too much heat kills the helpful micro-organisms.
- Once you have built the heap, do not keep adding fresh material. Start a second heap for new waste.

> **Safety:** The centre of a hot heap can be hot enough to scald. Wear gloves and closed shoes when you turn compost, and wash your hands afterwards.

### When is compost ready?

- A well-managed hot heap is usually ready in **2 to 3 months** in warm weather. It takes longer in winter. A heap that is never turned (a "cold" heap) can take 6 to 12 months.
- The heap will shrink by about a third to a half as it rots.
- Ready compost is dark brown, crumbly and smells like a forest floor. You can no longer recognise what went into it, except perhaps a few woody bits.
- If the heap is still warm when you turn it, it is not ready.

Do not use compost that is not ready. Half-rotted compost takes nitrogen from the soil to finish breaking down, and your plants will turn yellow and grow slowly.

When you add red wiggler worms (see below) to a cooling heap, they finish the job and make the compost even richer. Add them only once the heap has cooled, or the heat will kill them.

Use ready compost within a few months. Keep it covered, so that rain does not wash out the nutrients and sun does not dry it out.

## Way 5: Earthworms and worm farming

> "It may be doubted whether there are many other animals which have played so important a part in the history of the world, as have these lowly organised creatures." Charles Darwin, writing about earthworms in 1881.

### Earthworms in your soil

Earthworms are excellent helpers in the garden. They:

- pull dead leaves and mulch down into the soil
- dig tunnels that let air and water into the soil and help roots grow deeper
- eat soil and organic matter, and leave behind **castings** (worm droppings). Castings bind the soil into the small crumbs that are the sign of healthy soil
- make nutrients easier for plants to use.

Many earthworms in a spadeful of soil is a sign that your soil is healthy. Encourage them by keeping the soil covered with mulch, adding compost, digging less and not using poisons.

### Composting worms

For a worm farm you need special **composting worms**. These worms live in rotting leaves, manure and compost, not deep in the soil. They eat a lot, breed fast and are happy in a crowded box.

- **Red wiggler** (*Eisenia fetida*) is the most common composting worm in South Africa. It copes best with cold and heat.
- **African nightcrawler** (*Eudrilus eugeniae*) is bigger and works fast in warm areas, but does poorly in cold winters.
- **Indian blue worm** (*Perionyx excavatus*) is also used in warm areas.

Buy composting worms from a worm farm, nursery or organic grower. **Do not use earthworms dug from your garden.** Garden earthworms live in the soil and will not survive or breed well in a worm farm.

**Vermicomposting** means using worms to turn organic waste into compost. Worm farming is popular with small-scale and organic farmers, and some farmers sell worms, castings and worm liquid.

### What you get from a worm farm

- **Worm castings** (vermicompost). A fine, dark, rich compost full of nutrients and soil life. Castings have more plant-available nutrients than the soil or scraps they came from. Use them as a top-dressing around plants, mix them into seedling soil, or add a handful to each planting hole.
- **Worm farm liquid.** Many people call this "worm wee" or "worm tea", but it is really water that has drained through the farm and picked up nutrients from the castings and rotting food. It is a useful liquid feed.

> **Tip:** Dilute worm farm liquid with about 10 parts water, until it looks like weak tea. Pour it onto the soil around plants. If the liquid smells bad or rotten, your farm is too wet. Use that liquid only on the compost heap or on flowers and trees, not on vegetables.

### Types of worm farm

A worm farm is simply a safe, shady, well-drained home for worms, protected from chickens, birds, ants and moles. Use what you have.

- **Old bath or drinking trough.** Raise it on bricks, a little higher at one end, with the plughole at the lower end so that liquid can drain into a bucket. Put a layer of stones at the bottom, then shade cloth on top of the stones. Add bedding and worms. Cover with an old blanket or hessian sack and a sheet of roofing to keep off sun and rain.
- **Stacked crates or containers.** Stack three plastic crates, buckets or boxes of the same size. This is the easiest system for harvesting.
- **Stacked tyres.** Stack tyres on a solid base, such as a concrete slab or a sheet of metal on bricks, with a way for liquid to drain out. Use them like crates, and always put a lid on top. Black tyres get very hot in the sun, so keep them in full shade.

### What worms need

- **Shade.** Worms die quickly in direct sun and heat. Red wigglers do best between about 15 and 25 °C. Above about 30 to 35 °C they die. In hot weather, keep the farm in deep shade and add a wet sack on top. In cold areas, move the farm to a sheltered spot in winter.
- **Moisture.** Worms breathe through their skin, so their bedding must always be damp, like a wrung-out sponge. But they will drown if it is too wet.
- **Air.** Make air holes in the sides and lid of the farm.
- **Darkness.** Worms avoid light. Keep the farm covered.
- **Bedding.** A home to live in, such as shredded cardboard, newspaper, dry leaves, straw and well-rotted manure.
- **Food.** Small amounts of kitchen and garden scraps, added regularly.
- **Drainage.** Holes to let extra liquid out.

### How to build a stacking worm farm

1. Find three plastic crates, boxes or buckets of the same size, about 20 to 30 cm deep. Dark-coloured containers are best.
2. The **bottom container** collects the liquid. Do not make holes in its base. If you can, fit a small tap near the bottom, or stand it over a basin.
3. In the **two upper containers**, drill or punch many small holes (about 5 mm) in the base, so that liquid can drain down and worms can move up. Make a few small air holes near the top of the sides.
4. Stand the stack on bricks in a shady place.
5. Put the bottom container down. Place the first working container on top of it.
6. Fill the working container about two-thirds full with **bedding**: shredded cardboard or newspaper, dry leaves and straw that have been soaked and squeezed out, plus some well-rotted manure and a handful of garden soil or compost. Mix it well.
7. Add your worms. Start with at least 250 to 500 g of worms (several hundred to about a thousand). With only a handful, the farm will take many months to get going.
8. Cover the bedding with a damp sack, cardboard or a thick layer of newspaper, then close the lid or cover with shade cloth to keep out birds and chickens.
9. Wait a few days, then start feeding (see below).
10. When the working container is full of dark castings, usually after 2 to 4 months, place the third container on top. Put fresh bedding and food in it. It must touch the castings below.
11. Over the next few weeks, the worms will move up into the new container to find food.
12. Take out the lower container, now mostly castings. Pick out any worms you see and put them back. Use the castings in your garden.
13. Empty the container, and use it as the new top container next time. Keep swapping in this way.
14. Drain the liquid from the bottom container every few days. Do not let it fill up and flood the worms.

As the worms breed, you will find small, lemon-shaped **cocoons** (worm eggs) in the bedding. Each one hatches into several baby worms. Once your farm is full of worms, you can take out a few handfuls of worms and bedding to start a new worm farm or to add to a cooling compost heap. Always leave plenty behind.

### Feeding your worms

- Start slowly. Add a small amount of food, and add more only when most of the last lot has been eaten.
- Once the farm is well established, worms can eat up to about half their own weight in food a day. For example, 1 kg of worms can eat up to about 500 g of scraps a day.
- Chop or crush food into small pieces. It breaks down faster.
- Bury the food under the bedding, in a different corner each time. This keeps fruit flies away.
- Add a handful of dry bedding (shredded paper or dry leaves) each time you feed, to keep the balance right.

| Worms like | Worms do not like |
|---|---|
| Vegetable and fruit peels | Onions, garlic and chillies |
| Tea leaves and coffee grounds | Lots of citrus peel |
| Crushed eggshells | Meat, fish, bones and dairy |
| Soaked cardboard and paper | Oily, salty or cooked food |
| Well-rotted manure from cattle, horses or rabbits | Dog and cat droppings |
| Leaves and soft plant material | Too much food at once |
| Stale bread, in small amounts | Too much water |

Worms also dislike direct sun, heat, frost and being disturbed too often.

### Compost tea

Some farmers soak worm castings or compost in water, pump air into it with an aquarium pump, and add a little molasses. The molasses feeds the micro-organisms (not the worms). The result is called **compost tea**.

Research on whether compost tea helps plants is mixed. Plain diluted worm farm liquid, or castings dug into the soil, are simpler and work well.

> **Safety:** Adding molasses or sugar to compost tea can make harmful bacteria such as *E. coli* and *Salmonella* grow, especially if the compost contained manure. Do not spray compost tea or worm liquid on the leaves of vegetables that will be eaten raw, and never close to harvest. Pour it on the soil instead.

## Key points

- Soil is alive and forms very slowly. Protect it: keep it covered, dig less and never leave it bare.
- Learn your soil texture with the feel test and the jar test. Sandy and clay soils are both improved by adding organic matter.
- Most vegetables grow best at a pH of about 6 to 7. A soil test tells you whether you need lime.
- Grow green manures, especially legumes, and cut them before they seed. Never plant a listed invader such as Mexican sunflower.
- Compost manure before using it on food crops. Leave at least 4 months between applying raw manure and harvesting crops eaten raw. Never use human, dog or cat faeces, or raw pig manure, in the food garden.
- Mulch 5 to 10 cm deep, keeping it away from stems.
- A hot compost heap needs browns, greens, air and water, reaches 55 to 65 °C, is turned when it cools, and is ready in about 2 to 3 months.
- For a worm farm, use composting worms such as red wigglers (*Eisenia fetida*), not garden earthworms. Keep them shaded, damp and fed with small amounts of vegetable scraps.
- Dilute worm farm liquid about 1 to 10 and pour it on the soil, not on leafy greens eaten raw.
