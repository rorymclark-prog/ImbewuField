# Soil: feed the soil, and it will feed you

Soil is the beginning of everything. It is not dirt. It is a living ecosystem, and every meal we eat started in it.

Soil forms slowly. Sun, rain, frost and roots crack rock into small pieces. Plants, animals, fungi and countless tiny living things (**micro-organisms**) turn dead leaves, roots and dung into **organic matter**, which means anything in the soil that was once alive. It can take up to 1 000 years to build 1 cm of soil. One storm can wash that centimetre away.

So we turn the usual question around. We do not ask what the plant needs. We ask what the soil needs. Feed the soil, not the plant.

**By the end of this chapter you will be able to:**

- Read the layers of your soil and tell sand, silt, clay and loam apart with your hands and a glass jar
- Read a pH result and know when your soil needs lime
- Grow a green manure and use animal manure without making anyone sick
- Mulch a bed, and turn grass into a new bed without digging
- Build a hot compost heap and a worm farm that work

## The layers of the soil

A hole in the ground is a history book. Dig one, or look at a road cutting or gully, and you will see layers: the **soil profile**.

| Layer | What you see |
|---|---|
| Leaf litter | Dead leaves on the surface, starting to rot. |
| **Topsoil** | The darkest layer, full of roots, soil life and organic matter. Often only 10 to 30 cm deep. |
| Subsoil | Lighter, with less organic matter. Clay, iron and minerals washed down from above collect here. |
| Weathered rock | Broken parent rock mixed with a little soil. |
| Bedrock | Solid rock. |

Topsoil is where fertility lives. A deep, dark topsoil holds water well, because its organic matter and crumbly structure leave small spaces for air and water. Trees and plants with a **taproot** (one thick root that grows straight down) reach into the subsoil for water and minerals, and when their leaves fall and rot, some of those minerals come back to the surface.

Topsoil is also the first thing lost to **erosion**, the washing or blowing away of soil by water and wind. Keep it covered and fed, and the dark layer deepens.

## Soil texture: sand, silt, clay and loam

Texture decides how fast water drains through your soil and how much plant food it can hold. Soil is made of mineral particles of three sizes: gritty **sand** is the biggest, floury **silt** is medium, and sticky **clay** is the smallest. The mix of the three is the **soil texture**.

| Soil | In your hand | With water |
|---|---|---|
| Sandy | Gritty and loose. Falls apart. | Drains fast and dries out. Nutrients wash away. |
| Clay | Sticky when wet, hard when dry. Rolls into a long, thin sausage. | Holds water and nutrients but drains slowly. Can waterlog, then cracks and crusts. |
| Loam | Dark, soft and crumbly. | Drains well and still holds enough water and food. |

Clay can be red, brown, yellow, grey or black. Grey or blue-grey patches usually mean the soil stays wet for long periods.

Loam is the best soil for most vegetables. You cannot change your texture, but you can change your soil's **structure** (how the particles clump into crumbs) and how much organic matter it holds. Organic matter is the answer for both problem soils: it helps sand hold water like a sponge, and opens up clay so air and water can move. Fed like this year after year, sandy and clay soils both move towards loam.

## Testing your soil

Most smallholders have a feeling about their soil. The feeling is often wrong.

### The feel test (ribbon test)

1. Take a small handful of soil from about 10 cm deep. Remove stones and roots.
2. Add water a little at a time and knead until the soil is like putty and just stops sticking to your hand.
3. Feel it. Gritty means sand. Smooth and silky means silt. Sticky means clay.
4. Push the ball out between your thumb and first finger into a flat ribbon, and let it hang until it breaks.
5. Measure the ribbon.

| Ribbon length | Your soil |
|---|---|
| No ribbon, falls apart | Sand or loamy sand |
| Less than 2.5 cm | Sandy loam or loam |
| 2.5 to 5 cm | Clay loam |
| More than 5 cm | Clay |

### The jar test

1. Take soil from several places in the bed, from the top 15 cm. Mix it and remove stones, roots and leaves.
2. Fill a clear, straight-sided jar one-third with soil, almost to the brim with water, and add a teaspoon of dishwashing liquid to help the particles separate.
3. Shake hard for 3 to 5 minutes and set the jar where nobody will move it.
4. After **1 minute**, mark the top of the bottom layer: this is **sand**.
5. After **2 hours**, mark the top of the next layer: **silt**.
6. After **1 to 2 days**, when the water is mostly clear, mark the top layer: **clay**. Heavy clay can take a week or more. Anything floating is organic matter.
7. Compare each layer with the total height of soil in the jar.

A loam is roughly 40% sand, 40% silt and 20% clay. More than half sand means a sandy soil; a thick clay layer and water that stays cloudy for days mean a clay soil.

### Read the land

While you dig, write down what you see. Darker soil usually holds more organic matter. Sedges and reeds show wet ground. Stones such as sandstone, shale or basalt (the dark volcanic rock of Lesotho and the Drakensberg) tell you what the soil was made from. Healthy soil smells sweet and earthy; a sour smell can mean waterlogging. Count the earthworms in a spadeful. Watch whether rain soaks in or runs off. And ask what the land was used for before: old kraals, fields and rubbish sites all change the soil.

### Soil pH: acid or sweet?

**pH** measures how acid or alkaline (sweet) the soil is, on a scale from 0 to 14. A pH of 7 is neutral, below 7 is acid and above 7 is alkaline.

- Most vegetables grow best between about pH 6 and 7, measured in water.
- In very acid soil, phosphorus is locked up and aluminium poisons roots.
- Many soils in the wetter east of South Africa are acid. Soils in the dry west and in low-lying areas can be alkaline or salty.

A cheap pH kit gives a rough reading; a soil laboratory gives an exact one and the lime you need. South African laboratories often report **pH (KCl)**, which reads about 0.5 to 1 unit lower than pH in water. A pH (KCl) below about 4.5 is too acid for most crops. Spread agricultural or dolomitic lime at the rate on the report. Organic matter helps buffer pH too.

## Caring for your soil

Nature never leaves soil bare. The floor of a forest or of healthy veld is always covered, always fed, and never dug. We copy that.

- **Keep it covered** with **mulch** (a blanket of dry grass, straw or leaves) or living plants. Bare soil bakes, crusts and blows away.
- **Feed it.** Crop leftovers, **compost** (rotted plant and animal waste) and manure go back on the soil, never onto the fire.
- **Grow many kinds of plants.** Different roots reach different depths and feed different soil life.
- **Dig less.** Every dig or ploughing breaks the soil's structure and burns up organic matter. Loosen with a fork instead.
- **Stay off the beds**, especially when wet. Walk on paths.
- **Let it rest.** Give a tired bed a season under green manure or mulch.

Covered, undisturbed soil gets deeper and richer every year. There are five main ways to feed it: green manure, animal manure, mulch, compost and worms.

## Way 1: Green manure

Plants can make fertility right where you stand, out of sun, air and rain. A **green manure** is a crop you grow to feed the soil, not to harvest.

A green manure covers bare soil, smothers weeds, feeds bees when it flowers, and adds organic matter as it rots. Many green manures root deep and break up hard ground. If it is a **legume** (a plant of the bean and pea family), it also adds nitrogen: bacteria in small lumps on its roots, called **nodules**, take nitrogen from the air. That makes the legume a **nitrogen fixer**.

### How to use a green manure

1. Sow it thickly in a resting bed, or between crops.
2. Let it grow until it starts to flower, when it has the most leaf and nitrogen.
3. Cut it before it sets seed, and chop it into short pieces with a spade or slasher.
4. Leave it on the surface as mulch (**chop and drop**), or dig it lightly into the top 10 to 15 cm.
5. Wait 3 to 4 weeks before you plant into the bed, a little longer in cool weather.

> **Tip:** The first time you grow a legume in a field, buy the matching **rhizobium inoculant** (a powder of the helpful root bacteria) from your seed supplier and mix it with the seed. The plants will fix more nitrogen.

### Green manures for Southern Africa

| Season | Plant | Why grow it |
|---|---|---|
| Summer | Sunn hemp (*Crotalaria juncea*) | Legume. Fast and tall, with deep roots. |
| Summer | Cowpea (*Vigna unguiculata*) | Legume. Handles heat and drought, and you can eat the leaves and beans. |
| Summer | Lablab (*Lablab purpureus*) | Legume. Drought-tolerant creeper and good fodder. |
| Summer | Buckwheat (*Fagopyrum esculentum*) | Flowers in about 6 weeks. Bees love it. |
| Winter | Oats (*Avena sativa*) with vetch (*Vicia* species) | Oats give bulk; vetch, a legume, adds nitrogen. |
| Winter | Fodder radish (*Raphanus sativus*) | Long taproot that breaks hard soil. |

In summer-rainfall areas, sow the summer ones once the rains have started and the soil is warm. In the winter-rainfall Western Cape, or wherever you can irrigate, sow the winter ones in autumn. Mexican sunflower (*Tithonia diversifolia*), praised in many permaculture books, is an invader in South Africa; grow sunn hemp or lablab for bulk instead.

## Way 2: Animal manure

Every animal on the farm is a small fertiliser factory. Manure adds organic matter and nutrients, especially nitrogen, and feeds soil life. Cattle, sheep, goat, horse, rabbit and chicken manure all work; chicken manure is the strongest, and burns roots when fresh. An **animal tractor**, a movable pen of chickens standing on a resting bed, spreads manure straight onto the soil (see the chapter on animal systems).

### How to use manure

1. Compost manure before it goes near crops you eat raw. Composting kills most germs and weed seeds, stops the manure burning roots, and turns it into **humus**: the dark, stable part of organic matter that holds water and nutrients.
2. If you must use raw manure, dig it in at least 4 months (120 days) before you harvest crops that touch the soil or are eaten raw, such as lettuce, spinach and carrots. For crops that grow off the ground, such as mielies and fruit trees, allow at least 3 months (90 days). Never put fresh manure around vegetables that are already growing.
3. Never use human faeces, or dog or cat droppings, in the food garden. They carry worms and other parasites that infect people, children most of all.
4. Use pig manure only after thorough hot composting and long storage, and never on crops eaten raw. Its roundworm eggs survive for years.
5. Wash your hands after handling manure, and wash vegetables before you eat them.

Know where your manure comes from. Skip manure from sick animals, and dung from animals dosed against worms in the last few weeks (with ivermectin, for example), which harms dung beetles and earthworms. Some long-lasting weedkillers sprayed on grazing pass through animals and survive composting, and tiny amounts damage tomatoes, beans and potatoes, so ask whether the animals grazed sprayed pasture.

## Way 3: Mulch

Walk into healthy veld and push the grass aside. The soil underneath is dark, damp and cool. Mulch does the same job in the garden: a blanket of dry grass, straw, leaves, wood chips or crop leftovers spread on top of the soil. Of all the ways to care for soil, start here.

Mulch shields the soil from sun, wind and hard rain, helps rain soak in, greatly cuts evaporation so you water less, keeps the soil cooler in summer and warmer in winter, and slows weeds. As it rots, it feeds soil life and becomes humus.

### How to mulch

1. Water the bed well first, or mulch straight after good rain.
2. Spread the mulch **5 to 10 cm deep**. Use a thinner layer of fine material such as grass clippings, which packs into a wet mat.
3. Keep mulch about 5 cm away from vegetable stems and 10 cm from tree trunks, or the stems stay wet and rot.
4. Top it up as it breaks down.

Woody mulch such as wood chips or sawdust takes nitrogen from the soil surface while it rots. Put it on paths and around trees, or lay a thin layer of manure or compost underneath it.

> **Tip:** Slugs and snails love the damp dark under mulch and eat seedlings at night. Pick them off by torchlight in the evening, keep mulch a hand's width back from small seedlings, and let ducks or chickens hunt the beds once the plants are too big to be scratched out.

### Sheet mulching

Sheet mulching turns grass or weeds into a new garden bed without digging.

1. Cut the grass or weeds and leave them lying. Water well, and spread a thin layer of manure or compost over the cut grass.
2. Cover it with flat cardboard or thick newspaper, overlapping by 15 cm so no light gets through. Take off tape and staples, and leave out glossy or colour-printed paper.
3. Soak the cardboard, then cover it with 10 cm or more of straw, leaves or dry grass.
4. To plant, push a spade through the mulch and cut a hole in the cardboard. Fill it with a few handfuls of compost, plant your seedling, shrub or tuber, and water well.

The cardboard smothers the grass and slowly rots. Worms move in underneath and loosen the soil for you.

### Mulch banks

Where mulch is scarce, grow your own. A **mulch bank** is a patch of fast-growing plants you cut again and again for **biomass**, the bulk plant material that becomes mulch and compost. It also breaks the wind and holds soil on a slope.

Choose plants that are **indigenous** (naturally at home here) or known not to spread: thatch grass (*Hyparrhenia hirta*), red grass (*Themeda triandra*) and weeping love grass (*Eragrostis curvula*); sterile vetiver grass (*Chrysopogon zizanioides*) for contour strips; pigeon pea (*Cajanus cajan*); and shrubs that grow back after cutting, such as Cape honeysuckle (*Tecoma capensis*) and sand olive (*Dodonaea viscosa*).

## Way 4: Compost

A forest floor makes compost slowly, all the time. A good heap does it much faster. Compost is dark, crumbly and sweet-smelling. It improves both sandy and clay soils, holds water, feeds plants slowly, and turns waste into fertility instead of smoke.

### What compost needs

Compost needs **browns**, **greens**, **air** and **water**. Then it needs **time**.

Browns are dry and rich in carbon. Greens are fresh and rich in nitrogen. The balance between them is the **carbon to nitrogen ratio**. For a hot heap, aim for about 25 to 30 parts carbon to 1 part nitrogen, which in practice is roughly **2 to 3 buckets of browns for every bucket of greens**.

| Browns (carbon) | Greens (nitrogen) |
|---|---|
| Straw and dry grass | Fresh grass clippings |
| Dry leaves | Green weeds without seeds |
| Chopped mielie stalks | Vegetable and fruit peels |
| Shredded cardboard and newspaper | Animal manure |

Slimy and smelling of ammonia: add browns. Staying cool: add greens or manure. Smelling rotten: it is too wet, so turn it and add dry browns.

**Air.** Compost life needs oxygen. Turning lets air in, and so does a base of sticks.

**Water.** Keep the heap as damp as a wrung-out sponge. Squeeze a handful: only a drop or two should come out.

Keep out dog and cat droppings and human faeces; meat, fish, bones and dairy, which bring rats; diseased plants and seeding weeds, unless the heap gets properly hot; couch-grass roots (drown them in a bucket for a few weeks first); and treated wood, coal ash and plastic. A little plain wood ash is fine.

### How to build a hot compost heap

A **hot compost heap** reaches 55 to 65 °C, hot enough to kill most germs and weed seeds. To get that hot it must be at least 1 m wide, long and high, and no more than about 1.5 m high so you can still turn it.

1. Choose a shady, level spot near the garden and water. Build straight onto the soil so worms can move in.
2. Lay down about 20 cm of twigs, small branches or mielie stalks, to let air in and water out.
3. Add 10 to 15 cm of browns.
4. Add 5 to 10 cm of greens. Bury kitchen scraps in the middle, away from the edges, to keep flies and rats off.
5. Add a thin layer of manure, about 5 cm.
6. Sprinkle a handful of garden soil or old compost to bring in the micro-organisms.
7. Water each layer until it is damp but not soaking.
8. Repeat steps 3 to 7 until the heap is 1 to 1.5 m high.
9. Cover it with straw and then old sacks or a tarpaulin to keep heavy rain out, leaving the sides a little open for air.

### Looking after the heap

1. Within 2 to 4 days the heap should be hot. Push a metal rod into the middle for 5 minutes; if it is too hot to hold comfortably when you pull it out, the heap is working.
2. After 5 to 7 days it starts to cool. **Turn it now.** Fork the whole heap into a new pile beside it, outside to the middle and middle to the outside, adding water if it is dry.
3. It heats up again. Turn it each time it cools, about weekly for the first month. Four or five hot turns kill most germs and weed seeds.
4. If it goes above about 70 °C, turn it or add water. Too much heat kills the helpful micro-organisms.
5. Stop adding fresh material. Start a second heap for new waste.

A well-turned hot heap is ready in a few months in warm weather; a heap that is never turned can take 6 months to a year or more. It shrinks by a third to a half. Ready compost is dark, crumbly and smells like a forest floor. If it is still warm when you turn it, wait: half-rotted compost takes nitrogen from the soil, and your plants go yellow. Once the heap has cooled, red wigglers (see Way 5) will finish it. Keep ready compost covered and use it within a few months.

## Way 5: Earthworms and worm farming

Charles Darwin spent years watching earthworms. In 1881 he wrote: "It may be doubted whether there are many other animals which have played so important a part in the history of the world, as have these lowly organised creatures."

### Earthworms in your soil

Earthworms are the garden's quiet workforce. They pull dead leaves down into the soil, their tunnels let in air and water, and their **castings** (worm droppings) bind soil into the small crumbs that mark healthy soil. To bring more, mulch, add compost, dig less and use no poisons.

### Composting worms

A **worm farm** is a container in which special composting worms turn kitchen scraps into rich castings. These worms live in rotting leaves and manure, not deep in the soil, and they breed fast in a crowded box. The **red wiggler** (*Eisenia fetida*) is the most common in South Africa and copes best with cold and heat. The **African nightcrawler** (*Eudrilus eugeniae*) and **Indian blue worm** (*Perionyx excavatus*) work well in warm areas but do poorly in cold winters.

Buy them from a worm farm or nursery. Garden earthworms will not survive in a worm farm.

A worm farm gives you two things:

- **Worm castings**: a fine, dark compost with more plant-available nutrients than the scraps or soil they came from. Use it as a top-dressing, in seedling mix, or a handful per planting hole.
- **Worm tea**: the liquid that drains out of the farm. Dilute it with about 10 parts water, until it looks like weak tea, and pour it on the soil around plants. If it smells bad, the farm is too wet; give that liquid to the compost heap or the trees, not to vegetables.

### Building and running a worm farm

A worm farm is simply a shady, damp, dark, well-drained home, safe from chickens, birds and ants. An old bath on bricks works, plughole at the low end over a bucket, stones and shade cloth in the bottom, and a hessian sack and roofing sheet on top. The easiest to harvest is a stack of three crates or buckets:

1. Find three dark containers of the same size, about 20 to 30 cm deep.
2. Leave the base of the **bottom container** solid: it collects the liquid. Fit a small tap, or stand it over a basin.
3. Punch many holes of about 5 mm in the bases of the **two upper containers**, and a few air holes near the top of their sides.
4. Stand the bottom container on bricks in the shade, with one working container on top.
5. Fill the working container two-thirds full of **bedding**: shredded cardboard, newspaper, dry leaves and straw, soaked and squeezed out, with some well-rotted manure and a handful of soil.
6. Add about 500 g of worms, roughly a thousand. A handful takes many months to get going.
7. Cover with a damp sack and a lid. Start feeding after a few days.
8. When the working container is full of dark castings, usually after 2 to 4 months, put the third container on top with fresh bedding and food, touching the castings below.
9. Over a few weeks the worms move up. Take out the lower container, return any worms, and use the castings. The empty container becomes the next top one.
10. Drain the bottom container every few days so it never floods the worms.

Red wigglers do best between about 15 and 25 °C, and above about 30 °C they start to die. Keep the farm in deep shade with a wet sack on top in summer, sheltered in a cold winter, and the bedding as damp as a wrung-out sponge.

When the farm is full of worms and small, lemon-shaped **cocoons** (worm eggs), take out a few handfuls to start another farm or to add to a cooled compost heap.

### Feeding your worms

1. Start slowly. Add more only when most of the last meal has gone.
2. Chop or crush the food so it breaks down faster.
3. Bury it under the bedding, in a different corner each time, to keep fruit flies away.
4. Add a handful of dry bedding with each feed.

An established farm eats up to about half its own weight in food a day: 1 kg of worms, up to about 500 g of scraps.

| Worms like | Worms do not like |
|---|---|
| Vegetable and fruit peels | Onions, garlic, chillies and lots of citrus |
| Tea leaves, coffee grounds and crushed eggshells | Meat, fish, bones and dairy |
| Soaked cardboard and paper | Oily, salty or cooked food |
| Well-rotted cattle, horse or rabbit manure | Dog and cat droppings |

Some farmers brew **compost tea**, bubbling air through castings in water with a little molasses. Research on whether it helps is mixed, and the molasses can feed germs such as *E. coli* and *Salmonella*. Plain worm tea is simpler. Pour either on the soil, never over vegetables eaten raw.

## Try it

Read your own soil this week.

1. Choose three spots: your best bed, your worst bed and a patch of undisturbed veld.
2. At each, dig a hole one spade deep. Measure the dark topsoil and count the earthworms in the spadeful.
3. Do the ribbon test on a handful from 10 cm down.
4. Pour a bucket of water beside each hole. Does it soak in or run off?
5. Start a jar test with soil from your main bed and read it over the next two days.
6. Write it all down with the date. Mulch your worst bed, and test it again at the same time next year.

## Key points

- Soil is alive and forms very slowly. Keep it covered, feed it and dig less.
- Organic matter improves both sand and clay. Most vegetables like a pH of about 6 to 7.
- Cut green manures before they seed. Compost manure before it goes near crops eaten raw.
- Mulch 5 to 10 cm deep, clear of stems.
- A hot heap needs browns, greens, air and water. Worms need shade, damp bedding and small meals.

Dig a spadeful in the same place every year. The soil keeps its own record, centimetre by centimetre, of whether your care is working.
