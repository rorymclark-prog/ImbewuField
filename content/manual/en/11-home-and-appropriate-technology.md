# The Sustainable Home: Zone 0 and Appropriate Technology

Every farm has a centre, and it is the kitchen. That is where the fuel, the water and much of the money go. A family can grow plenty of food and still be poor in energy: buying paraffin, carrying wood for hours, sitting in the dark when the power goes off.

The home is where permaculture pays back fastest. Every litre of rain caught, every stick of wood saved and every hour of free sunshine used is money that stays in the household.

**By the end of this chapter you will be able to:**

- Explain what Zone 0 and appropriate technology mean
- Plan how to make a house warmer in winter and cooler in summer with the sun
- Choose the right solar tool for lights, hot water, cooking and drying food
- Cook with less fuel using a wonder bag and a rocket stove
- Explain how a biogas digester turns manure into cooking gas

## The home is Zone 0

In permaculture we plan the land in **zones**, by how often we visit each part. The house is **Zone 0**, the centre of the design.

Imagine a home that:

- is built from local materials, such as clay and timber from nearby, that can be replaced when they wear out
- stays warm in winter and cool in summer with little heating or cooling
- makes its own electricity from the sun, so the lights stay on when the grid goes off
- cooks on gas made from animal manure
- catches rainwater in tanks and ponds, enough to carry it through a dry season if it is used carefully

The tools that make this possible are called **appropriate technology**: simple, low-cost tools and methods that local people can build, use and repair themselves. They run on local skills and materials and meet basic needs for energy, water, food and waste. Some you can make this weekend; others need a small investment. All of them pay for themselves over time.

You do not need to do it all at once. Each step saves money and makes the household more secure.

## Design the house for the sun

In Southern Africa the sun is in the north. It rides high in summer and low in winter. A house that works with this heats and cools itself for free.

1. Put the main living rooms and most windows on the north side, to let in the winter sun.
2. Build a roof overhang (eaves) or a pergola over the north windows. It shades out the high summer sun and lets the low winter sun in.
3. Keep west windows small. The summer afternoon sun overheats a house.
4. Use heavy materials, such as stone, brick, clay or a concrete floor, where the winter sun falls. This **thermal mass** soaks up heat by day and gives it back at night.
5. Fit a ceiling with insulation. It keeps heat in during winter and out during summer.
6. Plant deciduous trees or vines (ones that drop their leaves in winter) on the north and west. They shade in summer and let the sun through in winter.

If you are building, decide these things before the first brick is laid. In an existing house, start with the ceiling.

## Solar energy

Renewable energy replaces itself: sun, wind, flowing water and **biomass** (plant and animal material, such as wood and manure). Coal does not: once burned, it is gone. Coal still made about 83% of South Africa's electricity in 2024, and burning it fouls the air and drives climate change.

The sun is the renewable we all share. In one hour, the sunshine reaching the Earth carries roughly as much energy as the whole world uses in a year. Southern Africa gets some of the strongest sunshine anywhere, and solar panels now cost less than 1% of what they did in the 1970s.

### Solar panels

Solar panels, also called PV panels, turn sunlight into electricity. Batteries store it for the night.

1. Place them where nothing shades them between about 9 a.m. and 3 p.m.
2. Face them north.
3. Tilt them at about the angle of your latitude: roughly 26 degrees in Johannesburg, 34 degrees in Cape Town.
4. Wipe off dust and bird droppings.

A small kit runs LED lights, charges phones and plays the radio. A bigger system runs a fridge, TV or computer. It needs an **inverter**, which changes the panels' direct current (DC) into the alternating current (AC) that most appliances use.

Keep solar electricity for lights, phones, fridges and TVs. Geysers, kettles, heaters and stoves eat power, and running them on panels needs a big, costly system. Heat water and cook with the tools below.

### Solar geyser

In many South African homes the geyser is the biggest electricity user: an Eskom survey put it at about 39% of an average household's use. A solar geyser heats water in a collector of flat panels or glass tubes on the roof and stores it in an insulated tank. In the simplest kind, the hot water rises into the tank by itself, so the tank must sit above the collector.

- Face the collector north and tilt it like a solar panel.
- Where it frosts, which is most of the inland plateau, use an **indirect** geyser. It heats an antifreeze liquid in the collector instead of water. A direct geyser can freeze and burst.
- Put the electric back-up element on a timer, so it runs only when the sun has not done the job.

> **Tip:** A black-painted drum or bottle of water set in the sun in the morning gives warm washing water by the afternoon, for nothing.

### Solar cooker

A solar cooker cooks with sunlight alone, and costs nothing to run.

- **Box cooker:** an insulated box, black inside, with a glass or clear plastic lid. It traps heat like a closed car in the sun and reaches about 100 to 150 °C, enough to cook and even bake, slowly.
- **Panel cooker:** shiny panels bounce sunlight onto a dark pot inside a clear bag. It is cheap and easy to make.
- **Parabolic cooker:** a curved dish of mirrors focuses sunlight onto the pot. It gets above 250 °C and cooks fast, but must be turned often to follow the sun.

1. Cook between about 10 a.m. and 3 p.m.
2. Use thin, dark pots with tight lids.
3. Turn the cooker to face the sun every hour or so.
4. Allow more time than on a stove. If the sun goes before meat or beans are cooked through, finish them on the stove.

## Drying food with the sun

Summer gives more than a family can eat; winter gives little. Drying carries the surplus across. It works because the moulds and germs that spoil food cannot grow without water.

### Sun drying

Open sun drying is the cheapest method, but it is slow: food can go mouldy before it dries, and flies, dust and dew get at it. Do it well:

1. Use fresh, clean produce. Wash it, and your hands.
2. Cut it into thin, even slices.
3. Dry it on raised racks or trays, never on the ground.
4. Cover it with clean netting against flies.
5. Bring it in at night, or cover it against dew.
6. Turn the pieces once or twice a day.

### Solar dryer

A **solar dryer** is faster and cleaner. It can be as simple as a wooden box with a clear plastic lid and trays of mesh or shade cloth, or as solid as a metal frame with glass. Dryers that let warm air flow through the trays work faster, because moving air carries the moisture away.

Food is dry enough when:

- **fruit** is leathery and bends without wet spots when squeezed
- **vegetables** are brittle and snap or rattle
- **mielies, beans and groundnuts** are hard and rattle, dried to below about 13% moisture within two or three days of harvest

Dry grain and groundnuts fast, and throw out any that are mouldy, discoloured or shrivelled. Mould on damp grain makes **aflatoxin**, a liver poison that cooking does not destroy. Store dried food in clean, airtight containers in a cool, dark place, and throw away anything that smells musty.

Dipping fruit slices in lemon juice and water keeps their colour. Blanching vegetables (a quick dip in boiling water) helps them keep.

## Heat-retention cooking: the wonder bag

A pot of samp and beans can simmer for hours, and every one of those hours burns wood, gas or paraffin. A heat-retention cooker lets the pot finish cooking in its own heat.

A **wonder bag** is an insulated bag that keeps a boiling pot hot for hours. The Wonderbag was developed in South Africa in 2008; a home-made hay box, packed with dry grass or old blankets, works the same way. Less fuel burned means less money spent and less smoke in the house.

1. Bring the food to a full boil and let it simmer for a few minutes. Samp, beans and tough meat need a longer simmer. Soak dry beans, then boil them hard for at least 10 minutes before they go in the bag.
2. With the lid on, move the pot straight into the bag and close it.
3. Use a pot that fits snugly and is well filled. A full pot holds its heat longer.
4. Do not open the bag to check. Every look lets heat out.
5. Eat the food while it is still steaming hot. If it has gone lukewarm, bring it back to the boil first. Never leave food in the bag overnight.

## Rocket stoves

Wood is heavy to carry and slow to regrow. And smoke kills. The World Health Organization estimates that about 2.1 billion people cook on open fires or simple stoves burning wood, dung, crop waste, coal or paraffin. Smoke in their homes caused an estimated 2.9 million deaths in 2021. Women and young children, who spend the most time by the fire, suffer most.

A **rocket stove** burns small sticks in a short, insulated, L-shaped chimney. The fire burns hot and clean, and the heat goes straight up into the pot. Field tests found that rocket stoves use roughly 30 to 50% less wood than an open three-stone fire, with much less smoke and carbon monoxide. Some are portable; others are built in, with a chimney.

1. Burn dry wood. Wet wood smokes and gives little heat.
2. Feed in a few thin sticks at a time and push them in as they burn.
3. Keep the air gap under the sticks clear.
4. Seat the pot snugly, with a small gap for the hot gases to flow around it.
5. Cook outside or in a kitchen with a chimney.

A rocket stove still makes carbon monoxide, a gas you cannot see or smell. Never bring a burning stove, imbawula or coal fire into a closed room to keep warm.

## Biogas digesters

Manure is fuel and fertiliser at once, if we handle it right. **Biogas** is a fuel gas made when bacteria break down manure, food scraps or toilet waste in a sealed tank with no air. The tank is a **biogas digester**. Biogas is about 50 to 70% methane, the part that burns. Most of the rest is carbon dioxide, with a little hydrogen sulphide, which smells of rotten eggs. For a household, the best use is cooking. The liquid that flows out, called **slurry**, is a good fertiliser.

You can buy a digester or build one from hardware-store tanks, pipes and fittings. It must be completely airtight.

1. Feed it fresh cattle, pig or chicken manure mixed with about the same amount of water, plus chopped vegetable scraps.
2. Keep out woody material, straw, bones, plastic, soap, bleach and other chemicals. Chemicals kill the bacteria.
3. Feed a little every day rather than a lot at once. A family usually needs the fresh dung of several cattle or pigs each day.
4. Keep it warm. The bacteria work best at about 30 to 38 °C and make very little gas below about 15 °C. On the Highveld and in Lesotho, bury it, insulate it or put a small plastic tunnel over it.
5. Test the joints for leaks with soapy water, where bubbles show a leak. Keep flames away from the digester and pipes, cook in a well-ventilated kitchen, and close the gas tap after cooking.
6. Cover the inlet and outlet pits. Never climb into a digester or pit, even an empty one: the gases inside can kill.
7. Let the slurry stand or compost it for some weeks. Then put it on fruit trees and crops you cook, never on morogo you eat raw.

## Try it

Make a hay box and cook with it this week.

1. Find a box, crate, basket or old bucket big enough to hold your pot with a hand's width of space all round.
2. Line the bottom thickly with dry grass, old blankets, cushions or pieces of foam.
3. Stand the pot in it and pack more of the same material tightly around the sides. Lift the pot out, leaving a nest.
4. Make a thick pillow of the same material for the top.
5. Cook something that normally simmers for a long time, such as samp and beans. Boil it, simmer it for a few minutes, put the lidded pot into the nest and cover it with the pillow.
6. Leave it for a few hours without opening it. Write down how long it took and how much fuel you used compared with the usual way.

## Key points

- The home is Zone 0. Appropriate technology is simple kit you can build and fix yourself.
- Face the house and the solar panels north, and use thermal mass and eaves to catch winter sun and block summer sun.
- Keep solar electricity for lights, phones and fridges. Heat water and cook with a solar geyser, solar cooker, wonder bag, rocket stove or biogas.
- Dry food fast, clean and off the ground.

Start with one tool this month. When the woodpile lasts longer, you will know which one to build next.
