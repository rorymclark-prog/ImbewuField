# The Sustainable Home: Zone 0 and Appropriate Technology

## The home is Zone 0

So far we have looked at growing food and meeting other needs in a sustainable way. Now we come to the centre of the design: the home itself. In permaculture the house is called Zone 0.

Imagine a home:

- built from local materials, such as clay from nearby or timber from a local woodlot, that can be replaced when they wear out
- designed for your climate, so it stays warm in winter and cool in summer with little heating or cooling
- that makes its own clean electricity from solar panels, so the lights stay on when the grid supply goes off
- that makes cooking gas from animal manure in a biogas digester
- that catches rainwater in tanks and ponds, with enough stored to last through a dry season if it is used carefully
- that uses many other simple technologies to cut costs, catch free energy and build resilience against hard times and climate change

You do not need to do all of this at once. Each small step saves money and makes the household more secure.

## Appropriate technology

Appropriate technology means simple, small-scale tools and machines that:

- suit the people who use them and their culture
- can be built, run and repaired with local skills and materials
- meet basic needs such as energy, water, food and waste disposal

The technologies in this chapter have been used successfully by households across Southern Africa. Some you can make yourself. Others need a small investment. All of them help to build a sustainable homestead and pay for themselves over time.

## Design the house for the sun

In Southern Africa the sun is in the **north**. It is high in the sky in summer and low in winter. A well-designed house uses this:

- Put the main living rooms and most windows on the north side, to let in the warm winter sun.
- Build a roof overhang (eaves) or a pergola over north windows. It shades the high summer sun but lets the low winter sun in.
- Keep windows on the west side small, as the afternoon sun in summer overheats the house.
- Heavy materials such as stone, brick, clay and concrete floors store the day's heat and release it at night. This is called thermal mass.
- A ceiling with insulation keeps heat in during winter and out during summer.
- Plant deciduous trees (trees that lose their leaves in winter) or vines on the north and west, so they shade in summer and let sun through in winter.

## Renewable energy

Renewable (clean) energy comes from natural sources that are constantly replaced, such as sunshine, wind and flowing water.

Coal is different. It is a non-renewable resource: once it is burned, it is gone, and one day it will run out. Most of South Africa's electricity still comes from coal-fired power stations. Burning coal pollutes the air, contributes to acid rain and releases large amounts of carbon dioxide, which drives climate change.

The main types of renewable energy are:

- solar energy (from the sun)
- hydropower (from flowing water)
- wind power
- biomass (from plants and animal waste, such as wood and biogas)

## Solar energy

People have used the sun's energy for cooking, heating and growing crops for thousands of years. The amount of sunlight reaching the Earth is huge: roughly one hour of sunshine carries about as much energy as the whole world uses in a year. Southern Africa has some of the best sunshine in the world.

The price of solar panels has also fallen dramatically. Panels now cost less than 1% of what they cost in the 1970s. For new power plants, solar is now one of the cheapest ways to make electricity.

### Solar electric (PV) panels

Solar panels, also called photovoltaic or PV panels, are made from materials such as silicon that turn sunlight into electricity.

How to get the most from them:

1. Place them on a roof or frame where nothing shades them between about 9 a.m. and 3 p.m.
2. In Southern Africa, face them **north**.
3. Tilt them at an angle roughly equal to your latitude. In most of South Africa this is about 25 to 35 degrees.
4. Keep them clean. Dust and bird droppings reduce the power they make.

The electricity can be stored in batteries for use at night.

**Small systems** power LED lights, charge cellphones and run radios. They are cheap and easy to install.

**Larger systems** can run a fridge, TV or computer. Solar panels make direct current (DC), but most household appliances use alternating current (AC), so a larger system needs an inverter to change DC into AC.

Use solar electricity wisely. Electric geysers, kettles, heaters and stoves use a lot of power, so running them on solar needs a large and expensive system. It is cheaper to keep solar electricity for lights, fridges, phones, computers and TVs, and to heat water and cook with other clean technologies: solar geysers, heat pumps, biogas, solar cookers, heat-retention bags and rocket stoves.

> **Safety:** Anything larger than a plug-in lighting kit must be installed by a qualified electrician, who should give you a Certificate of Compliance. A system connected to the municipal or Eskom grid must be approved by and registered with your municipality or Eskom before it is switched on. Batteries store a lot of energy: keep them in a dry, ventilated place away from children. Lead-acid batteries give off a flammable gas while charging, so never smoke or make sparks near them. Buy lithium batteries only with a proper built-in battery management system.

### Solar geyser (solar water heater)

A solar geyser uses the sun to heat water. Water or another liquid is heated in a collector (flat panels or glass tubes) on the roof, then moves to an insulated storage tank. In simple systems the hot water rises into the tank by itself, so the tank must be higher than the collector. Other systems use a small pump.

- Like solar panels, the collector should face north and be tilted to catch as much sun as possible.
- In areas with frost, which is most of the inland plateau, use an indirect (closed-loop) system. It heats an antifreeze liquid in the collector instead of water. A direct system, where household water flows through the collector, can freeze and burst in frosty areas.
- Most solar geysers have an electric element as a back-up for cloudy days. Put it on a timer so it only switches on when needed.

For many South African households, heating water is the biggest single part of the electricity bill: surveys suggest a geyser uses around 35 to 40% of an average household's electricity. A solar geyser can therefore save a lot of money or fuel and is a worthwhile investment.

> **Safety:** Water from a solar geyser can become hot enough to scald, especially on sunny days. Ask the installer to fit a mixing (tempering) valve. Use a qualified plumber who follows the South African installation standard for solar water heaters (SANS 10106).

> **Tip:** A black-painted container of water left in the sun in the morning will give you warm water for washing by the afternoon, at no cost.

### Solar cooker

A solar cooker uses the sun's energy to heat and cook food. There are three main types:

- **Box cooker:** an insulated box with a black inside and a glass or clear plastic lid. It traps heat like a closed car in the sun. It usually reaches about 100 to 150 °C, which is enough to cook and even bake, slowly.
- **Panel cooker:** shiny panels reflect sunlight onto a dark pot inside a clear bag. It is cheap and easy to make.
- **Parabolic cooker:** a curved dish of mirrors focuses sunlight onto the pot, like a magnifying glass. It gets very hot (over 250 °C) and cooks fast, but must be turned often to follow the sun.

Many solar cookers are cheap and simple, and can be bought or home-made. Once you have one, it uses no fuel and costs nothing to run.

Tips for solar cooking:

1. Cook in the middle of the day, roughly between 10 a.m. and 3 p.m.
2. Use dark, thin pots with tight lids.
3. Turn the cooker to face the sun every hour or so.
4. Allow more time than on a stove.

> **Safety:** Parabolic cookers focus sunlight into a very hot point. Never look into the reflector, and wear sunglasses when using it. Keep children away, and keep dry grass and paper away from the focus. Use oven gloves: pots and lids get very hot.

> **Safety:** Food cooked in a solar cooker must get properly hot (above 60 °C, and meat and beans must cook through). Do not leave raw meat in the cooker on a cloudy day or late in the afternoon, when it only becomes lukewarm. If the sun goes, finish cooking on a stove.

## Drying food with the sun

Drying preserves fruit, vegetables and grain for the months when fresh food is scarce. It works because the moulds and bacteria that spoil food cannot grow without water.

### Sun drying

Sun drying is the cheapest and easiest method. Place sliced fruit or vegetables on a clean surface in the sun for several hot, dry days.

The problems with open sun drying are:

- it is slow, and food can go mouldy before it is dry
- flies, other insects, birds, animals, dust and germs can reach the food
- rain or dew can wet it again

So the quality is often poor, and it can be unhygienic. You can make sun drying safer:

1. Use only fresh, clean, good-quality produce. Wash it and your hands first.
2. Cut food into thin, even slices.
3. Dry it on a raised rack or on trays, not on the ground.
4. Cover it with clean netting or cloth to keep off insects.
5. Bring it inside at night or cover it well, so dew does not wet it.
6. Turn the pieces once or twice a day.

### Solar dryer

A solar dryer is faster and cleaner than open sun drying. It can be home-made from simple materials or bought at a reasonable price. It can be basic, such as a wooden box frame covered with clear plastic, or more solid, such as a metal frame with clear plastic or glass and trays of shade cloth or mesh.

Some solar dryers pass warm air through the trays. Others are a hot box with little air movement. Air movement helps: it carries the moisture away.

When is food dry enough?

- **Fruit** should feel leathery and bend without any wet spots when squeezed.
- **Vegetables** should be brittle or hard, and snap or rattle.
- **Maize, beans and groundnuts** must be dried quickly after harvest, to below about 13% moisture. The grain should be hard and rattle, and must not stay damp for more than two or three days.

Store dried food in clean, airtight containers in a cool, dark place. Check it often. Throw away anything that smells musty or shows mould.

> **Safety:** Mould on damp maize and groundnuts can produce aflatoxin, a poison that damages the liver and can cause cancer. Cooking does not destroy it. Dry grain and groundnuts fast, store them dry, and throw away mouldy, discoloured or shrivelled grain and nuts. Do not feed mouldy grain to animals either.

> **Tip:** Dipping sliced fruit in lemon juice mixed with water before drying helps to keep its colour. Blanching vegetables (dipping them briefly in boiling water) before drying helps them keep better. Meat and fish need salt and special methods; follow a tested recipe.

## Heat-retention cooking: the wonder bag

A heat-retention cooker is an insulated bag or box that keeps a hot pot hot for hours. The best-known is the Wonderbag, developed in South Africa. You can also make your own "hay box" from a box or basket packed with dry grass, old blankets or cushions.

It works like this: you bring the food to the boil on the stove, then put the pot, with its lid on, straight into the bag and close it. The trapped heat keeps cooking the food slowly for several hours, with no extra fuel. It can save a large amount of wood, gas, paraffin or electricity, and less fuel burned means less smoke in the home.

How to use it:

1. Bring the food to a full boil and let it simmer for a few minutes. Beans, samp and tough meat need a longer simmer first.
2. Keep the lid on and move the pot into the bag immediately.
3. Use a pot that fits snugly and is well filled. A full pot keeps its heat longer than a half-empty one.
4. Do not open the bag to check the food during cooking. Each time you open it, heat escapes.
5. When you take the food out, it should still be steaming hot. If it has cooled to lukewarm, bring it back to the boil before eating.

> **Safety:** Food must stay above 60 °C the whole time it is in the bag. Germs that cause food poisoning grow fast between about 5 °C and 60 °C. Never put lukewarm food into the bag, and do not leave food in the bag overnight to eat the next day.

> **Safety:** Red kidney beans and other dry beans contain a natural poison that is destroyed only by proper boiling. Soak dry beans, then boil them hard for at least 10 minutes before putting them in the bag. Gentle heat without that first hard boil can make the poison stronger.

## Rocket stoves

A rocket stove burns small sticks of wood very efficiently. The fire burns in an insulated, L-shaped chimney. This makes it burn hot and clean, and sends the heat straight up to the pot.

Tests in the field found that rocket stoves use roughly 30 to 50% less wood than cooking on an open three-stone fire, and produce much less smoke and carbon monoxide.

This matters for health. The World Health Organization estimates that about 2.1 billion people, around a quarter of the world's population, cook on open fires or simple stoves burning wood, dung, crop waste, coal or paraffin. The smoke in their homes caused an estimated 2.9 million deaths in 2021. Women and young children, who spend most time near the fire, suffer most.

Rocket stoves come in many sizes. Many are portable. Others are built into the kitchen, like a normal stove, with a chimney to take the smoke outside.

How to use a rocket stove well:

1. Use dry wood. Wet wood makes smoke and gives little heat.
2. Feed in a few thin sticks at a time and push them in as they burn.
3. Keep the air opening under the sticks clear.
4. Fit the pot snugly on the stove, with a small gap for the hot gases to flow around it.

> **Safety:** A rocket stove is cleaner than an open fire, but it still makes smoke and carbon monoxide, a gas that you cannot see or smell and that kills. Cook outside or in a well-ventilated kitchen with a chimney. Never bring a burning stove, brazier (imbawula) or coal fire into a closed room to heat it, especially overnight. Every winter in South Africa people die in their sleep this way.

> **Safety:** Stand the stove on a firm, level base away from children, grass and anything that can burn. Never use petrol to light a fire. Keep paraffin in a clearly labelled container with a child-proof cap, never in a cooldrink or milk bottle. Swallowing paraffin is the most common cause of accidental poisoning of young children in South Africa.

## Biogas digesters

Biogas is a fuel gas made when bacteria break down organic waste, such as animal manure, human waste or food scraps, in a closed tank without air. A process without oxygen is called anaerobic. The tank is called a digester.

Biogas is usually about 50 to 70% methane, which is the gas that burns. Most of the rest is carbon dioxide, with small amounts of other gases, including hydrogen sulphide, which smells like rotten eggs.

A digester turns waste into energy. The gas can be used for cooking, lighting, or, in larger systems, making electricity. For a household, cooking is the most useful way to use it. The liquid that comes out of the digester, called slurry or digestate, is a good fertiliser.

Digesters can be bought ready-made, or built from materials available at a hardware store, such as plastic tanks, pipes and fittings. They must be completely airtight.

### What to feed a digester

- **Good:** fresh cattle, pig or chicken manure, mixed with about an equal amount of water; vegetable and food scraps chopped small; toilet waste in a digester designed for it.
- **Avoid:** woody material, straw, bones, plastic, soap, bleach, disinfectants and other chemicals. Chemicals kill the bacteria that make the gas.

Feed a little every day, rather than a lot at once. As a rough guide, the daily dung of one cow gives enough gas for about an hour of cooking on one burner. A family usually needs the dung of several cattle or pigs, collected every day.

The bacteria work best when the digester is warm, around 30 to 38 °C. Below about 15 °C they produce very little gas. In areas with cold winters, such as the Highveld and Lesotho, bury or insulate the digester, or cover it with a small plastic greenhouse, to keep it warm.

> **Safety:** Biogas burns and can explode. Methane mixed with air explodes at between 5 and 15% gas in air. Never smoke or light a flame near the digester, the gas bag or the pipes. Check for leaks with soapy water (bubbles show a leak), never with a flame. Methane rises and collects under roofs, so only use biogas appliances in a well-ventilated kitchen, and turn off the gas at the tap after cooking.

> **Safety:** Never climb into a digester, pit or tank, even an empty one. It can hold hydrogen sulphide, a poisonous gas, and carbon dioxide, which leaves no air to breathe. People have died trying to rescue others. Cover inlet and outlet pits and keep children away from them.

> **Safety:** Slurry still contains germs, especially from human or pig waste. Compost it or let it stand for some weeks before use. Use it on fruit trees, maize and crops that are cooked. Do not put it on leafy vegetables or other crops eaten raw. Wear gloves and wash your hands after handling it.

## Key points

- The home is Zone 0, the centre of the permaculture design.
- Appropriate technology is simple, affordable and can be built and repaired with local skills.
- Design the house for the sun: in Southern Africa, main windows and solar panels face north.
- Use solar electricity for lights, phones and fridges. Heat water and cook with solar geysers, biogas, solar cookers, heat-retention bags and rocket stoves.
- Solar panels and grid-connected systems must be installed by a qualified electrician and registered where required. In frost areas, use an indirect solar geyser.
- Dry food quickly, cleanly and off the ground. Dry maize and groundnuts fast and throw away mouldy grain.
- In a heat-retention bag, boil the food first and keep it above 60 °C. Boil dry beans hard for 10 minutes first.
- Rocket stoves save wood and reduce smoke, but never burn any fire in a closed room.
- Biogas is explosive and its pits can be deadly: no flames, check leaks with soap, never enter a digester, and do not use slurry on raw-eaten vegetables.
