# Glossary

Some permaculture words have no short everyday word in our home languages. In every language of this manual, these words are kept in **English**, so that farmers, extension officers, trainers and suppliers all use the same word. Each chapter explains the word the first time it appears. This glossary explains them all in one place.

| Word | What it means |
|---|---|
| Animal tractor | Animals such as chickens kept in a movable pen on a garden bed. They scratch, weed, eat pests and add manure, then the pen is moved to the next bed. Also called a *chicken tractor*. |
| Annual | A plant that grows, flowers, makes seed and dies in one year or season, such as maize, beans or lettuce. |
| Appropriate technology | Simple, low-cost tools and methods that local people can build, use and repair themselves, such as a rocket stove or a solar dryer. |
| Berm | A low bank of soil, usually built on the downhill side of a swale, that helps hold water on the slope. |
| Biodiversity | The variety of living things in a place: plants, animals, insects, birds, soil life and fungi. More variety makes a farm stronger. |
| Biogas digester | A sealed tank where manure and food waste rot without air and give off biogas (mostly methane) for cooking, plus a liquid fertiliser called slurry. |
| Biomass | All the plant and animal material that grows on your land: leaves, stems, grass clippings, prunings, manure. It can be used as mulch, compost, fodder or fuel. |
| Biosecurity | The steps you take to stop diseases and pests coming onto your farm or spreading between animals, such as keeping new animals apart for a while. |
| Blackwater | Water from toilets and nappies. It carries germs and must never be used on a food garden. |
| Canopy | The top layer of a food forest: the tallest trees, whose leaves form a roof over the plants below. |
| Carbon to nitrogen ratio | The balance between "brown" materials (carbon, such as dry leaves and straw) and "green" materials (nitrogen, such as fresh grass and manure) in a compost heap. About 25 to 30 parts brown to 1 part green works well. |
| Catchment | The area of land or roof from which rainwater flows to one place, such as a tank, pond or stream. |
| Chop and drop | Cutting plants and leaving the cut material on the ground as mulch, where it rots and feeds the soil. |
| Climate change | The long-term change in weather patterns caused mainly by human activity. In Southern Africa it brings hotter days, less reliable rain, longer droughts and heavier storms. |
| Companion planting | Growing plants together that help each other, for example by attracting helpful insects, confusing pests or giving shade. |
| Compost | Plant and animal waste that has rotted down into dark, crumbly, sweet-smelling material that feeds the soil. |
| Contour | A line across a slope that stays at the same height all the way along. Swales, beds and ploughing should follow the contour. |
| Coppicing | Cutting a tree or shrub back close to the ground so that it grows new shoots, which can be cut again later for poles, firewood or fodder. |
| Crop rotation | Moving each family of crops to a different bed every season or year, so that pests and diseases do not build up and the soil stays balanced. |
| Double-reach bed | A raised garden bed narrow enough (about 1 to 1.2 m wide) to reach the middle from both sides, so you never step on the soil. |
| Drip irrigation | Watering through pipes with small holes or drippers that let water out slowly at the base of each plant. It saves a lot of water. |
| Ecosystem | A community of plants, animals, soil life and fungi living together with the soil, water and air around them. |
| Edge | The place where two different areas meet, such as the edge of a pond or a forest. Edges are often the richest places for life and growth. |
| Erosion | The washing or blowing away of soil by water or wind, especially from bare or overgrazed land. |
| Firebreak | A strip of land cleared or burnt so that a veld fire cannot cross it. South African law requires firebreaks on many farm boundaries. |
| First-flush diverter | A device on a gutter or pipe that throws away the first, dirtiest rain from a roof before the clean water flows into the tank. |
| Fodder | Plants grown or collected to feed animals, such as grass, lucerne, pods and tree leaves. |
| Food forest | A garden planted like a natural forest, in layers of trees, shrubs, herbs, groundcovers and climbers, that gives food with little work once it is established. |
| Frost pocket | A low place where cold air settles on still, clear nights, so frost is worse there than on the slope above. |
| Grafting | Joining a piece of one plant (such as a good fruit variety) onto the roots of another plant, so they grow together as one tree. |
| Green manure | A crop, often a legume, grown to be cut or dug back into the soil to feed it, rather than to be harvested. |
| Greywater | Used water from washing, baths and basins (not toilets). With care it can water trees and non-food plants. |
| Groundcover | Low plants that spread over the soil and protect it, such as pumpkins, sweet potato or clover. |
| Guild | A group of plants (and sometimes animals) placed together around a main plant, usually a fruit tree, so that they support each other. |
| Habitat | The natural home of a plant or animal, with the food, water and shelter it needs. |
| Humus | The dark, stable part of organic matter in the soil, left after plant and animal material has fully broken down. It holds water and nutrients. |
| Indigenous | A plant or animal that occurs naturally in an area and was not brought in by people. |
| Infiltration | Water soaking down into the soil instead of running off the surface. |
| Invasive species | A plant or animal from elsewhere that spreads out of control and harms natural areas, water supplies or farming. In South Africa, listed invaders are controlled by law (NEMBA). |
| Keyhole bed | A round garden bed with a path cut into it like a keyhole, so you can reach the whole bed without stepping on it. Often built with a compost basket in the middle. |
| Legume | A plant of the bean and pea family. Most legumes can take nitrogen from the air and add it to the soil. |
| Microclimate | The small area of climate around a place, such as a warm north-facing wall or a cool, shady hollow. It can differ a lot from the area as a whole. |
| Mulch | A cover of dry grass, leaves, straw or other material spread on the soil to keep it moist, cool and protected, and to stop weeds. |
| Nitrogen fixer | A plant, usually a legume, whose roots work with bacteria to take nitrogen from the air and add it to the soil. |
| Organic matter | Anything in the soil that was once alive: roots, leaves, manure, dead insects and soil life. |
| Perennial | A plant that lives for more than two years, such as fruit trees, rhubarb, herbs and many grasses. |
| Permaculture | A way of designing farms, gardens and communities so that they work like natural systems and keep producing for generations. The word joins *permanent* and *agriculture*. |
| pH | A measure of how acidic or alkaline the soil is, on a scale from 0 to 14. Most crops grow best between about 6 and 7. |
| Pollinator | An animal, such as a bee, butterfly, beetle or bird, that carries pollen from flower to flower so that plants can make fruit and seed. |
| Predator | An animal that eats other animals. In the garden, helpful predators such as ladybirds, birds and frogs eat pests. |
| Rocket stove | A simple, well-insulated stove that burns small sticks very hot and cleanly, using much less wood and making less smoke. |
| Rootstock | The root part of a grafted tree, chosen for its strength or its suitability to the soil or climate. |
| Runoff | Rainwater that flows over the surface of the ground instead of soaking in. |
| Sector | A force or energy that comes onto your land from outside, such as sun, wind, fire, water or noise. Sector planning maps where these come from. |
| Slurry | The liquid left over from a biogas digester. It can be used as fertiliser, but not on crops eaten raw. |
| Solar dryer | A box or cabinet that uses the sun's heat, with shade from direct sun, to dry fruit, vegetables and grain safely. |
| Succession | The natural, step-by-step change in the plants and animals of a place over time, for example from bare ground to grass to shrubs. |
| Swale | A level ditch dug along the contour of a slope to catch rainwater and let it soak into the soil. |
| Thermal mass | Heavy material such as stone, brick or water that soaks up heat during the day and gives it back slowly at night. |
| Topsoil | The top, darkest layer of soil, where most roots, soil life and organic matter are found. |
| Trap crop | A plant grown to attract pests away from your main crop, so they can be removed. |
| Understorey | The layer of smaller trees and shrubs that grow beneath the canopy in a food forest. |
| Windbreak | A row or belt of trees, shrubs or tall grass that slows the wind and protects crops, animals and homes. |
| Wonder bag | A heat-retention cooker: an insulated bag into which a pot of boiling food is placed so that it keeps cooking in its own heat without more fuel. |
| Worm farm | A container in which special compost worms (red wigglers) turn kitchen scraps into rich worm castings and worm tea. |
| Worm tea | The liquid that drains from a worm farm. Diluted with water, it is a gentle liquid fertiliser. |
| Zone | An area of the farm planned according to how often you need to visit it. Zone 0 is the house and Zone 1 is the kitchen garden, out to Zone 5, which is left wild. |

## Key points

- The words in this glossary are kept in English in every language of this manual.
- Each chapter explains a word the first time it appears; come back here any time you forget one.
