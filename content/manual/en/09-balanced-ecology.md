# Creating a balanced, productive ecology

## Learning from nature

In permaculture the goal is to build farms and gardens that work like natural systems: they produce food, fodder, fuel and timber, they stay healthy, and they need fewer outside inputs as time goes on.

You do not need to be a scientist to use ecology on your farm. But you do need to understand how nature works, and to keep looking to nature for ideas and solutions. The most important tool is **observation**: spend time watching your land, the veld around it, and how plants, animals, water and weather change through the seasons.

When we understand what drives nature, we can design our farm so that its parts are in the right balance and plugged into natural cycles. Then we use the free energy of sun, rain, soil life and wild creatures, instead of fighting them.

## What is an ecosystem?

**Ecology** is the study of how living things relate to each other and to their surroundings.

An **ecosystem** is all the living things in one place, together with the non-living things around them, and all the ways they affect each other.

- **Living things** include plants, animals, insects, fungi and micro-organisms (tiny living things too small to see).
- **Non-living things** include soil, water, air, sunlight, rocks and climate.

Living and non-living parts depend on each other. Together they form cycles and food chains. An ecosystem can be as small as a pond or a compost heap, or as big as the grasslands of the Highveld or the ocean. All the ecosystems on Earth together make up the **biosphere**.

Every ecosystem has three main groups of living things:

- **Producers:** green plants. They use sunlight to make their own food, and they feed everything else.
- **Consumers:** animals, from insects to birds to people. They eat plants or other animals.
- **Decomposers:** fungi, bacteria, earthworms and other soil life. They break down dead plants, dung and bodies and return the nutrients to the soil.

For a permaculture system to work, we need to support life at all these levels. For example, if we give insect-eating birds a place to live, they help to keep pests down.

## Food chains and the ecological pyramid

We can draw the life in an ecosystem as a **pyramid**.

- At the bottom are the **plants**, which capture the sun's energy. They rest on the **soil** and its decomposers, which recycle nutrients so that plants can keep growing.
- Above them are the **plant-eaters**, such as caterpillars, aphids, mice and cattle.
- Higher up are the **predators** that eat them, such as ladybirds, spiders, birds and lizards.
- At the top are a few **top predators**, such as owls, eagles and jackals.

Each level up has fewer living things, because energy is lost at every step. It takes a lot of grass to feed one cow, and many mice to feed one owl.

This is why we should not try to kill every pest. If we poison all the aphids and caterpillars, the ladybirds and birds that eat them go hungry and move away. Then, when pests come back, there is nothing left to control them, and pest outbreaks get worse.

Because the whole pyramid rests on plants and soil, **soil health decides how much life the land can carry** and how much it can yield.

## How ecosystems work

Four processes keep every ecosystem running:

- **The water cycle.** Rain falls, soaks into the soil or runs off, is used by plants, and returns to the air.
- **The mineral (nutrient) cycle.** Nutrients move from soil to plants to animals, and back to the soil through dung and decay.
- **Energy flow.** Energy from the sun is captured by plants and passes up the food chain. Unlike water and minerals, energy is not recycled. It must be captured again every day by green leaves.
- **Community dynamics.** The mix of plants and animals changes over time. Succession (below) is part of this.

When these processes work well, the land gives us **ecosystem services**: clean water, fertile soil, pollination, pest control, grazing, fuel, and a more stable local climate.

In the rest of this chapter we look at three ideas from ecology that help with design: **succession**, **habitat** and **biodiversity**. Use them together with the permaculture principles in the first chapter.

## Succession

### How land heals itself

After land has been disturbed, for example by ploughing, overgrazing, a fire or a flood, nature starts to cover and repair it. The plant community changes step by step over time. This is called **succession**.

1. **Pioneer plants** come first. They are often the annual weeds that farmers dislike. They grow fast, cover bare soil, and hold it in place. When they die, their roots and leaves feed the soil.
2. **Hardier plants follow.** Perennial grasses, herbs and shrubs move in. Many of them are **legumes** that add nitrogen to the soil. They give shelter to smaller plants and food and homes to insects, birds and other animals.
3. **The long-lived community** develops last. Soil gets deeper and richer, and the land supports more life and more kinds of life.

The end point of succession is not the same everywhere. It depends on rainfall, frost, soil, fire and grazing.

### Not all land becomes forest

Many books, written in wetter countries, say that all land naturally grows into forest. **In Southern Africa this is not true.**

Natural forest covers less than 1% of South Africa. It grows only in small patches where rainfall is high and frost is rare, such as the Garden Route and the moist eastern escarpment. Most of our land is naturally covered by other ecosystems that are just as old, stable and rich in species:

- **Grassland** covers much of the Highveld, the Drakensberg and Lesotho. Frost, fire and grazing keep trees out. These grasslands are ancient and hold many rare plants. They also catch and store the water that feeds our main rivers.
- **Savanna** (bushveld), grass with scattered trees, covers much of the north and east. Fire, grazing and browsing keep the balance between grass and trees.
- **Fynbos**, in the winter-rainfall Western Cape, needs fire roughly every 10 to 15 years. Many fynbos plants, such as proteas, only release and sprout their seed after fire. Without fire, fynbos loses species.
- The dry **Karoo** and other arid areas are naturally shrubland.

In these places, fire and grazing are part of how the natural system stays healthy. When fire is kept out and land is overgrazed, grassland and savanna can become thick with thorn bush. This is called **bush encroachment**. It has already spread over more than 7 million hectares of South Africa and reduces grazing and water.

> **Note:** Planting trees in natural grassland or fynbos does not "improve" it. It destroys a rare, water-producing ecosystem. Plant trees around your homestead and in your food forest, but let the natural veld on your land stay as grassland, savanna or fynbos.

### Using succession on your farm

On the farm, we use the idea of succession in our own designs:

- **Pioneers first.** On bare, poor soil, start with tough pioneer plants, green manures and nitrogen-fixing legumes to cover and feed the soil. Then plant more demanding crops.
- **Stages in a food forest.** A food forest planted near the home copies the stages of succession: fast-growing support plants first, then fruit and nut trees that slowly take over. Legumes grow between the young trees to feed the soil.
- **Choose support plants carefully.** Many pioneer "nitrogen-fixing trees" in overseas permaculture books, such as black wattle, *Leucaena* and red sesbania, are declared invaders in South Africa. Use crops such as pigeon pea, and indigenous legumes such as sweet thorn (*Vachellia karroo*), where they suit the site. Sweet thorn spreads readily in overgrazed veld, so keep it inside your planted areas.
- **Respect the local end point.** In a grassland area, your wild zone (Zone 5) should be healthy grassland, not a planted forest.

### Farming against nature, or with it

Much modern farming works against succession. The soil is ploughed, sprayed with weedkillers and pesticides, fed with chemical fertiliser and planted with a single crop, season after season. Nature keeps trying to cover the bare soil with weeds to start the healing process, and the farmer keeps spraying them.

Keeping land at this early, bare stage costs a lot of time, fuel and chemicals. The system stays unstable and low in diversity, and it is easily damaged by drought, pests and erosion.

In permaculture we aim for the opposite. We want to build soil and diversity, so that our farm becomes more fertile and more stable over time. In such a system, nutrient cycles are working and there is enough life to keep pests and diseases in check. It still changes all the time, but it can give good yields at lower cost and with less effort.

## Habitat

A **habitat** is the home of a plant or animal. It is the place that gives it everything it needs to live: food, water, shelter, space and a safe place to breed.

### Example: a frog's habitat

- Most frogs lay their eggs in water, so they need a pond, wetland or stream.
- They need shelter from predators such as birds and snakes. Long grass, rocks and overhanging plants around the pond give them places to hide.
- They need food: insects, and for some kinds, slugs and snails.

This pond environment is the frog's **habitat**. Different plants and animals need different habitats. Losing habitat is one of the main reasons why plants and animals become rare and die out.

### Habitat as a job advert

We can also think of habitat as a **niche**: a job that needs doing. There are many jobs on a farm, such as eating pests, pollinating flowers and recycling waste. If we had to do them all ourselves, or pay for them, it would cost a lot. It is wiser to create habitats that "advertise" for wild helpers to come and share the work. The advert must be attractive: enough food, water and a safe place to live.

Here are some simple ways to create habitat.

**Provide water.** Every living thing needs water. A bird bath, small pond or dam quickly brings in birds, frogs, dragonflies and other helpers. Frogs, toads and dragonflies eat large numbers of insects. Put a few stones or sticks in the water so that bees and other insects can land and drink without drowning.

> **Safety:** Small children can drown in very little water. Fence ponds or keep them covered with strong mesh, and cover all tanks and wells. To stop mosquitoes breeding, keep water clean and let frogs, tadpoles and dragonfly larvae eat the mosquito larvae. Do not stock garden ponds with mosquitofish (*Gambusia*): it is a listed invasive fish in South Africa, and it eats tadpoles too.

**Leave wild areas.** If you have natural veld, bush or wetland on your farm, keep it. This is your **Zone 5**. If not, return a small area, and some of the uncultivated corners, to indigenous plants. This is probably the most important thing you can do for habitat. Local indigenous plants support far more local insects, birds and other animals than exotic plants do. Manage grassland with fire and grazing according to local rules, and keep proper firebreaks.

**Plant hedgerows.** Plant mixed rows of trees, shrubs and herbs along fences, field edges and paths. Indigenous hedge plants include Cape honeysuckle (*Tecoma capensis*), plumbago (*Plumbago auriculata*), sand olive (*Dodonaea viscosa*), kei apple (*Dovyalis caffra*) and karee and other *Searsia* species. Hedgerows give shelter, food and nesting places, and they also act as windbreaks.

**Make piles of rocks and logs.** Rock piles make good homes for lizards. Lizards bask on the warm rocks during the day and eat many insects. Log piles shelter beetles, frogs and fungi.

> **Safety:** Snakes and scorpions may also shelter in rock and log piles. Build them away from the house, paths and places where children play, and wear gloves and boots when you move rocks or logs.

**Put up owl boxes.** Barn owls nest in holes, and they will move into a wooden owl box. Mount it on a pole, tree or building at least 3 m above the ground, out of reach of cats and people. A family of barn owls can eat 1 000 or more rodents in a year, so owls are a big help in controlling rats and mice.

> **Warning:** Rat poison kills owls. Owls, eagles, cats and dogs die when they eat rats and mice that have eaten poison. If you want owls to control rodents, do not use rat poison. In some communities owls are feared as a bad sign. Talk to your neighbours about how owls help the farm.

## Biodiversity

**Biodiversity** means the variety of life: the many different kinds of plants, animals, insects, fungi and micro-organisms in a place, and the differences within each kind.

Diversity is the key to a healthy and productive permaculture farm. It is the opposite of a **monoculture**, which is row after row of only one crop.

You see real biodiversity when you walk through healthy natural veld, fynbos or an indigenous forest: many kinds of grasses, flowers, trees, shrubs, insects, birds and fungi all living together.

### When diversity is lost

A pine plantation is an example of very low diversity. There are only pine trees, hectare after hectare, and very little grows underneath them. Some people call such plantations "green deserts". In South Africa, several kinds of pine are also declared invaders: they spread into grassland and fynbos and use a lot of water.

### Why diversity helps

- **It spreads the risk.** If one crop fails because of drought, pests or disease, other crops still give a harvest.
- **It helps control pests.** A mix of plants gives food and shelter to predators and parasites that eat pests. Pests also find it harder to find their favourite plant among many others. Diverse gardens are less likely to suffer serious pest outbreaks, although they are never pest-free.
- **It keeps something to harvest all year.** Different plants flower, fruit and ripen at different times. Different trees drop their leaves in different seasons, so there is always food for people and for soil life.
- **It builds healthy soil.** Different roots and different plant leftovers feed a wider range of soil life.

### How to build diversity on your farm

- Grow **polycultures**: many different crops together in the same bed or field, instead of one crop in long rows.
- Plant **perennials** such as fruit trees, herbs and perennial vegetables alongside annual crops.
- Plant a **food forest** with many layers, from tall trees to groundcovers and root crops.
- Keep a mix of **animals**, and let them play their part in the system, such as chickens eating pests and cattle manure feeding the compost.
- Leave room for **wild plants and animals** in hedgerows, ponds and wild areas.
- Grow **flowers**, including indigenous flowers, to feed bees and other helpful insects all year round.

When the parts of a farm are linked so that the outputs of one part feed another, the whole farm becomes stronger. Dung feeds the soil, crop leftovers feed the animals, and insects and birds control the pests. This is how we build a strong, resilient farm ecosystem that looks after most of its own needs.

## Key points

- An ecosystem is all the living and non-living things in one place and how they affect each other. It has producers, consumers and decomposers.
- The pyramid of life rests on plants and soil. Do not try to kill every pest, or you starve the predators that help you.
- Ecosystems run on the water cycle, the nutrient cycle, energy flow from the sun, and changes in the plant and animal community over time.
- Succession is how disturbed land heals. In Southern Africa most land does not end as forest: grassland, savanna and fynbos are natural, stable systems that need fire and grazing.
- Do not plant trees in natural grassland or fynbos, and never use invasive "pioneer" trees.
- Create habitat with water, wild areas, indigenous hedgerows, rock and log piles, and owl boxes. Fence or cover ponds, and do not use rat poison if you want owls.
- Biodiversity spreads risk, reduces pest outbreaks and keeps the farm productive all year.
