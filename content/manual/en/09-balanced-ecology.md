# Creating a balanced, productive ecology

Nature farmed this land long before us, without a bag of fertiliser. Grass catches the sun and rain. Cattle and buck eat the grass. Dung feeds the soil, and the soil feeds the grass. Nothing is wasted, and nobody sprays.

We do not need to be scientists to farm like this. We need to understand nature's engine well enough to plug our farm into its free energy instead of fighting it. The best tool is still the oldest: time spent watching.

**By the end of this chapter you will be able to:**

- Explain what an ecosystem is and how the pyramid of life works
- Use succession to heal bare or worn-out land
- Build homes on your farm for wild helpers such as frogs, lizards and owls
- Add diversity so that your farm spreads its risk and keeps pests in check

## What is an ecosystem?

**Ecology** is the study of how living things relate to each other and to their surroundings. An **ecosystem** is a community of plants, animals and soil life living together with the soil, water, air and sunlight around them. It can be as small as a pond or a compost heap, or as big as the Highveld grasslands.

Every ecosystem has three working groups:

- **Producers:** green plants. They catch sunlight and feed everything else.
- **Consumers:** animals, from aphids to cattle to people. They eat plants or each other.
- **Decomposers:** fungi, bacteria, earthworms and other soil life. They break down dead plants, dung and bodies and return the nutrients to the soil.

Four processes keep it running:

- **The water cycle.** Rain soaks in or runs off, plants use it, and it returns to the air.
- **The nutrient cycle.** Nutrients move from soil to plant to animal, and back to the soil through dung and decay.
- **Energy flow.** Plants catch the sun's energy and pass it up the food chain. Energy is not recycled: green leaves must catch it fresh every day.
- **Change over time.** The mix of plants and animals shifts from year to year (see Succession below).

When these work well, the land gives us clean water, fertile soil, pollination, pest control and grazing for free.

## Food chains and the ecological pyramid

Picture the life on a farm as a pyramid.

- At the base are the **plants**, which catch the sun's energy. They rest on the **soil** and its decomposers.
- Above them are the **plant-eaters**, such as caterpillars, aphids, mice and cattle.
- Higher up are the **predators** that eat them, such as ladybirds, spiders, birds and lizards.
- At the top are a few **top predators**, such as owls, eagles and jackals.

Each level up holds fewer living things, because energy is lost at every step. It takes a lot of grass to feed one cow, and many mice to feed one owl.

What this means on the farm:

1. Do not try to kill every pest. Poison all the aphids, and the ladybirds and birds that eat them go hungry and leave. When the aphids return, nothing stops them.
2. Feed the base. The whole pyramid rests on plants and soil, so soil health decides how much life, and how much harvest, your land can carry.
3. Give the predators a home (see Habitat below).

## Succession

**Succession** is the step-by-step change in the plants and animals of a place over time. It is how land heals. After ploughing, overgrazing, fire or flood:

1. **Pioneer plants** come first, often the annual weeds farmers dislike. They grow fast, cover bare soil and hold it. When they die, they feed it.
2. **Hardier plants follow:** perennial grasses, herbs and shrubs, many of them **legumes** (the bean and pea family) that add nitrogen to the soil. They shelter smaller plants and feed insects and birds.
3. **A long-lived community** settles last. The soil grows deeper and richer, and the land carries more life, and more kinds of life.

### Not all land becomes forest

Books written in wetter countries say that all land grows into forest. In Southern Africa it does not. Natural forest covers less than 1% of South Africa, in small patches where rain is high and frost is rare, such as the Garden Route and the moist eastern escarpment. Most of our land settles into something else, just as old and rich in species:

- **Grassland** covers much of the Highveld, the Drakensberg and Lesotho. Frost, fire and grazing keep the trees out. These ancient grasslands hold many rare plants and catch the water that feeds our main rivers.
- **Savanna** (bushveld), grass with scattered trees, covers much of the north and east. Fire, grazing and browsing keep the balance between grass and trees.
- **Fynbos**, in the winter-rainfall Western Cape, needs fire roughly every 10 to 15 years. Many fynbos plants, such as proteas, release and sprout their seed only after fire.
- The dry **Karoo** is naturally shrubland.

Keep fire out and overgraze, and grassland and savanna thicken with thorn bush. This **bush encroachment** already covers more than 7 million hectares of South Africa and costs grazing and water.

> **Note:** Planting trees in natural grassland or fynbos does not improve it. It destroys a rare, water-giving ecosystem. Plant trees around the homestead and in the food forest, and let your wild veld stay grassland, savanna or fynbos.

### Using succession on your farm

1. **Pioneers first.** On bare, poor soil, sow tough cover crops and **green manures** (crops grown to feed the soil), such as cowpea. Plant demanding crops after them.
2. **Stage your food forest.** Grow fast support plants between young fruit trees, and cut them back as mulch as the trees take over.
3. **Choose support plants that stay put.** Many "nitrogen-fixing trees" in overseas books, such as black wattle, *Leucaena* and red sesbania, spread out of control in South Africa. Grow pigeon pea instead, and indigenous sweet thorn (*Vachellia karroo*) where it suits the site. Sweet thorn spreads into overgrazed veld, so keep it inside your planted areas.
4. **Respect the local end point.** In grassland country, your wild zone (Zone 5) should be healthy grassland, not a planted forest.

### Farming against nature, or with it

Much modern farming holds the land at the bare first stage: plough, spray, fertilise, one crop, repeat. Nature covers the bare soil with weeds to start healing, and the farmer sprays them. This costs time, fuel and chemicals, and leaves land that is unstable, poor in life and easily hurt by drought, pests and erosion.

Permaculture goes the other way. We build soil and diversity, so the farm grows more fertile and more stable each year, with enough life in it to keep pests and diseases in check.

## Habitat

A **habitat** is the home of a plant or animal: the place that gives it food, water, shelter, space and a safe place to breed. A frog needs water for its eggs, long grass and rocks around the pond to hide from birds and snakes, and insects to eat. That pond is its habitat. Losing habitat is one of the main reasons plants and animals die out.

Think of habitat as a job advert. A farm has many jobs: eating pests, pollinating flowers, recycling waste. Instead of paying for them, we advertise for wild helpers, and the advert must offer food, water and a safe bed.

1. **Provide water.** A bird bath or small pond soon brings birds, frogs and dragonflies, which eat large numbers of insects. Put stones or sticks in the water so bees can drink without drowning. Fence ponds or cover them with strong mesh so small children cannot fall in. Frogs, tadpoles and dragonfly larvae eat mosquito larvae; do not add mosquitofish, which eat the tadpoles too.
2. **Leave wild areas.** Keep any natural veld or wetland on your land. This is your **Zone 5**. If you have none, return a corner to indigenous plants. It is probably the most important thing you can do for habitat, because local plants feed far more local insects and birds than exotic ones.
3. **Plant hedgerows.** Plant mixed rows along fences and field edges: Cape honeysuckle (*Tecoma capensis*), plumbago (*Plumbago auriculata*), sand olive (*Dodonaea viscosa*), Kei apple (*Dovyalis caffra*), and karee and other *Searsia* species. They give shelter, food and nesting places, and they break the wind.
4. **Pile rocks and logs** away from the house and where children play. Lizards bask on the warm rocks and eat many insects. Logs shelter beetles, frogs and fungi.
5. **Put up an owl box** on a pole, tree or building at least 3 m above the ground, out of reach of cats. A family of barn owls can eat 1 000 or more rodents in a year. Owls die from eating poisoned rats, so if you want owls, stop using rat poison. Where owls are feared as a bad sign, talk to your neighbours about the work they do.

## Biodiversity

**Biodiversity** is the variety of life in a place: the many kinds of plants, animals, insects, fungi and soil life, and the differences within each kind. Walk through healthy veld or fynbos and you see it. A pine plantation shows the opposite: hectare after hectare of one tree, with very little growing underneath. Several kinds of pine also spread out into grassland and fynbos, and use a lot of water.

Diversity pays:

- **It spreads the risk.** If one crop fails to drought, pests or disease, others still give a harvest.
- **It helps control pests.** A mix of plants feeds and shelters the predators that eat pests, and pests struggle to find their favourite plant among many. Diverse gardens are less likely to suffer serious outbreaks, although they are never pest-free.
- **It builds healthy soil.** Different roots and plant leftovers feed a wider range of soil life.

To build it:

1. Grow **polycultures**: many crops together in the same bed or field, instead of one crop in long rows.
2. Plant **perennials** (plants that live for years, such as fruit trees, herbs and perennial vegetables) alongside your annual crops.
3. Plant a **food forest**, a garden in layers from tall trees down to groundcovers and root crops.
4. Keep a mix of animals, each with a job: chickens eat pests, and cattle manure feeds the compost.
5. Grow flowers, including indigenous ones, to feed bees and other helpful insects all year.

When the output of one part feeds another (dung to the soil, crop leftovers to the animals, pests to the birds), the farm begins to look after itself.

## Try it

Make a habitat corner this week.

1. Choose an unused corner near your vegetable beds, at least 2 m by 2 m, away from where children play.
2. Sink an old basin, a cut-down drum or a tyre lined with plastic into the ground. Fill it with water and edge it with stones. Put a few stones or sticks inside so insects can climb out. Cover it with strong mesh if small children live nearby.
3. Build a small rock pile on the sunny (north) side and a log pile on the shady side.
4. Plant, or leave, indigenous plants and some long grass around it.
5. Visit at dawn and dusk once a week. Write down every creature you see.

## Key points

- The pyramid of life rests on plants and soil. Kill every pest and you starve the predators that help you.
- Succession is how land heals. In Southern Africa it often ends in grassland, savanna or fynbos, not forest.
- Water, wild corners, hedgerows, rock piles and owl boxes bring wild helpers to work on your farm.
- Diversity spreads risk and keeps pests in check.

In a month, sit by the pond and count what has moved in. Every new visitor is a worker you did not have to hire.
