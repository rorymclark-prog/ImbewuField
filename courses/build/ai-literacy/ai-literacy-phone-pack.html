Course 5 · AI Literacy — using AI safely and usefully on a phone

For: farmers with smartphones (often entry-level Android on prepaid data), youth (18–35), community mentors and facilitators on ACT·SEF-type programmes. Mixed literacy. Mixed languages. Many learners first met AI inside WhatsApp or a chat app — some without knowing it was AI. Class size: 20–30 learners (25 is ideal), in groups of 5. Two facilitators. Hours: 2 days, 09:00–16:00, plus a Day 0 preparation day. Alternative: 4 half-days (see Four half-days below). Venue: a hall, school, church or lapa with shade, chairs, a wall or fence for posters, a small garden or a few plants and a tree within 50 m, and — if at all possible — a plug point or a charging station. The course runs without electricity if phones and power banks arrive charged.

The one idea behind the course

AI is a fast guesser. Use it to think, not to decide. Check before you trust.

Everything else hangs on that sentence. Learners leave able to use AI (ask well, use voice and photos, get a first draft) and to protect themselves (check answers, keep private things private, spot scams and fakes).

Nobody is left out: phones, no phones, and data

  • Phones are shared. Each group of 5 has at least one working smartphone (the "group phone"); pairs share where there are more. The kit carries 2 spare phones for groups with none.
  • The no-phone path is a full path, not a lesser one. Every activity can be done with printed AI answer cards (prompt-cards.md) and a role-play in which a facilitator plays the AI (wearing the cardboard "AI" sign). Learners without phones practise the same skills: asking well, checking, spotting danger and scams.
  • Data costs real money. The course brings its own Wi-Fi hotspot or data bundles (resources.md). No learner is asked to use their own airtime or data. Session A-D1-S2 teaches data-saving settings before anyone uses AI.
  • Learner phones stay the learner's. Facilitators never take a learner's phone to fix it without asking. Learners do every tap themselves; a facilitator talks them through it.

What learners leave with

By the end of Day 2 every learner can:

  1. Explain in their own words what AI is (a pattern-guesser trained on lots of text and pictures) and three things it is not (not a person, not always right, not knowing your farm).
  2. Name three places they already meet AI on a phone.
  3. Ask a clear question using the Question Hand (Who I am · What I have · What I want · How to answer · Ask again) — in English or their own language.
  4. Use voice typing and a clear photo to ask a question.
  5. Check an AI answer with the Check Ladder, and find at least one mistake in a printed AI answer.
  6. Sort questions by the traffic light, and name the red-light topics that must go to a person: poisons and sprays, medicine, animal health, money, loans, legal and grants.
  7. Choose what never to share (ID number, bank details, PINs and codes, SASSA details, children's faces, exact location) and set two-step verification in WhatsApp.
  8. Spot a scam message or fake voice note with Stop · Check · Ask, and plan a family code word.
  9. Use AI to draft a work message, price list or CV — then correct and own it.
  10. Make a personal AI plan: what I will use AI for, what I will always check with a person.

They take home: their own Question Hand card, a Check Ladder card, a Scam Stop card, their personal AI plan, a corrected piece of work (message, price list or CV), and a certificate of attendance.

App on / app off

The course is tool-neutral. It teaches habits that work with any AI tool.

  • App track ON (programme uses ImbewuField): sessions marked [APP] use Lima (the in-app assistant), Lima Vision ("What's growing" and "Weigh my harvest"), Tree ID, scan a till slip in Records → Spent, and AI-written Reports. Slides for these carry Track: app.
  • App track OFF: use the [PAPER] twin — same time, same outcome — with tools learners already have (WhatsApp, Meta AI where it shows up, Google Lens, voice typing on the keyboard) or the no-phone role-play with printed answer cards.
  • Switch the whole programme with one decision on Day 0: "Do all groups have the ImbewuField app installed, logged in, and AI features switched on for this programme?" If any answer is no, run [PAPER] for everyone. Programme admins can switch AI features off; check on Day 0.

Be honest with learners about tools: - AI features change quickly. A button may move or disappear between courses. Test on Day 0. - Some AI features cost data; some need a newer phone; some do not work in every language. - We never tell learners one product is "the best". We name tools they already have. - We never promise that AI will make them money or replace their mentor.

Facilitators

  • Two facilitators. At least one is confident on Android phones and WhatsApp settings; at least one speaks the learners' first language well. Both must have done Day 0 together.
  • The facilitator does not need to be an AI expert. They need to model the habit: "I don't know — let's check." Saying this aloud in front of learners is part of the lesson.
  • Co-facilitator plays the AI in role-plays, keeps the teacher-talk tally (session sheet, shared/forms.md §3), and runs the Phone Help Desk (a chair and a table where anyone can bring a phone problem at tea and lunch).

Delivery rhythm

  • Day 0 is never the same day as Day 1.
  • At most three courses back to back, then a week at home (same as all courses).
  • This course is light on digging but heavy on talking and small screens. Plan eye breaks: every 20 minutes of screen work, 2 minutes looking at something far away.

How it connects to the other courses

  • Course 1 (Grow Food, Grow Strong): the farm questions in this course come from Course 1 topics (water, soil, seedlings, pests, seed, money). Learners who have done Course 1 check AI answers against what they learned there.
  • Course 2 (Walking With Farmers): mentors are the "person to ask" on the Check Ladder. Mentors should take this course so they can support farmers who use AI — and so they check AI-cleaned visit notes and AI-written reports before signing them.
  • Course 3 (Teach the Teachers): facilitators micro-teach A-D1-S1 (The Guessing Machine) and A-D2-S2 (scams) as practice sessions.
  • Course 4 (Farmer Money): A-D2-S3 (till slip, price list) builds on its record-keeping; the scam session protects savings groups and stokvels.

3. Phone and data test (09:30–11:00)

Do this on the cheapest phone you have (entry-level Android), not your own good phone.

  2.   3. WhatsApp safety settings (for A-D2-S2), same phones: - Settings → Account → Two-step verification → turn on (practise with the spare phone). - Settings → Privacy → Groups: "My contacts" (who can add me to groups). - Settings → Privacy → Profile photo, About, Last seen: "My contacts". - Settings → Privacy → Calls → Silence unknown callers (if the option shows). - How to block and report a chat. shared/facilitation-toolkit.md

Facilitation toolkit (all courses)

The day files refer to these by name ("run Groups of 5", "use Three-question debrief"). Read this once before any course; keep it on your phone.

1. The shape of every session: Look · Try · Talk · Take home

Step What happens Who talks
Look (hook) A question, an object, a story, a poster reveal Facilitator asks, learners answer
Try Learners do it — in groups of 5, with their hands Learners
Talk Three-question debrief; the poster fills in the "why" Learners, facilitator sums up
Take home One thing to do at home, said aloud by each group Learners

Teacher talk ≤ 30%. In a 60-minute session that is 18 minutes. The co-facilitator keeps a tally on the session sheet (see forms.md). If you are over, stop and ask a question.

2. Opening circle (every morning, 30 min)

  1. Register (10 min) — learners sign themselves in on the sheet at the door while the circle gathers. Late arrivals sign at tea. Never skip it: funders pay against it.
  2. Greeting and song or energiser (5 min).
  3. "What did you sleep on?" (10 min) — three or four learners say one thing they remember from yesterday, one question they woke up with. Questions go on the Question Tree.
  4. Today (5 min) — point to today's posters; say the day's promise in one sentence.

3. Closing circle (every afternoon, 20 min)

  1. One word — each learner says one word about today (right to pass).
  2. Group report — each group's Reporter says their Take home action.
  3. Question Tree — answer what's left or promise a time tomorrow.
  4. Tools back — Materials people count tools back into the kit. Nobody leaves until the count matches.

4. Groups of 5 (for every practical)

Form groups on Day 1 and keep them all course (they become support groups afterwards). Mix them: young and old, men and women, readers and non-readers, near neighbours together (so they can help each other at home).

Name each group after a plant: Bean, Pumpkin, Maize, Banana, Marula, Spinach.

Role Job Rotate?
Leader Keeps everyone doing, not watching Daily
Timekeeper Watches the time, calls "5 minutes left" Daily
Materials Collects, checks and returns the group's kit Daily
Recorder Draws or writes what the group found (drawing is fine) Daily
Reporter Speaks for the group in the debrief Daily

In the AI Literacy course the Materials person is also the Phone Keeper (charges, hands out and collects the group's shared phone).

Put the roles on a small card per group. Rotate every morning so everyone does every role.

Never demo-only. If you have only one set of materials: demonstrate in 3 minutes, then every group takes a turn while the others do a different task (a "carousel"). Nobody sits and watches for longer than 10 minutes.

5. Getting attention back without shouting

  • Hand up, mouth shut: raise your hand; anyone who sees it raises theirs and goes quiet.
  • Clap back: you clap a rhythm, the room claps it back.
  • Call and response: you call "Imbewu!", they answer "Iyamila!" (the seed grows) — agree your own call on Day 1 in the local language.
  • Whisper: talk very quietly. People lean in.

6. Energisers (5 minutes, when energy drops — after lunch always)

Use one whenever you see yawning, phones, or people leaving. Choose ones that teach.

Name How Teaches
Sun and Shade Facilitator is the sun, walks north side; learners must stand in the "shade" of a partner Southern hemisphere sun
Water Drop Circle holds hands = soil; one person is a raindrop trying to get in; "mulch" players protect gaps Infiltration, mulch
Seed Swap Each person gets a paper "seed"; swap with 3 people, say your name and one crop you love Names, sharing
Compost Heap Call "green!" (sit), "brown!" (stand), "water!" (wave), "air!" (jump) — faster and faster Compost needs
Web of Life Ball of string passed across the circle: "I am the worm, I feed the…" Connections
Zones Walk Call a zone number; learners run to the matching spot (0 = the door, 5 = far wall) Zones
Pass the Clap Clap passes round the circle; reverse on "hey!" Focus
Two Truths and a Crop Say two true things and one false about your garden; others guess Getting to know
Mirror Pairs mirror each other's slow movements Listening (mentor course)
Rain Storm Rub hands → click → pat knees → stamp → back again Calming, rain

Check first: nobody forced to touch or run. Elders may sit and call the game.

7. The three-question debrief (after every practical)

  1. What did you do / see? (facts)
  2. What did you learn — why does it work? (meaning) ← point to the poster here
  3. What will you do at home? (action)

Take one answer per group for each question. Write the home answers on the flipchart beside the poster.

8. Feedback that builds people ("two stars and a wish")

Used in micro-teaching (Course 3), mentor role-plays (Course 2) and on farm visits:

  • Star 1: one thing that worked, specific ("You let the group dig before you explained.")
  • Star 2: a second thing that worked
  • Wish: one wish, as a suggestion ("Next time, maybe ask them first where the water goes.")

The person who was taught speaks first about their own session. Never correct someone's language, spelling or accent.

9. Mixed literacy, mixed ages, mixed languages

  • Assume some learners do not read. Draw, point, do. Recorders can draw.
  • Never ask someone to read aloud unless they volunteer.
  • Speak the local language first; English words for key terms second, so learners can read seed packets and product labels.
  • Give language time: after you explain, groups retell the idea to each other in their own words for 2 minutes. This is the best check of understanding you have.
  • Elders hold knowledge: ask "How did your grandmother do this?" in every topic. Honour it, then add what's new.
  • Youth often have phones: pair them with elders for photographs, records and the app.
  • Women often carry the garden and the household: plan start and end times with them.
  • Disability: every practical has a seated role (sorting seed, mixing seedling soil, recording).

10. Safety

  • First aid kit open and visible; know the nearest clinic and have a vehicle plan.
  • Sun and heat: hats, water, shade breaks; heavy digging before 11:00.
  • Tools: carry spades and forks points down; nobody behind a swinging pick; count tools.
  • Manure and compost: gloves or wash hands with soap before eating.
  • Chilli/garlic sprays: keep away from eyes; wash hands.
  • Weather: lightning = everyone indoors, stop the practical.

11. The Question Tree

A flipchart sheet on a tree or wall, labelled with a drawing of a tree. Questions that don't fit the moment are written (or drawn) on sticky notes and hung there. Before the closing circle, answer them or say when you will. It keeps the session on track without ignoring anyone.

12. Timekeeping

  • Start on time with whoever is there — the opening circle is the reward for being early.
  • Say "5 minutes left" in every practical.
  • Never tell learners you are behind. Cut from the could know list, never from the practical. Each session in the day files marks what can be cut: ✂ cut if short.

Bilingual posters: isiZulu first, English second

One poster carries both languages (cheaper to print, and every learner can read one of them):

  • Headline: isiZulu on top at full size; English directly below at ~60% size, in the course's accent colour.
  • Labels: isiZulu in bold; English underneath in regular weight, smaller.
  • Room question: isiZulu only on the poster; English is on the back card.
  • Word limit becomes ≤ 25 words per language. isiZulu runs ~30% longer — size the label band for it, not for English.
  • Other languages (Sesotho, siSwati, Xitsonga, Sepedi, isiXhosa) replace the isiZulu line in a separate print run: <ID>.st.pdf, <ID>.ss.pdf …
  • Text source: <course>/i18n/zu.json (drafts — must be read back by a first-language speaker who farms before printing).

Art rules (all courses)

  1. Southern hemisphere. Sun in the north, shadows fall south. Any compass shows N up.
  2. No text inside the artwork. Words are typeset on the layout so they can be translated.
  3. Generic plant shapes unless the poster is about telling crops apart (then the art brief names the crop and it is drawn accurately). Never draw a recognisable invasive species.
  4. People look like the learners: Black South African smallholders, mixed ages and genders, gogos and youth, work clothes, gumboots, headscarves, caps. Nobody in a lab coat.
  5. Flat, warm, instructional illustration. Thick dark outlines. Not photoreal, not 3D.
  6. One idea per poster. If you need two ideas, it is two posters.
  7. Show local materials: 20 ℓ drums, 2 ℓ bottles, tins, wire, droppers, kraal manure, grass mulch, a corrugated-iron roof, a JoJo-style tank, fencing with droppers.

Translation

Every poster ships as an editable layout (Figma, Affinity/Illustrator SVG or a PowerPoint master) with all words in text boxes. Order of translation for ACT·SEF sites: isiZulu, then Sesotho, siSwati, Xitsonga, Sepedi, isiXhosa as programmes need. A translation must be read back by a first- language speaker who farms before it is printed.

The back-of-poster card (A5)

Each poster has a "Teach it" card: 5–8 numbered prompts that let a facilitator teach the poster without a phone. It is written in posters.md under Teach it. Format:

F-P06  Build an A-frame                     (≈ 15 min + practical)
1. ASK: How do we know water will stay in a ditch and not run away?
2. POINT ①②: two legs, one crossbar, one string with a stone
3. …
KEY: A level line is where water stops and soaks.

Poster wall habits (teach these in Course 3)

  • Posters go up before the session and stay up for the whole course: by Day 5 the room is a map of everything learned.
  • Hang them at eye level of a seated learner, in the order they were taught, left to right.
  • Leave one blank sheet of flipchart beside each poster for the group's own words and drawings.
  • The Question Tree (a flipchart sheet on a tree or wall): any question that doesn't fit the session is written there and answered before the day ends.
