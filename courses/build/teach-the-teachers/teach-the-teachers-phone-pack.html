Teach the Teachers

README.md

Course 3 · Teach the Teachers — permaculture & agroecology facilitation

For: people who will deliver Course 1 (Grow Food, Grow Strong) and other permaculture or agroecology courses: co-facilitators, young graduates (22–35), extension staff and lead farmers. Some trainees have a permaculture certificate. Some have only practical farming. Both are welcome: the course builds teaching skill and content confidence together.

Class size: max 20 trainees, in 4 groups of 5. Two trainers (lead and co-trainer). Why 20, not 30: every trainee must teach five times and be seen teaching. With 20 trainees the micro-teaching arithmetic works (see "Micro-teaching ladder" below). With 25 it does not.

Hours: 08:30–16:30, five consecutive days, plus Day 0 for the trainers. Venue: a room for 20 with wall space for 25 posters, plus outdoor space (a tree, a fence, a patch of ground to dig), plus a second teaching space (a second room, a veranda or a big tree) for the two parallel micro-teaching streams on Days 3–5.

Why 08:30–16:30 (and not 09:00–16:00 like Course 1)

  • Trainees are staff, graduates and lead farmers, usually transported or staying nearby. An 8-hour day is normal working time for them.
  • Each day holds five things: a teaching skill, a content refresh, a poster studio or teaching mode, a micro-teaching round and a feedback circle. Micro-teaching alone needs 110–200 minutes a day. At 09:00–16:00 something would be cut, and it would be the practice.
  • 65 minutes of breaks a day, a 45-minute lunch, and no new content after 15:50.
  • If transport makes 08:30 impossible, see "Short-day version" at the end of this file.

What trainees leave with

By the end of Day 5 every trainee can:

  1. Plan a session in the shape Look · Try · Talk · Take home, with an outcome that starts with a doing word.
  2. Teach a Course 1 session for 30 minutes with a partner, keeping teacher talk at or under 30%, measured by an observer.
  3. Run a hands-on practical for groups of 5 with named roles, without it turning into a demonstration.
  4. Ask questions that climb from closed to open to deeper, and wait for answers.
  5. Make a hand-drawn teaching poster that follows the poster rules (≤ 25 words, one idea, numbered callouts) and teach from it with no device.
  6. Teach the same lesson three ways: poster only, slides (laptop, TV, projector or tablet for a small group) and with the ImbewuField app — and switch to paper when a device fails.
  7. Explain the permaculture ethics and principles, the FAO 10 elements of agroecology, and the core practices of Course 1 (land, water, soil, plants, seed, food and money) well enough to answer farmers' questions or say "I will find out".
  8. Use stories, songs, role play, a gallery walk, real objects and improvised teaching aids.
  9. Include everyone: non-readers, elders, youth, people with a disability, and speakers of several languages.
  10. Handle difficult moments calmly (a dominant talker, a silent group, conflict, a learner in distress).
  11. Run the paperwork: daily register, POPIA consent, session sheet, skills checklist, picture quiz, evaluation and attendance certificates.
  12. Give and receive feedback with two stars and a wish.
  13. Plan their own delivery: kit, Day 0, co-teaching handovers, and a rhythm that does not burn them out.

And they take home: every poster they made (5+), their five lesson plans with observer sheets, a personal teaching plan, the course phone pack, and a certificate of attendance.

5 5 30 min Pairs, a real Course 1 session Half-class (8 peers as farmers) 10 min 5 pairs × 40 = 200, 2 streams

Why not the whole class on Day 5? 10 pairs × 40 minutes = 6 h 40 min — the whole day, with no evaluation or certificates. Two parallel streams of 10 (one trainer each) give every pair a real audience of 8 and a trainer's eye. Streams swap membership between Day 4 and Day 5 so every trainee sees 15+ different colleagues teach.

Three-mode rule: by the end of Day 5 every trainee has taught at least once from a poster only, once with slides (or the paper flip-book twin) and once with the app (or its paper twin). A Mode tracker flipchart on the wall is ticked after each round.

Topics come from the micro-teaching bank: 24 assignments cut from Course 1 sessions, each with an outcome, a poster, materials and time.

Delivery rhythm (for trainers of this course, and for trainees afterwards)

  • Day 0 is never the same day as Day 1. Trainers need the full day.
  • A trainer pair runs at most two Teach-the-Teachers courses back to back (it is more tiring than Course 1: four streams to watch, 100+ observer sheets to read). Then a week off.
  • Trainees, once qualified to co-facilitate Course 1: max three courses back to back, then a full week at home (as in the Course 1 README).
  • A new facilitator's first Course 1 is as co-facilitator beside an experienced lead, teaching about half the sessions. Lead on the second or third course.
  • Mentoring after the course: each trainee is observed teaching one real Course 1 session within 8 weeks, with the same observer sheet (micro-teaching-bank.md). Plan it on Day 5.

Certificates

Attendance certificate for at least 4 of 5 days and all five micro-teaching rounds (a missed round can be made up on the spot the next day). It is not an accredited qualification. Accredited trainer or facilitator qualifications go through the QCTO / a SETA process — check locally if your programme needs one. Wording is in day-5.md, T-D5-SC.

How it connects to the other courses

  • Course 1 is the content trainees will teach. Micro-teaching uses real Course 1 sessions.
  • Course 2 (mentors) uses the same two stars and a wish, the same skills checklist and the same Mentor → Record visit screen. Good facilitators often become mentors.
  • Courses 4 and 5 use the same lesson shape, poster rules and groups of 5, so a trainee who finishes this course can co-facilitate them after reading the day files.

Short-day version (09:00–15:30)

If transport forces a short day: drop Course craft 15 into Day 0 reading, cut Round 1 to groups of 5 without the stretch, and run S2 model lessons at 75 minutes (each day file marks ✂ cut if short). Do not cut micro-teaching minutes or the feedback after each teach.

A note on sources

This course draws on well-known adult-learning practice (the experiential learning cycle; adults learn best from problems they recognise and their own experience), farmer field schools, farmer-to-farmer (campesino-a-campesino) extension, and FAO's 10 elements of agroecology (2018). The session design, posters and wording are ImbewuField's own.

Say / ask / do

  1. DO (08:15): Co-trainer sits at the register table. Each trainee signs AM, takes a name tag and writes their first name big. Say: "Sign the register first — always."
  2. SAY (08:30, lead): "Welcome. For five days you are learners and teachers. We will teach this course the way you will teach farmers. Watch how we teach, not only what." Point to the day's promise, then to T-P01's empty space.
  3. DO (08:33): Name-and-object circle. SAY: "Say your name, where you farm or work, and hold up one thing from your pocket or bag that you could teach with." Go first: hold up a coin. "I could teach money in, money out with this." Then round the circle, 30–40 seconds each. (Expected: a phone, a pen, a key, a seed, a water bottle, a hat.) ASK after the circle: "What did we just learn about teaching?" (Any object can teach; we remember people by their object.)
  4. DO (08:48, co-trainer): POPIA consent. Hand out forms. Read forms.md §2 aloud in English, then ask a trainee volunteer to read it in isiZulu / Sesotho as fits the room. SAY: "You will do this with farmers on Day 1 of Course 1, before any photograph. A thumbprint counts." Green dot on the tags of anyone who ticks "No photos".
  5. DO (08:58): Hopes and worries. Green sticky note: "one hope for this week". Orange: "one worry about teaching". One idea per note, words or a drawing. 4 minutes to write. Trainees stick them on two flipcharts; two volunteers cluster similar notes (5 min). ASK: "What do most of us worry about?" (Forgetting content, languages, big groups, being asked a question I can't answer, elders not respecting young facilitators.) SAY: "Every worry here has a session this week." Point to where (e.g. questions you can't answer → Day 1 S2; elders → Day 4 S1).
  6. DO (09:10): Hand out group cards (Bean, Pumpkin, Maize, Marula). Groups sit together. GROUPS (6 min): "Agree three rules that would help you learn this week. One per sticky note." DO: Reporters stick them on T-P01. Lead clusters and reads back (4 min). Add any missing must-haves (see box). Everyone signs or marks the poster (3 min).
  7. DO (09:25): Agree the call-and-response signal in the local language (the room chooses; example from the toolkit: "Imbewu!" / "Iyamila!"). Practise it twice. Hand out role cards (T-P06). Each group decides today's Leader, Timekeeper, Materials, Recorder and Reporter in 1 minute. SAY: "Roles rotate every morning."

Must-haves for the agreement (add if the room doesn't): Phones on silent · Start on time · Right to pass · One voice at a time · No correcting anyone's language or accent · Feedback is a gift: say thank you · What is said about a person stays in the room · Questions that don't fit go on the Question Tree.

Debrief

Skip a formal debrief. Ask one question: "What did we do in the last hour that you will do on Day 1 of your own course?" (Register first, consent, names, agreement, groups, a signal.)

Key messages

  • The register and consent come before anything else.
  • A learning agreement made by the group is kept by the group.
  • Groups of 5 and roles start in the first hour.

If things go wrong

  • Trainees arrive late: start on time; late arrivals sign at the door and join the circle. Never restart. Their object can be shown at tea.
  • Someone will not sign POPIA: they don't have to. They can attend. No photos, no data in the app beyond the register (explain the register is needed for attendance).
  • More than 20 arrive: see Day 0. Stay kind; take names for the next course.

Language & inclusion

  • Read the POPIA form aloud; never ask a trainee to read aloud unless they volunteer.
  • Drawing is fine on every sticky note.
  • ✂ Cut if short: hopes and worries to 6 minutes (no clustering; read 5 aloud).

Four facts about adult learners (plain words, from adult-learning research): 1. Adults want to know why before they spend time on something. 2. Twelve permaculture design principles (T-P20), with a garden example each: 1. Observe & interact — watch the site for a season before big changes. 2. Catch & store energy — a tank, mulch, seed, compost. 3. Obtain a yield — plant something you can eat soon, even while building. 4. Self-regulate & accept feedback — if the bed floods, change it. 5. Use & value renewables — sun, rain, manure, not bought inputs. 6. Produce no waste — every output is someone's input. 7. Design from patterns to details — see the slope and water first, then choose the bed. 8. Integrate rather than segregate — chickens next to the garden, not far away. 9. Use small & slow solutions — one good bed before ten weak ones. 10. Use & value diversity — many crops; nobody's "wrong" plant. 11. Use edges & value the margins — the fence line, the wet corner, the quiet learner. 12. Creatively use & respond to change — a drought year → plant drought-tolerant crops. (These twelve are David Holmgren's widely taught set. Course 1 uses six simpler garden principles on F-P04; trainers should know how the two fit.)

Farmers' questions you will hear — good short answers:

Question Answer
Is agroecology just organic farming? No. Organic is a rule set for what you may use. Agroecology is also about knowledge, fairness, culture and markets. Many agroecological farmers are not certified organic.
Can I never use fertiliser? Build the soil first; many farmers find they need less or none. The course won't tell you what you must never do — it shows you cheaper ways that last.
Why not hybrid seed? Hybrid (F1) seed can yield well but won't grow true if you save it; you must buy each season. Open-pollinated seed can be saved. Many farmers use both.
Is permaculture from overseas? The word is Australian (1970s). Many of the practices — mixed crops, mulch, seed saving — are old African practice. Ask the elders.

Content refresh notes

  • Sun (southern hemisphere): the sun is always in the northern half of the sky at noon in South Africa; shadows point south. Winter sun is low → long shadows; plant tall crops on the south side so they don't shade short ones. North-facing slopes are warmer.
  • Wind: know the direction of hot dry winds and cold winds on your site; windbreaks on that side protect crops (up to about 5–10 times the windbreak's height downwind).
  • Frost & cold air: cold air flows downhill and pools in low spots — frost pockets.
  • Water: slow it (mulch, plants, stones), spread it (along contours, swales), sink it (pits, swales, mulch, trench beds), store it (tanks, soil, ponds). Start at the top of the slope. Water on contour (a level line, found with an A-frame) soaks in; water down the slope runs away and takes topsoil.
  • Roof water: litres = roof area (m²) × rain (mm); first-flush diverter keeps the dirty first flow out of the tank.
  • Greywater: bath and washing water with mild soap can go onto mulch around trees; not kitchen water with fat, not bleach, not onto leaves you eat raw.

Content refresh notes

  • Texture: sand (gritty, no ribbon, drains fast), silt (smooth, floury), clay (sticky, long ribbon, holds water and food but can waterlog). Loam = a mix. Organic matter improves every soil: it holds water in sand and opens up clay.
  • Soil life: worms, fungi, bacteria, insects. More life = better structure and food for plants. Bare soil in the sun loses life fast → cover it (mulch).
  • Compost heap: at least about 1 m × 1 m × 1 m to heat up; layers of brown (dry, carbon: dry grass, leaves, maize stalks) and green (fresh, nitrogen: kitchen scraps, fresh cuttings, manure) — roughly 2 parts brown to 1 part green by volume; moist like a squeezed sponge; turn every 1–2 weeks; ready in about 2–3 months (longer if not turned).
  • Liquid manure: a sack of manure in a drum of water, stirred, for about 2 weeks; dilute to weak-tea colour before use.
  • pH: home vinegar/bicarbonate tests are rough; for real numbers, a lab test — check locally (Department of Agriculture or a co-op may help).
  • Farmer field school (FFS): a group of farmers meets regularly through a season on a learning plot, observes and compares (e.g. with and without compost), and decides what works. Started by FAO in Asia (1989) for pest management; used across Africa.

Content refresh notes

  • Healthy-garden ladder = IPM (integrated pest management): prevent first (soil, diversity, timing), watch often, act early with the gentlest method, spray last. Even natural sprays kill helpers — use them only on the problem plants.
  • Helpers: ladybirds (eat aphids), lacewings, hoverflies, spiders, praying mantis, wasps that parasitise caterpillars, birds, lizards, frogs. Flowers (e.g. marigold, coriander, dill, cosmos-type daisies) feed helpers.
  • Spacing: roughly — cabbage 45–60 cm; spinach/Swiss chard 30 cm; onions 10 cm; tomatoes 50–60 cm; beans 10–15 cm in rows 45 cm. Check the seed packet; closer spacing in rich deep beds, wider in poor soil.
  • Seedlings: sow at about twice the seed's width deep; keep moist not wet; harden off by giving more sun and less water for a week before transplanting; transplant in the late afternoon or on a cloudy day.
  • Rotation: leaf → fruit → root → legume (beans/peas) is a simple 4-bed rotation.
  • Food forest layers: tall tree, small tree, shrub, herb, ground cover, climber, root. Plant fruit trees where they won't shade the vegetable beds (usually south of the beds — the sun is in the north).
  • Seed: open-pollinated varieties grow true from saved seed (if not crossed); F1 hybrids don't grow true. Dry seed crops (beans, maize, pumpkin): dry fully, store cool and dry in a sealed jar. Wet seed (tomato): ferment 2–3 days in water, rinse, dry on a plate. Label with crop, variety, date. Germination test: 10 seeds on a damp cloth — count after 7–10 days.
  • GMO question: SA allows some GM maize, soya and cotton. Saving seed from GM or hybrid maize is not advised (it won't grow true, and GM seed may carry contract terms — check the packet). Open-pollinated seed avoids both issues.

Why this look: trainees on this course learn to make their own posters by hand. The course itself looks hand-made: marker and chalk sketch-notes on brown kraft paper and blackboards, sticky notes, arrows, doodle icons. It shows them what "good enough to teach from" looks like.

shared/facilitation-toolkit.md

Facilitation toolkit (all courses)

The day files refer to these by name ("run Groups of 5", "use Three-question debrief"). Read this once before any course; keep it on your phone.

1. The shape of every session: Look · Try · Talk · Take home

Step What happens Who talks
Look (hook) A question, an object, a story, a poster reveal Facilitator asks, learners answer
Try Learners do it — in groups of 5, with their hands Learners
Talk Three-question debrief; the poster fills in the "why" Learners, facilitator sums up
Take home One thing to do at home, said aloud by each group Learners

Teacher talk ≤ 30%. In a 60-minute session that is 18 minutes. The co-facilitator keeps a tally on the session sheet (see forms.md). If you are over, stop and ask a question.

2. Opening circle (every morning, 30 min)

  1. Register (10 min) — learners sign themselves in on the sheet at the door while the circle gathers. Late arrivals sign at tea. Never skip it: funders pay against it.
  2. Greeting and song or energiser (5 min).
  3. "What did you sleep on?" (10 min) — three or four learners say one thing they remember from yesterday, one question they woke up with. Questions go on the Question Tree.
  4. Today (5 min) — point to today's posters; say the day's promise in one sentence.

3. Closing circle (every afternoon, 20 min)

  1. One word — each learner says one word about today (right to pass).
  2. Group report — each group's Reporter says their Take home action.
  3. Question Tree — answer what's left or promise a time tomorrow.
  4. Tools back — Materials people count tools back into the kit. Nobody leaves until the count matches.

4. Groups of 5 (for every practical)

Form groups on Day 1 and keep them all course (they become support groups afterwards). Mix them: young and old, men and women, readers and non-readers, near neighbours together (so they can help each other at home).

Name each group after a plant: Bean, Pumpkin, Maize, Banana, Marula, Spinach.

Role Job Rotate?
Leader Keeps everyone doing, not watching Daily
Timekeeper Watches the time, calls "5 minutes left" Daily
Materials Collects, checks and returns the group's kit Daily
Recorder Draws or writes what the group found (drawing is fine) Daily
Reporter Speaks for the group in the debrief Daily

In the AI Literacy course the Materials person is also the Phone Keeper (charges, hands out and collects the group's shared phone).

Put the roles on a small card per group. Rotate every morning so everyone does every role.

Never demo-only. If you have only one set of materials: demonstrate in 3 minutes, then every group takes a turn while the others do a different task (a "carousel"). Nobody sits and watches for longer than 10 minutes.

5. Getting attention back without shouting

  • Hand up, mouth shut: raise your hand; anyone who sees it raises theirs and goes quiet.
  • Clap back: you clap a rhythm, the room claps it back.
  • Call and response: you call "Imbewu!", they answer "Iyamila!" (the seed grows) — agree your own call on Day 1 in the local language.
  • Whisper: talk very quietly. People lean in.

6. Energisers (5 minutes, when energy drops — after lunch always)

Use one whenever you see yawning, phones, or people leaving. Choose ones that teach.

Name How Teaches
Sun and Shade Facilitator is the sun, walks north side; learners must stand in the "shade" of a partner Southern hemisphere sun
Water Drop Circle holds hands = soil; one person is a raindrop trying to get in; "mulch" players protect gaps Infiltration, mulch
Seed Swap Each person gets a paper "seed"; swap with 3 people, say your name and one crop you love Names, sharing
Compost Heap Call "green!" (sit), "brown!" (stand), "water!" (wave), "air!" (jump) — faster and faster Compost needs
Web of Life Ball of string passed across the circle: "I am the worm, I feed the…" Connections
Zones Walk Call a zone number; learners run to the matching spot (0 = the door, 5 = far wall) Zones
Pass the Clap Clap passes round the circle; reverse on "hey!" Focus
Two Truths and a Crop Say two true things and one false about your garden; others guess Getting to know
Mirror Pairs mirror each other's slow movements Listening (mentor course)
Rain Storm Rub hands → click → pat knees → stamp → back again Calming, rain

Check first: nobody forced to touch or run. Elders may sit and call the game.

7. The three-question debrief (after every practical)

  1. What did you do / see? (facts)
  2. What did you learn — why does it work? (meaning) ← point to the poster here
  3. What will you do at home? (action)

Take one answer per group for each question. Write the home answers on the flipchart beside the poster.

8. Feedback that builds people ("two stars and a wish")

Used in micro-teaching (Course 3), mentor role-plays (Course 2) and on farm visits:

  • Star 1: one thing that worked, specific ("You let the group dig before you explained.")
  • Star 2: a second thing that worked
  • Wish: one wish, as a suggestion ("Next time, maybe ask them first where the water goes.")

The person who was taught speaks first about their own session. Never correct someone's language, spelling or accent.

9. Mixed literacy, mixed ages, mixed languages

  • Assume some learners do not read. Draw, point, do. Recorders can draw.
  • Never ask someone to read aloud unless they volunteer.
  • Speak the local language first; English words for key terms second, so learners can read seed packets and product labels.
  • Give language time: after you explain, groups retell the idea to each other in their own words for 2 minutes. This is the best check of understanding you have.
  • Elders hold knowledge: ask "How did your grandmother do this?" in every topic. Honour it, then add what's new.
  • Youth often have phones: pair them with elders for photographs, records and the app.
  • Women often carry the garden and the household: plan start and end times with them.
  • Disability: every practical has a seated role (sorting seed, mixing seedling soil, recording).

10. Safety

  • First aid kit open and visible; know the nearest clinic and have a vehicle plan.
  • Sun and heat: hats, water, shade breaks; heavy digging before 11:00.
  • Tools: carry spades and forks points down; nobody behind a swinging pick; count tools.
  • Manure and compost: gloves or wash hands with soap before eating.
  • Chilli/garlic sprays: keep away from eyes; wash hands.
  • Weather: lightning = everyone indoors, stop the practical.

11. The Question Tree

A flipchart sheet on a tree or wall, labelled with a drawing of a tree. Questions that don't fit the moment are written (or drawn) on sticky notes and hung there. Before the closing circle, answer them or say when you will. It keeps the session on track without ignoring anyone.

12. Timekeeping

  • Start on time with whoever is there — the opening circle is the reward for being early.
  • Say "5 minutes left" in every practical.
  • Never tell learners you are behind. Cut from the could know list, never from the practical. Each session in the day files marks what can be cut: ✂ cut if short.

Art rules (all courses)

  1. Southern hemisphere. Sun in the north, shadows fall south. Any compass shows N up.
  2. No text inside the artwork. Words are typeset on the layout so they can be translated.
  3. Generic plant shapes unless the poster is about telling crops apart (then the art brief names the crop and it is drawn accurately). Never draw a recognisable invasive species.
  4. People look like the learners: Black South African smallholders, mixed ages and genders, gogos and youth, work clothes, gumboots, headscarves, caps. Nobody in a lab coat.
  5. Flat, warm, instructional illustration. Thick dark outlines. Not photoreal, not 3D.
  6. One idea per poster. If you need two ideas, it is two posters.
  7. Show local materials: 20 ℓ drums, 2 ℓ bottles, tins, wire, droppers, kraal manure, grass mulch, a corrugated-iron roof, a JoJo-style tank, fencing with droppers.
